The Pleiades

The Seven Sisters in a cloud of borrowed dust

The Pleiades, the Seven Sisters, about 440 light-years away in Taurus: a cluster of young stars about 120 million years old drifting through a cloud of dust that isn't theirs, which reflects their blue light in fine streaks lined up with the Galaxy's magnetic field. Behind them, through the dust, galaxies a billion light-years away and a quasar whose light set out about 10 billion years ago. Eleven hours from a fast telescope used once, remotely.

The light from the Pleiades left them about 440 years ago, around 1580. In 1579, as it set out, Michael Mästlin was counting these very stars by eye, and in 1583 Giordano Bruno lectured at Oxford, arguing that the Earth goes round the Sun. And behind them, probably the farthest thing in the picture, a faint point at the lower left is a quasar whose light set out about 10 billion years ago, more than five billion years before the Sun and the Earth formed.

This picture is from an 8-inch Schmidt-Cassegrain telescope, a Celestron C8, with a Hyperstar, which puts the camera at the front of the telescope, at its main mirror’s focus, and makes it fast, about f/1.9; I used it once, remotely, in December 2022. It covers 2.8 degrees of sky by 2, about five full Moons by four, in Taurus. It’s in natural colour, from a colour camera. The Pleiades have their own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the right, and the picture is a mirror image of the sky, as a Hyperstar’s pictures are: the light reaches the camera after only one reflection, off the main mirror, and a single reflection flips it. So east is down, west up, and south to the left. The cluster fills the middle. Alcyone, the brightest star, is near the centre; Merope is up and to the left of it, with the brightest part of the nebula fanning out to its left; Electra and Celaeno are near the top, Maia and Taygeta up and to the right of the middle, and Atlas and Pleione are the pair near the bottom. The blue haze round them all is dust reflecting their light, and faint brown dust frames it, strongest in the top right corner. The orange star at the right, HD 23712, isn’t one of them. At the cluster’s distance, about 440 light-years, the frame is about 22 light-years across and 15 high, and each pixel covers about 140 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

The Pleiades

The Pleiades, M45, are the nearest rich cluster of young stars to us. About 1,300 stars lie within the distance where the cluster’s own gravity holds them, and about 700 of the stars Gaia counts as members are in this frame, together perhaps 800 to 1,000 times the Sun’s mass.

Their distance was the subject of a long argument. In 1997 the Hipparcos satellite put them about 390 light-years away, about a tenth nearer than other methods did, and if it had been right, models of how stars shine would have been wrong. In 2014 radio telescopes across the world, measuring the parallaxes of stars in the cluster together, found about 444 light-years, and Gaia has since found about 440. The fault seems to have been small errors in Hipparcos’s measurements of this patch of sky.

You’ll often read that they’re about 100 million years old, from the brightest stars’ evolution. A newer method gives about 120: low-mass stars destroy the lithium in their cores as they contract, the more massive sooner, so in a cluster of one age there’s a sharp boundary in brightness between stars that still have lithium and stars that don’t, and it moves to fainter stars as the cluster ages. In the Pleiades it gives 112 to 130 million years.

They won’t last. The cluster is already shedding stars, in a stream reaching about 130 light-years from it, and a study of 2025 found it’s the bound core of a much larger group of stars of the same age, spread over 2,000 light-years. Over the next few hundred million years to a billion, the Galaxy’s tidal effects will pull most of what’s left away. You may read a figure of 250 million years; the simulations give longer.

The Seven Sisters

In Greek myth the stars are the seven daughters of Atlas and Pleione: Alcyone, Asterope, Electra, Maia, Merope, Taygeta, and Celaeno. Usually only six are easy to see; Galileo wrote that he said six intentionally, since the seventh was scarcely ever visible. Homer and Hesiod mention them, and so do Job and Amos.

Alcyone, the brightest, is a hot star spinning fast enough to have thrown off a disc of gas. Atlas is a pair whose orbit, measured with interferometers, gave one of the first geometric distances that showed Hipparcos was wrong. Maia once gave its name to a class of variable stars, but its variation turns out to be rotation, carrying a large chemical spot round. Pleione spins once in about 12 hours, and over about 35 years throws off and loses shells of gas, changing its brightness and spectrum; it began its present cycle in 2005. And all seven of the brightest vary: in 2017 the Kepler telescope, in its second mission, found that every one of them changes slightly.

In 1579, before the telescope, Michael Mästlin drew 11 of the stars by eye. In 1610 Galileo turned his telescope on them and drew 36 stars in his diagram. In 1767 John Michell used them to argue that star clusters are real groups, not chance alignments, putting the odds of so many bright stars falling together by chance at about one in half a million. Charles Messier added them to his catalogue on 4 March 1769.

The dust

The nebula isn’t what’s left of the cloud the stars formed in. The cluster is passing through it by chance: radio measurements of its gas show it moving at a different speed from the stars, many times faster than the cluster’s escape speed, and most of the light comes from dust in front of the stars, on average about 2 light-years in front. Two clouds are involved, part of the Taurus clouds and one approaching from the west, and the cluster is moving through them at about 11 kilometres a second.

It’s blue because it’s the stars’ own light, reflected: in 1912 Vesto Slipher found that its spectrum copies the spectra of the stars lighting it, and small dust grains scatter blue light more than red. The fine streaks running through it, from the lower left to the upper right round Merope, lie along the Galaxy’s magnetic field there: in 2026 the James Webb telescope’s images showed their direction matching the field measured by the Planck satellite.

The brightest part, NGC 1435, the Merope Nebula, was found by Wilhelm Tempel on 19 October 1859 with a 4-inch refractor in Venice. Edward Barnard found a much smaller one, IC 349, with the 36-inch Lick refractor on 14 November 1890: a knot about 35 arcseconds from Merope, lost in its glare here unless the picture is stretched hard. NGC 1432, round Maia, was found on a photograph taken by the brothers Paul and Prosper Henry in 1885.

Far beyond

None of the big surveys that measure galaxies’ redshifts has covered this patch of sky, but China’s LAMOST telescope found a few galaxies among its spectra of stars. Two near the bottom, small fuzzy orange galaxies, are the farthest with measured distances: their light set out about 1.5 billion years ago. A cluster of galaxies, a scatter of small orange smudges at the top edge, is almost as far. UGC 2838, near the top, a long, thin disc seen edge on, is the most striking galaxy in the frame, about 280 million light-years away.

The farthest thing that shows is probably a faint point at the lower left, a quasar whose redshift, estimated from Gaia’s low-resolution spectrum, says its light set out about 10 billion years ago. Another faint point, at the right, might be farther, about 11.4 billion years, but Gaia’s own classifier calls it a star. And at the right edge, in January 2026, three years after this picture, a supernova went off in a galaxy whose light takes about 1.9 billion years to reach us.

In front and behind

HD 23712, the orange star at the right, is a giant about 900 light-years away, twice as far as the cluster. HD 23642, right of Alcyone, is the cluster’s only bright pair of stars that eclipse each other, and its eclipses gave another distance for the cluster. Teide 1, one of the first brown dwarfs ever confirmed, was found here in 1995; it’s in the frame, but too faint for this picture.

The colours

The colours are natural, from a colour camera. The cluster’s stars are blue-white, hot and young, and the nebula is blue because it’s their light reflected; the brown dust round it is lit only faintly, by ordinary starlight.

The picture

The telescope was a Celestron C8, an 8-inch Schmidt-Cassegrain, with a Hyperstar, working at about f/1.9 and a focal length of about 390 mm, with a ZWO ASI2600 colour camera. The picture is 11 hours 24 minutes in all, in December 2022. It’s 9,579 by 6,647 pixels, each covering 1.06 arcseconds, about half the camera’s own 2: it was enlarged in processing. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
