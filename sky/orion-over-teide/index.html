Orion over Teide

Orion setting, the winter Milky Way and the zodiacal light

Orion setting over the Teide plateau in Tenerife on a spring night, with the winter Milky Way across the sky and the zodiacal light rising beside the mountain. Looking at Orion, we look out through our own arm of the Galaxy: past the Hunter's stars and the red rims of the bubbles they've blown, to the Rosette and, high on the right, a nebula in the next arm out.

The light in this picture spans almost 7,000 years. The Orion Nebula’s and the belt stars’ set out between about 680 and 780: in 779 Offa of Mercia beat Cynewulf of Wessex at Bensington, now Benson, in Oxfordshire, and took the town. Betelgeuse’s left somewhere between about 1300 and 1530, depending on whose distance you trust, and Bellatrix’s around 1773, while Oxford’s Radcliffe Observatory was being built. Sirius’s set out in 2016, and Jupiter’s light was only 48 minutes old. At the other end, the Rosette’s left around 2800 BC, when Stonehenge was still a ditch and a bank and Wayland’s Smithy on the Ridgeway was already centuries old, and the Monkey Head’s, from the next arm of the Galaxy out from ours, around 4800 BC on its radio distance, before the first farmers reached Britain.

I took this from the Minas de San José, a plain of pale pumice on the Teide plateau in Tenerife, looking west-south-west in the first hour of darkness on an evening in April 2025. It’s from my portable rig, my Nikon D850 and a Nikon 20 mm lens on a small tracking mount: the sky is 22 one-minute exposures, and the ground was taken separately and blended in. It takes in about 67 degrees of sky by 65, about 130 full Moons across. Orion has a gallery of its own, and the picture is in the Orion Nebula’s too. Closer views are on other pages: the clouds behind the belt and sword in Orion Wide, the nebula in The Orion Nebula, and the Rosette in The Rosette and NGC 2244.

What’s in the frame

The camera faced west-south-west, with the summit of Teide, 5 kilometres away, to the right of centre. In April, Orion is already setting at the end of twilight: its belt was about 20 degrees up, and the whole sky was sinking towards the horizon on the right, where north is. So Orion stands leaning to the right, with Betelgeuse at the top, orange, Rigel at the bottom just above the rocks, the belt across the middle, and the sword hanging below and to the left of it.

The band of the winter Milky Way crosses the whole frame, almost level, from Canis Major and Puppis on the left to Auriga on the right. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, is on the left, and Procyon near the top. The brightest point of all, right of Orion, is Jupiter, and the pale cone rising past it from the horizon right of Teide is the zodiacal light. Each pixel covers about 32 arcseconds, about a sixtieth of the Moon’s width.

Orion’s stars

Orion’s bright stars look like a group, but they’re at very different distances. Bellatrix, the shoulder on the right here, is about 250 light-years away, a star in the foreground that only happens to lie in front of the rest. Betelgeuse is about 550, though estimates run from 500 to 720; Saiph, the other foot, is about 650 on the only measurement there is; and Rigel is about 860, or perhaps as much as 1,170.

The three stars of the belt, Alnitak, Alnilam, and Mintaka, are all about 1,250 light-years away, as far as the Orion Nebula. That’s a recent finding. The Hipparcos satellite measured them at anything from 700 to nearly 2,000 light-years, and many books still give those figures, but newer measurements, the last published in 2025, put all three at the same distance, among the young stars that formed out of Orion’s clouds.

Betelgeuse is a red supergiant late in its life, so large that in the Sun’s place it would reach beyond the orbit of Mars. In 2019 and 2020 it faded to well under half its usual brightness, and there was talk of a supernova: it was a cloud of dust the star had thrown off. Rigel, a blue supergiant, is Orion’s brightest star, and one of the most luminous the eye can see.

The Orion Nebula

The pink spot in the sword is the Orion Nebula, about 1,270 to 1,350 light-years away, the nearest place where massive stars are forming. Its youngest stars are under 300,000 years old, and the hottest of them, in the Trapezium at its heart, light the gas around them. To the eye it’s a misty star in the sword. The small red patch beside Alnitak is the Flame Nebula; the Horsehead, just south of it, is too small to show at 20 mm. Both are in Orion Wide, with the vast clouds behind them.

The red glow around Orion

Most of the red in the picture is hydrogen, made to glow by the light of Orion’s hottest stars. The arc curving above the belt is the brightest part of Barnard’s Loop, which E. E. Barnard photographed in 1894. It’s the edge of a bubble about 650 light-years across, blown in the gas by the winds and supernovae of Orion’s massive stars over the last few million years. Around Meissa, the star at Orion’s head, is a fainter ring, Meissa’s ring, about 10 degrees across: a bubble around a young cluster, thought to have been broken open by a supernova about a million years ago.

Hydrogen glows a deep red, and the filter in front of an unmodified camera’s sensor blocks much of that light, so it takes a fast lens and a long exposure to show it at all.

The Orion Arm

When we look at Orion, we’re looking outwards, away from the centre of the Galaxy. The Sun lies in a spiral arm named after Orion, the Orion Arm, or Local Arm, because Orion’s clouds and stars sit in the middle of it, about 1,300 light-years away. The Galaxy’s centre is the other way, in Sagittarius, in the summer sky; the winter Milky Way is our view outwards. The point directly away from the centre, the Galactic anticentre, is in this picture, in Auriga, about 4 degrees east of Elnath, up and to its left here. So the right half of the band looks out across our arm, and the left half, towards Canis Major and Puppis, partly along it.

Whether our arm is a true arm or a spur, a short branch between two of the Galaxy’s great arms, has long been argued. Gaia’s maps of young stars now trace it for at least 26,000 light-years, and on those maps, and on radio measurements of its star-forming clouds, it looks more than a spur, though not one of the great arms that wind all the way around.

Beyond it, about 6,200 light-years out in this direction, is the next arm, the Perseus Arm. The Monkey Head Nebula, the small pink patch high on the right, is in it, about 6,850 light-years away. That distance comes from a maser in its clouds, a spot where molecules amplify microwaves the way a laser amplifies light, sharp enough for radio telescopes to measure its tiny yearly shift against the distant sky. Gaia’s measurement of its cluster puts it nearer, at about 5,800. The Rosette, about 4,700 to 4,900 light-years away, lies in between, and on one recent model between the two arms.

Sirius, Procyon and the Winter Triangle

Sirius is one of the nearest stars, 8.6 light-years away, and Procyon is 11.5. Both have a white dwarf for a companion, the core left by a star that has already died. With Betelgeuse they make the Winter Triangle, the whole of which is in the frame. Of the larger Winter Hexagon, the ring of six bright stars around it, only Sirius, Procyon, and Rigel are: Capella, Castor, and Pollux are just beyond the edges, and Aldebaran, the eye of Taurus, is hidden behind the shoulder of Teide.

Jupiter and the zodiacal light

The brightest point in the picture is a planet: Jupiter, in Taurus that night, brighter than Sirius, its light 48 minutes old. It lies on the ecliptic, the Sun’s path around the sky, which is also where the planets are, and so it’s inside the zodiacal light: sunlight scattered by a thin cloud of dust in the plane of the planets, most of it shed by comets.

The zodiacal light is best seen on spring evenings in the west, when the ecliptic stands up steeply from the horizon after sunset. From Tenerife, much further south than Britain, it stands up more steeply still, at about 75 degrees that night against at most 62 from London, and twilight is over sooner, so it’s still high when the sky is fully dark.

Giovanni Cassini studied it in Paris in the 1680s, and decided it was out in space, not in the air. Nearly three centuries later, the first telescope at the Teide Observatory, about 8 kilometres from the Minas, was used to study it, and the measurements made there between 1964 and 1975 are still the basis of the standard maps of how bright it is.

Teide and the plateau

The pale ground is pumice, thrown out about 2,000 years ago by the last eruption of Montaña Blanca, on the east side of Teide; the Minas de San José are old workings where it was dug. The dark blocks scattered on it are lava. The plateau, at about 2,300 metres, is usually above the sea of clouds that the trade winds pile against the north of the island, and the sky over it is protected: the summits of Tenerife were made a Starlight Reserve in 2013.

The picture

This is from my portable rig, the one I take on trips: my Nikon D850, unmodified, with a Nikon 20 mm lens at its widest aperture, f/1.8, on a Sky-Watcher Star Adventurer GTi, a small mount that turns with the sky so the stars stay still. The sky is 22 exposures of a minute each at ISO 800, 22 minutes in all. A mount that follows the stars blurs the ground, so the ground was taken separately with the same lens, and blended with the sky. I cropped the picture and finished it in Photoshop.
