NGC 7331 and Stephan's Quintet

Three depths of galaxies in Pegasus

NGC 7331, a big spiral galaxy about 48 million light-years away, and Stephan's Quintet, half a degree from it in the sky but six times farther: four galaxies tangled in a collision about 290 million light-years away, and a fifth, much nearer, that happens to lie in front of them. The little galaxies round NGC 7331 aren't its companions either. Taken from my garden in Oxfordshire.

NGC 7331’s light left about 48 million years ago, in the Eocene, when the animals now found as fossils in the Messel Pit near Darmstadt, early horses and primates among them, were living by a lake there. The Quintet’s light left about 290 million years ago, in the early Permian, when the sail-backed Dimetrodon was alive. NGC 7336’s set out about 395 million years ago, in the Devonian, about when the oldest known footprints of four-legged animals were left in Poland. And the quasar’s light had been travelling for about 6 billion years before the Sun formed.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over three nights in early July 2025. It covers 1.3 degrees of sky by 0.9, about two and a half full Moons across, in Pegasus. It’s nine hours of exposures with a colour camera, through a filter that passes red, green, and blue light in three separate bands, with gaps between them that shut out much of the light of street lamps. Both of its main subjects have galleries of their own: NGC 7331 and Stephan’s Quintet.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left and east is down, so south is to the right and west is up. NGC 7331 is the big tilted spiral left of the middle, with four small galaxies, the Deer Lick Group, just below it. Stephan’s Quintet is the knot of galaxies at the upper right, half a degree away. The round glow at the upper left is NGC 7315, the ringed spiral at the lower right is NGC 7343, and the bright orange star at the left-hand edge is HD 214372, in our own Galaxy, about 1,550 light-years away.

The galaxies are at three depths. NGC 7331 is about 48 million light-years away. Nearly everything else, the Quintet, the four little galaxies below NGC 7331, NGC 7315, and NGC 7343, is between about 280 and 330 million light-years away, and a few fainter galaxies are farther still, at about 370 to 395 million. At NGC 7331’s distance the frame is about 1.1 million light-years across, and each pixel covers about 200 light-years; at the Quintet’s, it’s about 6.6 million light-years across.

NGC 7331 is about 4 degrees north-north-west of η Pegasi, north-west of the Great Square, and at magnitude 9.4 it shows in 50 mm binoculars from a dark site. From Oxfordshire it climbs to about 73 degrees in the south. In July it’s highest at dawn, and the nights are short: at this latitude the sky isn’t fully dark at all until about 22 July, and even nautical darkness, with the Sun 12 degrees below the horizon, lasts only about 3.4 hours at the start of the month and 5.4 at the end. The nine hours took three nights.

NGC 7331

NGC 7331 is a big spiral galaxy without a bar, about 145,000 light-years across, which we see tilted about 75 degrees from face-on. Its Cepheid variables, stars whose pulsing gives away how bright they really are, put it about 48 million light-years away. You’ll often read 40, 45, or 50 million: those come from other methods and roundings, which disagree by up to 20 per cent. Its near side is the west, at the top here, where the dust lanes cross in front of the bright bulge; the bulge’s glow spills out below them, on the far side.

It’s often called the Milky Way’s twin: NASA’s Spitzer team said so in 2004, and ESA’s Hubble team in 2018. The likeness holds in broad strokes, a big spiral with similar arms, but NGC 7331 has one and a half to two times the Milky Way’s mass in stars, forms stars about twice as fast, has a larger bulge, and has no bar. Astronomers who studied its neighbours in 2017 called it a twin of the Andromeda Galaxy instead. You may also read that its bulge spins the opposite way to its disc. That was claimed in 1996, but later measurements found no sign of it, and it’s now thought to have been an artefact of the analysis.

William Herschel found it on 6 September 1784. In 1849 George Johnstone Stoney, observing with Lord Rosse’s great telescope in Ireland, strongly suspected a faint spiral arm. Edwin Hubble’s list of 1929, from which he found that the farther a galaxy is, the faster it’s receding, put NGC 7331 at about 3.6 million light-years: about 13 times too close. And in 1965 Vera Rubin, with Margaret and Geoffrey Burbidge and others, measured how it rotates, five years before her better-known work on the Andromeda Galaxy.

Several supernovae have been seen in it. SN 2014C, just right of the core, was found in January 2014 as the explosion of a star stripped of its hydrogen; within a year its light was dominated by hydrogen, because the blast had run into a shell of about a Sun’s mass of gas that the star had thrown off decades or centuries before.

NGC 7331 does have real companions, though not the famous ones. NGC 7320, in the Quintet’s direction, is probably one. Another is a faint dwarf galaxy, dwarf M, left of NGC 7331 and a little lower, which German amateur astrophotographers imaged deeply and a Russian 6-metre telescope confirmed in 2016: it’s moving at almost NGC 7331’s speed, and its faint edges are drawn out towards it.

The Deer Lick Group

The four small galaxies below NGC 7331 are often called the Deer Lick Group. The amateur astronomer Tom Lorenzin named them in the 1980s, after the place he’d watched them from, Deerlick Gap Overlook on the Blue Ridge Parkway in North Carolina. They look like a family, but they’re six to eight times farther away than NGC 7331, so they’re big galaxies themselves, seen small.

NGC 7337A barred spiral inside a ring of arms, about 295 million
NGC 7340An elliptical galaxy, about 285 million

Herschel saw NGC 7335 in 1784, and Stoney, in 1849, saw the other three among “4 knots” following NGC 7331. NGC 7335, NGC 7337, and NGC 7340 belong to the same loose group of galaxies as Stephan’s Quintet, about 290 million light-years away, which spreads over several degrees of this part of the sky.

Stephan’s Quintet

At the upper right, Stephan’s Quintet was the first compact group of galaxies ever found. Édouard Stephan published it in 1877, from Marseille, with Léon Foucault’s 80 cm telescope, an early reflector with a silvered glass mirror; he saw the middle pair as one nebula. In 1982 Paul Hickson listed it as number 92 of the 100 compact groups he found on the Palomar Sky Survey’s plates.

Four of its five galaxies are about 290 million light-years away, tangled together within about 260,000 light-years. The fifth, NGC 7320, the largest and bluest, at the right of the group, isn’t one of them. It’s between about 40 and 50 million light-years away, receding at less than an eighth of the others’ speed, and it just happens to lie in front of them. Its low redshift fuelled decades of argument, through the 1960s and 70s, over whether a galaxy’s redshift really measures its distance; Halton Arp argued that the five belonged together, and so that redshifts couldn’t be trusted. Radio maps of the hydrogen round the galaxies split it cleanly into near and far, and in 2000 Hubble settled it by resolving NGC 7320 into individual stars, which it can’t do in the others.

The group is in the middle of a collision. NGC 7318B is falling in for the first time, about 900 kilometres a second slower in recession than the others, and as it ploughs into gas stripped from the group earlier, it drives a shock wave about 130,000 light-years long down its eastern side. The shock was found in radio waves in 1972, and in 2006 the Spitzer Space Telescope found it glowing in the infrared light of warm molecular hydrogen, the first time that light had been seen to dominate the spectrum of anything beyond our Galaxy. It doesn’t show in ordinary light, so it’s invisible here, but the thin blue streak running down from NGC 7319 is that galaxy’s young tidal tail, pulled out in an earlier encounter, and a fainter, broader tail comes out from behind NGC 7320. The small ringed galaxy below the group, NGC 7320C, is thought to have passed through it about 500 million years ago, stripping much of its gas.

NGC 7319’s nucleus hides a black hole of about 24 million times the Sun’s mass, shining like 40 billion Suns. The James Webb Space Telescope pictured the group in one of its first full-colour images, released on 12 July 2022, and measured gas flowing out from that nucleus. And the Quintet has been in a film: NASA says it’s prominently featured in It’s a Wonderful Life, of 1946.

The background

The rest of the frame is the background group and beyond. NGC 7343, at the lower right, is a barred spiral whose arms nearly close into a ring, about 330 million light-years away and about as luminous as NGC 7331; Truman Safford saw it in 1866, but his position was off and his list came out too late, so Stephan, who found it again in 1876, gets the credit. NGC 7315, the smooth round glow at the upper left, is a lenticular galaxy seen face-on, nearly as luminous as NGC 7331 but about six times farther away; Stephan found it in 1872. Near the right-hand edge, the thin needle of NGC 7320A is an edge-on galaxy at the Quintet’s distance.

Several of the NGC numbers round NGC 7331 turn out to be stars. NGC 7325 and NGC 7326, listed by observers looking hard at NGC 7331 in the 19th century, are double stars, and the galaxy above and left of NGC 7331 that some catalogues call NGC 7326 is really CGCG 514-066, a spiral about 370 million light-years away.

The most distant thing I found in the picture is a faint blue dot in the upper right, left of the Quintet: a quasar, the bright centre of a galaxy at a redshift of 2.08, whose light set out about 10.5 billion years ago.

The colours

The filter passes three bands, one each of red, green, and blue, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record, so the colours are close to natural. The ellipticals and the bulges of the spirals are cream, the light of old stars; the spiral arms and NGC 7320 are blue-white, the light of young ones; and the pink knots along NGC 7331’s arms are clouds of hydrogen lit by newborn stars.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here, behind an Altair TRI-RGB filter. The picture is nine hours of exposures over three nights in early July 2025, from the garden in Oxfordshire, and it’s 5,286 by 3,639 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight and finished it in Photoshop.
