Tarantula Wide

A starburst in another galaxy

The Tarantula Nebula, 30 Doradus, in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy about 162,000 light-years away that orbits our own. It's the biggest and brightest star-forming region in our Local Group of galaxies, and the cluster at its heart holds the most massive stars known. Around it are older clusters, the debris of supernovae, and fainter nebulae to the east.

The light in this picture left the Tarantula about 162,000 years ago. Our own species was young then: the fossil skulls found at Herto in Ethiopia are from about 160,000 to 154,000 years ago. Britain, as far as anyone can tell, was empty of people, and stayed so for another hundred thousand years.

This picture is two frames from iTelescope’s T72, a 20-inch reflector at Deep Sky Chile, joined side by side, taken in January 2023. It covers 47 arcminutes of sky by 20, about one and a half full Moons across. The nebula is from three narrowband filters, which pass only the light of hydrogen, oxygen, and sulphur, an hour through each; the stars are from red, green, and blue filters, 10 minutes through each. The Tarantula has its own gallery, and The Tarantula and Its Neighbours shows it in a frame more than three times as wide, with the Large Magellanic Cloud round it.

What’s in the frame

North is up and east is to the left. The Tarantula Nebula fills the middle and right of the picture, with its brightest part, round the cluster R136, just right of centre. At its distance the frame is about 2,200 light-years wide and 900 high, and each pixel covers about 0.28 light-years, nearly 18,000 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

It’s far south, 69 degrees below the celestial equator, so it never rises anywhere north of about 21 degrees north, which rules out all of Britain and Europe. From Deep Sky Chile it never sets. At its highest it’s 51 degrees above the southern horizon, and at its lowest, below the pole, it still skims about 10 degrees above it. It’s due south at midnight around 20 December, so January evenings are a good time for it.

Another galaxy

Everything in this picture, apart from a few foreground stars, is in another galaxy. The Large Magellanic Cloud is a satellite of the Milky Way, and the most accurate measurement of its distance, from twenty pairs of stars that eclipse each other, puts it at about 162,000 light-years, to within about 1 per cent. With its dark matter it’s about 140 billion times the Sun’s mass. It’s moving at about 320 kilometres a second relative to our Galaxy, and is probably on its first close pass, though that’s now debated. From a dark site in the southern hemisphere it looks like a detached piece of the Milky Way, about 10 degrees long, and the Tarantula is the bright knot north-east of its middle.

People in the southern hemisphere knew it long before Europeans: the Mapuche in Chile saw the two Magellanic Clouds as ponds in the sky, and the Boorong people of north-western Victoria as a man and a woman. Amerigo Vespucci reported them from his voyage of 1501 to 1502, and Antonio Pigafetta, on Magellan’s voyage, described them in 1520. Magellan never named them; the earliest record of “Magellanes Clouds” seems to be in a book of 1594, as sailors’ usage. A passage in al-Sufi’s Book of Fixed Stars, written in 964, is sometimes said to describe the Large Cloud, but it probably doesn’t: it mentions stars, not a cloud, and the Cloud would have been below his horizon.

The Clouds have mattered to astronomy ever since. Henrietta Leavitt catalogued 1,777 variable stars in them in 1908, and her rule of 1912, which lets Cepheid variables measure distance, came from 25 of them in the Small Cloud. Today the Large Cloud’s distance is one of the foundations under measurements of how fast the universe is expanding.

30 Doradus

The nebula’s other name, 30 Doradus, is a star’s: it’s the number Johann Bode gave it, as a star, in his catalogue of 1801, and Johann Bayer had already drawn it as a star in 1603. From a dark site it still looks like a fuzzy star to the naked eye. Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, cataloguing the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751 and 1752, listed it among his nebulae, straight after the globular cluster 47 Tucanae. His whole description: “It resembles the preceding, but it is fainter.” John Dreyer’s New General Catalogue gave it three exclamation marks: magnificent, very bright, very large, looped.

It’s the biggest and brightest star-forming region in our Local Group of galaxies, about 600 light-years across or more, depending on where you draw its edge. The cluster at its centre gives out almost 500 times as much of the ultraviolet light that makes hydrogen glow as the stars lighting the Orion Nebula. At the Orion Nebula’s distance it would stretch about 30 degrees across the sky. You’ll often read that it would cast shadows there too; that seems to have started in a press release, and it’s a flourish rather than a measurement.

It’s a starburst, a place where stars have formed far faster than usual, and for a long time: here for at least 25 million years, peaking in the last few million. So the frame holds several generations of clusters:

R136, at the heartAbout 1.5 million years old
NGC 2044, in 30 Doradus CA few million to 10 million
NGC 2100About 15 to 25 million
SL 639About 22 million
Hodge 301About 25 to 30 million

Astronomers have studied it closely. The VLT-FLAMES Tarantula Survey took repeated spectra of more than 800 of its massive stars, Hubble’s Tarantula Treasury Project catalogued more than 800,000 stars in its middle, and it was an early target for the James Webb Space Telescope, its picture released in September 2022.

R136 and the heaviest stars

The whitest part of the picture, just right of centre, is where R136 is: the cluster that lights the Tarantula, only about 1.5 million years old, with at least 50,000 times the Sun’s mass in stars. In my picture its stars are lost in the glow, and what you see there is the nebula. Its brightest star, R136a1, is the most massive star known. Its mass has come down as telescopes have separated it from its neighbours: 265 times the Sun’s in 2010, 315 in 2016, and about 200 in 2022, from speckle imaging that found a companion close beside it. It’s between about five and nine million times as bright as the Sun.

Just beside it, Melnick 34 is a pair of stars of about 139 and 127 times the Sun’s mass, orbiting each other every 155 days: the most massive pair known. Above R136 and a little to the left, the bright star R144 is another massive pair, weighed directly from its orbit at 74 and 69 times the Sun’s mass, and probably thrown out of R136. Further out to the right, VFTS 16 was flung out of the cluster about 1.5 million years ago and is now about 400 light-years away from it.

Hodge 301 and the northern ridge

Up and to the right of R136, the loose group of stars against blue nebula is Hodge 301, about 150 light-years from it. It’s the Tarantula’s old face, about 25 to 30 million years old, more than ten times R136’s age, and about 40 of its stars have already exploded as supernovae, helping to carve the cavities round it. The long bright ridge above the heart, running up to the top edge, is NGC 2069, the nebula’s northern leg.

30 Doradus B and a fast pulsar

Below and to the right of the heart, about 300 light-years from R136, the pale patch with orange filaments south of it is 30 Doradus B, NGC 2060, the debris of at least two supernovae. The younger, about 5,000 years ago, left a pulsar that spins 62 times a second, the fastest-spinning young pulsar found so far, and the most energetic of the more than 2,800 known. It shows only in X-rays. Nearby, VFTS 102 is one of the fastest-spinning massive stars known, its equator moving at more than 500 kilometres a second. It was once thought to be the pulsar’s former partner, thrown free when its star exploded; later measurements of its motion suggest not.

30 Doradus C, and a supernova just outside

In the lower right, the scatter of stars among orange and blue arcs is NGC 2044, a group of young massive stars whose winds and supernovae have blown 30 Doradus C, a bubble about 300 light-years across. It was the first such bubble detected in very high-energy gamma rays, by the H.E.S.S. telescopes in 2015, which means its shell is accelerating particles to very high energies. Right of it, near the edge, the small blue oval is the ring nebula round Brey 55, blown and lit by a single hot Wolf-Rayet star.

The most famous thing near here isn’t quite in the picture. SN 1987A is 42 arcseconds below the bottom edge, near the right-hand corner. Ian Shelton noticed it at Las Campanas in Chile on 24 February 1987, on a photograph and then with his own eyes, and Oscar Duhalde and Albert Jones saw it independently. It was the first supernova visible to the naked eye since 1604, and the only one so far whose neutrinos have been detected: they reached Earth hours before its light. In 2024 the James Webb Space Telescope found the best evidence yet that it left a neutron star.

The eastern side

The left third of the picture is quieter. The large orange nebula with blue-green knots, left of the middle, is N164, about 1,000 light-years east of R136, lit by massive stars buried inside it. In the top left corner, the small bright orange nebula round a supergiant star is N165, and the dark cavity with faint rims below it is DEM L 299, where the remnant of a supernova about 9,000 years ago lies along the same line of sight as an older bubble blown by a group of stars. To its right, DEM L 297 is a small blue-green bubble split in two by a lane of dust in front of it. The European Southern Observatory pictured this corner in 2004 as the Tarantula’s north-eastern outskirts; much of it lies in or near the rim of an even bigger shell, LMC 2, about 2,500 light-years across.

Two smaller things are worth finding. N161, up and left of the Tarantula, is a compact nebula with a blue-green core. And left of centre, the sharp blue streak running up and down for about 300 light-years is BSDL 2816, a filament of glowing gas inside LMC 2 that doesn’t seem to have been studied on its own.

Older clusters

At the bottom left, the rich round cluster is NGC 2100, about 900 light-years from R136 and about 15 to 25 million years old, old enough that its biggest stars have swollen into red supergiants: it has between 15 and 20 of them. Below the heart and to its left, the small clump of stars in blue-green nebula is SL 639, about 22 million years old. Like Hodge 301 and NGC 2100, it has red supergiants too massive for their age, which probably gained mass from a companion.

The colours

The colours are the Hubble palette: sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue. Hubble used exactly that mapping on 30 Doradus itself in 1994. They map something real. Oxygen glows in this line only once it has lost two electrons, which takes the energetic ultraviolet of the hottest stars, so the gas near R136 and inside the cavities is blue and cyan. Sulphur’s line comes from atoms that have lost only one, which survive only where the radiation has been filtered and softened, at the walls of the cavities and the faces of the dust, so the edges are gold. The stars are from separate red, green, and blue frames, so their colours are their own.

The picture

T72 is a PlaneWave CDK20, a 0.51 m reflector, at f/6.8, with an FLI ML16200 camera, at Deep Sky Chile in the Río Hurtado valley. Each of its frames is 27 arcminutes by 21.5, so this is two of them side by side, overlapping. The nebula is from an hour each through the Hα, OIII, and SII filters, and the stars from 10 minutes each through red, green, and blue, across the two panels: three and a half hours in all. Joined, it’s 7,904 by 3,296 pixels, each covering 0.36 arcseconds. I processed it in PixInsight and finished it in Photoshop.
