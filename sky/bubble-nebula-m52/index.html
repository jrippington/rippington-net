The Bubble Nebula and M52

A shell blown by one star's wind

The Bubble Nebula, about 9,200 light-years away in Cassiopeia: a shell of gas about 9 light-years across, blown by the wind of one hot star, in the glowing cloud round it, with the cluster M52 in the foreground, NGC 7538 forming massive stars at the edge, and the gold clouds of the Perseus Arm between. Six nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from the Bubble left it about 9,200 years ago, around 7,200 BC. Britain was still joined to the continent then, across Doggerland, low country now under the North Sea, and the people here were hunter-gatherers; within a thousand years or so the rising sea had made Britain an island. The light from M52 left about 5,100 years ago, around 3,100 BC, a few centuries after the long barrow at Wayland’s Smithy, on the Ridgeway in Oxfordshire, was built, and shortly before the first ditch and bank at Stonehenge.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over six nights in September 2025. It covers 1.5 degrees of sky by 1, about three full Moons by two, in Cassiopeia, on its border with Cepheus. It’s from two filters that each pass two narrow bands of light, one hydrogen’s red line and oxygen’s green-blue, the other sulphur’s red and hydrogen’s blue, and the stars are from exposures with no filter. The Bubble Nebula has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is down, turned a little to the left, so east is to the right and west to the left. The Bubble is the small, nearly perfect ring in the teal cloud at the upper right, with its star just below its centre, and the cloud round it is Sh2-162. The rich cluster at the lower right is M52. At the left edge is the bright knot of NGC 7538, at the top left the gold knot of Sh2-159, and across the lower left the broad gold and blue glow of Sh2-161, crossed by dark lanes of dust. At the Bubble’s distance, about 9,200 light-years, the frame is about 240 light-years across and 160 high, and each pixel covers about 2,500 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

It’s about 6 degrees from Caph, the western end of the W of Cassiopeia, roughly on the line from Schedar through Caph, carried on. From Oxfordshire it never sets: it passes 10 degrees north of the zenith, crossing the meridian around 1.30 am at the start of September, and through those nights it was more than 55 degrees up all through the darkness. M52 is easy in binoculars; the Bubble is faint, and hard to see by eye even through a telescope.

The Bubble

The Bubble Nebula, NGC 7635, is a shell of gas about 3 arcminutes across, blown by the wind of one hot star, BD+60 2522. Gaia puts the star about 9,200 light-years away, which makes the shell about 9 light-years across. You’ll often read 7,100 light-years away and 7 across, NASA’s figures from 2016; Gaia’s latest measurements put it nearly a third further. The gas in the shell isn’t the star’s own: its make-up shows it was swept up from the cloud round it. The shell is expanding at about 15 to 25 kilometres a second, and if it has done so all along, it’s only about 50,000 years old. Astronomers studying it in 2019 knew of no other bubble this size round a star of its kind that could be seen in visible light.

The star isn’t at its centre. It’s 53 arcseconds off, about 2.4 light-years, towards the shell’s northern side, below the middle here, where the shell meets denser gas. Gaia shows the star moving that way too, about 30 kilometres a second relative to the stars round it. So in 2019 one group suggested that the Bubble may be a bow shock, like the wave in front of a moving boat, seen from an angle that makes it look round. The next year another group, looking with the XMM-Newton X-ray telescope, found far less hot gas inside it than that idea predicts, and argued for a shell shaped by the star’s motion and by bursts of its wind in uneven gas. It isn’t settled.

William Herschel found it on 3 November 1787, with NGC 7538 on the same night. He noted a bright star with faint nebulosity, too late in the night to make sure of it, and suspected it might be an illusion; a year later he saw the faint nebulosity again. Hubble photographed it for the telescope’s 26th anniversary, in April 2016.

The star

BD+60 2522 is an O star, one of the hottest kinds there are: about 35,000 degrees at its surface and about 250,000 times as bright as the Sun, with about 27 times the Sun’s mass, from its spectrum. NASA’s 2016 release says 45, and you’ll read both. Its wind blows at about 2,000 kilometres a second and carries off about 0.4 times the Earth’s mass a year. It’s the bright star just below the Bubble’s centre. The brighter star up and left of the Bubble isn’t it: HD 220057 is in the foreground.

Sh2-162 and its trunks

The Bubble is a small part of Sh2-162, the teal cloud round it, lit by the same star, 40 arcminutes across in Stewart Sharpless’s catalogue of 1959; the speeds of their gas show they’re together. The Bubble is on the near side of a molecular cloud, and to its north, below it here, the star’s light is eating into it: the bright-rimmed trunks under the shell are dense, cold gas whose edges its light is boiling away. One of them reaches into the shell itself.

M52

M52, at the lower right, is a rich cluster of about 1,200 stars, about 5,100 light-years away, a little over half as far as the Bubble: the two are 37 arcminutes apart on the sky but about 4,100 light-years apart in depth. Its age is less certain, between about 85 and 155 million years in recent studies from Gaia’s data. Charles Messier found it on 7 September 1774 while following the comet of that year.

Its brightest star, BD+60 2532, on the cluster’s west side, left here, is a yellow supergiant that looks orange, reddened by the dust in front of the cluster. John Herschel noted a ruddy star on that side in 1829, and in 1835 William Henry Smyth saw the cluster as a triangle like a bird with outspread wings, an orange star at its point. Another supergiant, BD+60 2534, a cooler one, is above and right of the cluster, 15 arcminutes south of its centre, but it has the cluster’s distance and moves with it.

NGC 7538

At the left edge, just over the border in Cepheus, NGC 7538 is a younger, busier cloud. It’s lit by two pairs of hot stars, one of them an O3.5 star, among the hottest kinds there are, and hundreds of stars in it are less than a few million years old. On its southern side, above it here, massive stars are still forming. IRS 1, at the small yellow knot, is an O star still hidden in dust and still growing, gathering gas fast enough to smother the glowing region it would otherwise make round itself. The parallax of radio masers beside it, measured in 2009, puts NGC 7538 about 8,600 light-years away, to within about 5 per cent.

The gold clouds and the dark lanes

The gold knot at the top left is Sh2-159, a small cloud lit by one hot star, with dark globules on its western side, left here; its gas belongs to the same molecular cloud as NGC 7538’s. The broad gold and blue glow across the lower left is Sh2-161, large and faint, 55 arcminutes across in Sharpless’s catalogue, lit by a single hot supergiant about 9,700 light-years away. It’s easily taken for its neighbour the Lobster Claw, Sh2-157, but that’s beyond the top of the frame.

Dark lanes of dust cross the glow: LDN 1231, the broad dark band between the Bubble’s cloud and the left half of the picture, and LDN 1229 and 1230, the inky patches in the bottom left corner. Both are rated 5 on the scale of 6 for darkness in Beverly Lynds’s catalogue of 1962.

KjPn 8

Near the top right corner is a tiny red dot, KjPn 8: a planetary nebula, gas thrown off by a dying star. Its core is a ring only 4 by 2 arcseconds across, a few pixels here, but round it are giant lobes 14 arcminutes long, thrown out in at least three bursts in different directions, about 3,200, 7,200, and more than 50,000 years ago. They’re too faint to pick out here, and the frame’s edge cuts across them. It’s about 5,900 light-years away.

Near and far

Everything glowing in this picture is on one stretch of the Perseus Arm, the next spiral arm of the Milky Way out from ours, about 8,000 to 10,000 light-years away: Gaia puts the Bubble’s star and NGC 7538’s hottest stars at the same distance, within its errors. The Galactic equator, the midline of the Milky Way, crosses the top right corner.

The things in front stand out. M52 is a little over half as far away as the Bubble, and KjPn 8 about two thirds as far. The bright star up and left of the Bubble, HD 220057, is about 1,250 light-years away, seven times nearer, and has nothing to do with it, though some guides still say the Bubble surrounds it. V649 Cassiopeiae, the brightest star in the picture, at the bottom left, is a hot star with a companion that dims it by about a tenth of a magnitude every 2.4 days; its distance is uncertain, because its orbit confuses Gaia. And one star works the other way: BD+60 2537, an orange supergiant at the right, is listed in an old catalogue of M52’s stars, but it’s far behind the cluster, about 8,200 light-years away, with a young cluster just beyond the right edge.

The colours

The colours are the Hubble palette: sulphur red, hydrogen’s red line green, and oxygen blue, with hydrogen’s blue line blended in with the oxygen. So the Bubble’s cloud is teal, where the star’s hard light makes oxygen glow with the hydrogen, and the outer clouds are gold, where hydrogen and sulphur glow together. The stars are from exposures with no filter, so their colours are their own.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. In front of it, two Antlia dual-band filters each pass two bands 5 nm wide: one hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III, the other sulphur II and hydrogen-beta. The picture is 10 hours 10 minutes through the first and 18 hours 51 minutes through the second, and 38 minutes with no filter for the stars, over six nights in September 2025. It’s 6,080 by 4,080 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight and finished it in Photoshop.
