Melotte 15, Closer In

The cluster and the pillars that point at it

September 2024, 11 min read

On this page

  2. Melotte 15
  3. Everything points at the stars
  4. Beyond the wider picture

The heart of the Heart Nebula, closer in, about 6,800 light-years away in Cassiopeia: the young cluster Melotte 15 in its blue cavity, and round it the trunks, pillars, globules, and a lone tadpole of dust that its light is eating away, every one pointing back at the stars, with newborn stars at their tips. About eleven hours over two September nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from the Heart left it about 6,800 years ago, around 4,800 BC, as on the wider page; the fourteen months between the two pictures make no difference to that. The light from Tombaugh 4, behind it, left around 7,700 BC.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over two nights in September 2024. It covers about 1 degree of sky by 1.5, about two full Moons by three, in Cassiopeia. It’s from a filter that passes two narrow bands of light, hydrogen’s red and oxygen’s blue-green. It’s a closer view of the cluster and the pillars round it than Melotte 15, my two-panel picture of the Heart’s middle and the Fish Head, taken fourteen months later; this one is centred further south-east, and takes in the cavity’s southern and eastern walls in full. The Heart Nebula has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the upper left, so east is to the lower left, west to the upper right, and south to the lower right. The cluster, Melotte 15, is the group of bright stars a little above and right of the middle, in the Heart’s pale blue glow. Just below it, a dark trunk points back up at it. Along the bottom of the blue glow, the cavity’s lower wall rises in dark peaks, the tallest BRC 8, with BRC 9 to its left, and to the right, cut off on its own, is a dark tadpole. On the left edge, BRC 7 is a V of dust on its side, and its bright rim runs up to the top left corner. At the top, Tombaugh 4 is a knot of faint stars far behind. At the Heart’s distance, about 6,800 light-years, the frame is about 113 light-years across and 174 high, and each pixel covers about 1,800 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

The plane of the Milky Way lies beyond the lower right corner, so the chimney the Heart is venting, rising away from the Galaxy’s disc, would rise out of the left side of this picture; on the wider page, it rises out of the top. From Oxfordshire the Heart never sets. In September it was about 40 degrees up in the north-east at nightfall and climbed to about 80 degrees, 10 degrees north of the zenith, in the small hours.

Melotte 15

All nine of Melotte 15’s hottest stars, its O stars, are in this frame, and by a rough sum about four fifths of their ultraviolet light comes from the three earliest. HD 15558, the brightest, is a pair of massive stars, perhaps three, circling each other every 446 days. HD 15570, the bright star with spikes just below and right of the cluster’s middle, is the most evolved of them, its spectrum part-way to a Wolf-Rayet star’s, its surface showing nitrogen made in its core. HD 15629, left of the middle, is one of the hottest, still in the prime of its life. By the models of 2000 they’re about 90, 80, and 60 times the Sun’s mass, but later studies find them cooler and less luminous, which would make them lighter.

Two more show how different such stars can be. BD+60 497, the bright star above the cluster, is two O stars almost touching, circling each other in under four days. And BD+60 513, the brightest star below the cluster, just left of the trunk, spins so fast, about 300 kilometres a second at its equator, that its spectral lines are smeared out.

The pale blue is oxygen, made to glow only by the hardest ultraviolet light of the hottest stars. Here it fills a region about 70 light-years across round the cluster, spreading down and to the left, and runs out of the left edge.

Everything points at the stars

Every pillar and globule here has its bright edge, or its dark head, on the side towards the cluster, and its tail away: the cluster’s light is eating them away from that side, and their dense heads shade the gas behind them. A study of 2019 counted about 38 of these trunks round the Heart, most with one or a few stars at their tips: small, very young stars, less than a million years old, perhaps squeezed into forming by the cluster’s light, though whether it made stars that wouldn’t otherwise have formed is hard to tell.

The trunk just below the cluster is about 14 light-years long, its head edged with light, and young stars and an infrared source are catalogued at that head. The bright star on it is in front, only about 700 light-years away. Between it and the cluster runs a wavy bright-rimmed ridge, about 16 light-years long.

Below and left, CG 7S is a small cometary globule, an open arch of glowing hydrogen round a dense core, its apex towards the cluster; models suggest the cluster’s light squeezed it, and that it’s now re-expanding. Further down, BRC 8 is the tallest peak of the cavity’s lower wall, a ragged pillar like a witch’s hat, roughly 15 light-years high, with a star at its tip. BRC 9, left of it, has two horns, with a young star at its tip, and round it are tiny dark specks: all eighteen of the globulettes catalogued in the Heart are in this frame, clouds weighing from a few to 255 times Jupiter’s mass, too little for an ordinary star. Further left, a small pointed pillar has a newborn star at its tip.

The tadpole, to the right, is a dark globule on its own in the open glow, its head about a light-year across and its tail streaming about 5 light-years away from the cluster. Infrared surveys find a newborn star at the edge of its head, on the side facing the cluster. The tiny speck just beside its head is one of the globulettes.

On the left, BRC 7 is a V of dust with its tip towards the cluster, the end of the cavity’s north-eastern wall, and the stars forming in it are youngest nearest the bright rim: a sign that the cluster’s light set them off. At the top, below Tombaugh 4, a dark cloud hangs a trunk down towards the cluster, with a newborn star at its tip, and at the upper right, a small dark comet has a young star at the end facing the cluster, one of the youngest objects where the Heart meets its neighbour W3. Far down at the lower right, BRC 6, about 80 light-years from the cluster, is a small globule capped with glowing gas on the side facing it.

Beyond the wider picture

The strip along the bottom of this frame isn’t in the wider picture, and it holds two curiosities. LS I +61 303, at the lower left, is a massive star with a neutron star, the core left by a supernova, circling it every 26.5 days and giving off gamma rays as it swings round; in 2022 China’s FAST radio telescope caught it pulsing every 0.269 seconds. In 2004 it was proposed that the supernova had thrown it out of the Heart’s cluster, but Gaia puts it about 1,400 light-years farther away than the cluster. And just right of it, WeBo 1 is a thin ring of gas thrown off by a dying star, seen tilted; its companion, a giant, swept up some of that star’s wind and was polluted with carbon and heavier elements. Ron Webbink spotted it in 1995 on the Digitized Sky Survey while checking the position of LS I +61 303, about 5 arcminutes away. One survey had listed it as a possible galaxy.

Far beyond

Looking along the Milky Way’s plane, behind several magnitudes of dust, nothing beyond our Galaxy shows. The farthest thing known in the frame is a galaxy found only by its radio glow, in a search of 1987 for galaxies hidden behind the Milky Way, about 200 million light-years away; its position is known only to within 5 or 10 arcminutes, and it can’t be seen.

Tombaugh 4, at the top, is an old cluster of stars about 9,700 light-years away, about 700 million years old, far behind the Heart. And the farthest star with a well-measured distance is a hot star at the upper left, about 20,900 light-years away, three times as far as the Heart, perhaps in the Milky Way’s next spiral arm out. The nearest is HD 15851, below the tadpole, a near twin of the Sun about 159 light-years away; its light left it around 1865.

The colours

Only two narrow bands of light were taken, through the dual-band filter: hydrogen’s deep red, which falls on the camera’s red pixels, and oxygen’s blue-green, which falls on its green and blue ones. So the gas is brown and orange where hydrogen glows, pale blue where the hottest stars make oxygen glow too, and the dark trunks and globules are dust in front of the glow.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. In front of it, an Antlia dual-band filter passes two bands 5 nm wide, hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III. The picture is about 11 hours through it, over two nights in September 2024. It’s 3,940 by 6,030 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
