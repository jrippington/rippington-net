M106 and Its Neighbours

A spiral and the galaxies round it

March 2024, 9 min read

On this page

  2. M106
  3. Its neighbours, and galaxies that only look like neighbours
  4. Far beyond
  5. In front

M106, a spiral about 24.7 million light-years away in Canes Venatici, in a wide field from my portable 150 mm, in my garden in Oxfordshire: its companions, galaxies that only look like neighbours, clusters of galaxies billions of light-years behind, and quasars whose light set out nearly 12 billion years ago.

The light from M106 left it about 24.7 million years ago, in the late Oligocene, not long after some of the earliest known apes and Old World monkeys were living in what’s now Tanzania. The light from the group of galaxies behind it, about 340 million light-years away, left in the early Carboniferous, about when Westlothiana, nicknamed Lizzie, one of the earliest four-legged animals on the way to becoming reptiles, lived in what’s now Scotland: its fossil was found in a quarry at East Kirkton, near Edinburgh.

This picture is from my portable 150 mm Newtonian, set up in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken in February and March 2024. It covers 1.3 degrees of sky by 1.2, about two and a half full Moons each way, in Canes Venatici. It’s in natural colour, from a filter that passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands. M106 has its own gallery, and my deeper picture of it, from the 250 mm a year later, adds hydrogen’s light to show its jets and tells its story in full.

What’s in the frame

North is down, so east is to the right and west to the left. M106 is the large tilted spiral at the upper right. The edge-on galaxy beside the bright stars at the upper left is NGC 4217, and the small irregular galaxy left of M106 and a little below it is its companion NGC 4248. At M106’s distance the frame is about 560,000 light-years across and 530,000 high, and each pixel covers about 180 light-years.

From Oxfordshire M106 never sets, and it crosses the meridian only 4.5 degrees from the zenith, so in February and March it stays high all night, in long dark nights. At about magnitude 8.4 it’s a binocular object from a dark site.

M106

M106, NGC 4258, is a spiral galaxy about 134,000 light-years across. Water masers in a disc less than a light-year across, orbiting the black hole at its centre, give its distance by pure geometry, about 24.7 million light-years, and make it one of the three anchors of the cosmic distance scale; they weigh the black hole too, at about 40 million times the Sun’s mass. Its active nucleus fires jets that shock the gas of its disc into two extra, anomalous arms, which glow in hydrogen’s light. They don’t show in this picture, without a hydrogen filter; the deeper picture shows one of them.

Its neighbours, and galaxies that only look like neighbours

Some of the galaxies round M106 are its companions. NGC 4248 is at about its distance and disturbed by it; KDG 101, a faint dwarf above M106, and LV J1218+4655, a thin edge-on dwarf near the top, are two more.

Others only look close. NGC 4217, the edge-on spiral at the upper left, and the lenticular NGC 4220, near the bottom, are probably two or three times further away than M106, though catalogues that group galaxies by their speeds put them in its group. Further still, NGC 4226 near the top, the interacting pair NGC 4232 and NGC 4231 in the middle, and UGC 7325 at the top edge are a group about 340 million light-years away.

Far beyond

Behind those are clusters of galaxies, their members small orange smudges. One at a redshift of about 0.27, WHL J121739.7+472923, left of M106 and below it, has light about 3.25 billion years old; another, WHL J121627.7+465908, near the top, about 4.4 billion. The farthest cluster that shows, WHL J121828.9+474558, is below M106, its brightest galaxy a faint smudge whose light set out about 5.1 billion years ago, before the Sun formed. And a single galaxy, SDSS J1216+4701, a small pinkish blob near the top, is about 5.9 billion light-years away.

The quasars, the bright cores of distant galaxies lit by gas falling into huge black holes, look like faint stars. SDSS J1214+4717, at the left, is the brightest of the distant quasars here, its light about 11.8 billion years old. SDSS J1213+4719, near the left edge, is the farthest that shows clearly: a quasar whose light set out about 11.8 billion years ago, with gas flowing out of it at thousands of kilometres a second that carves troughs in its spectrum, which is why estimates of its redshift differ. The farthest catalogued in the frame, SDSS J1216+4655, near the top, is only just there in this picture, a faint smudge; it shows clearly in the deeper one, its light 12.1 billion years old.

In front

The brightest star, HD 106556, at the upper left, is an orange star of our own Galaxy, about 2,740 light-years away.

The colours

The colours are close to natural: the filter passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record. M106’s core is yellow with old stars, its arms bluer, with pink knots of glowing hydrogen even without a hydrogen filter.

The picture

The telescope is a Sky-Watcher Quattro 150P, a 150 mm Newtonian reflector, with its coma corrector, at about 519 mm and f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 1.49 arcseconds here. The picture is 9 hours 12 minutes through an Altair TRI-RGB filter, in February and March 2024. It’s 3,120 by 2,970 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
