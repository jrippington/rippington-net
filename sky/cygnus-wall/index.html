The Cygnus Wall

Where the light eats into the cloud

The Cygnus Wall, the brightest edge of the North America Nebula, about 2,600 light-years away in Cygnus: a front about 40 light-years long where the light of one hidden star eats into a dense cloud, with towers and pillars left standing, the dark Gulf of Mexico cloud above it full of young stars, and a star that flared in 2010. Five nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light in this picture left the nebula about 2,600 years ago, around 570 BC. Astronomers in Babylon were recording the Moon and planets night after night then; one of their clay tablets, now in Berlin, records their positions for the year 568 to 567 BC, in the reign of Nebuchadnezzar II. In Oxfordshire it was the early Iron Age, and the White Horse may already have been on the hill at Uffington.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over five nights in July 2025. It covers 1 degree of sky by 1.5, about two full Moons by three, in Cygnus. It’s from two filters that each pass two narrow bands of light, one hydrogen’s red line and oxygen’s green-blue, the other sulphur’s red and hydrogen’s blue, and the stars are from exposures with no filter. The North America Nebula has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is up, tilted about 20 degrees to the left, so east is to the left and west to the right. This is the southern part of the North America Nebula. Across the lower half runs the Cygnus Wall, a bright edge of gas, pale blue above and red-brown below, rising at the left into a tower and, further right, a pillar. Above it, filling the middle right, is the near-black cloud of the Gulf of Mexico, and the pale blue glow between them is the nebula’s Mexico. At the nebula’s distance, about 2,600 light-years, the frame is about 45 light-years across and 69 high, and each pixel covers about 700 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 23 times Neptune’s distance.

It’s about 3 degrees east-south-east of Deneb, the bright star at the swan’s tail. From Oxfordshire it never sets: it passes 8 degrees from the zenith, crossing the meridian between about 3.30 am at the start of July and 1.30 am at the end. But in the first three weeks of July the sky here never gets fully dark: the Sun doesn’t sink 18 degrees below the horizon until about the 21st. Through narrow filters the twilight matters much less. The whole nebula is a faint glow in binoculars from a dark site.

The North America Nebula

The North America Nebula, NGC 7000, is a cloud of hydrogen lit by one hot star, about 120 by 100 arcminutes, four full Moons wide, and about 90 light-years across. It’s the eastern half of one big glowing region; the Pelican Nebula, beyond the top right of the frame, is the western half. The continent’s outline isn’t the gas’s: it’s drawn by dark clouds of dust in front of it, the Gulf of Mexico and, further north, the Atlantic. Gaia’s measurements of the young stars in it put it about 2,600 light-years away. You’ll often read 1,500 or 2,000, older figures.

William Herschel saw it from Slough on 24 October 1786, as plainly visible diffuse nebulosity. Max Wolf photographed it at Heidelberg on 1 June 1891, a three-hour exposure, and later called it the America Nebula; in 1903 E. E. Barnard suggested “North America Nebula” instead, since that was the continent Wolf meant.

The Cygnus Wall

The Cygnus Wall is the nebula’s brightest edge, where ultraviolet light eats into the surface of a dense cloud: the gas boiling off the cloud glows as it’s stripped, and the harder light that makes oxygen glow fills the space in front. On the nebula’s map it’s Mexico’s Pacific coast. From the top of the tower at the left to the wall’s western end the bright edge is about 40 light-years long, and the crest, the classic wall, about 16. Where denser knots stand in the light they shade the gas behind them, and are left as towers and pillars. The pillar rising from the middle of the wall is about 4 light-years tall, lit on its top and its right side. At the wall’s western end the edge turns down, round the bright patch Beverly Lynds catalogued as LBN 354, the nebula’s Central America. The small dark lane at the bottom of the frame is Barnard 355, one of the dust clouds E. E. Barnard catalogued from his photographs of the Milky Way.

You’ll read that the wall has the most concentrated star formation in the nebula. The studies put that next door, in the Gulf of Mexico cloud.

The Bajamar Star

The light comes from the upper right: the wall’s lit faces look that way. The star that sends it, the Bajamar Star, is just 3 arcminutes beyond the right edge of the frame, hidden behind the Gulf of Mexico cloud, which dims it by about 9.6 magnitudes, about 7,000 times. It was only found in 2005, by its infrared colours. It’s a close pair of O stars, of about 70 and 30 times the Sun’s mass, and the brighter is the nearest star we know of that’s as hot as it is. Without its dust it would shine at about magnitude 3.5, as bright as ξ Cygni, half a degree beyond the left edge of the frame. It seems to have been thrown out of a cluster to the north-west, beyond the frame, about 1.6 million years ago.

Its name was chosen in 2016, from its place on the nebula’s map: just off the coast of Florida, where the Bahamas are, whose old Spanish name was the Islas de Bajamar, the low-tide islands. Before it was found, astronomers had blamed other stars, among them HD 199579, a hot star 20 arcminutes beyond the top of the frame, proposed in 1952. By 1958 astronomers suspected that the real one was hidden behind the dark cloud.

The Gulf of Mexico

The Gulf of Mexico, LDN 935, is a cold, dense cloud of gas and dust in front of the glowing gas, dark enough at its densest to dim the light behind it by 30 magnitudes or more. It gives the continent its gulf, and it’s the busiest place in the nebula for new stars: over 300 young stars have been found in it, almost all of them invisible in visible light. From its lower edge dozens of dark threads hang down towards the wall.

Young stars and a jet

At the eastern end of the dark cloud, the left-hand edge here, is the Herbig cluster, a small knot of very young stars still wrapped in their cloud, whose brightest members George Herbig found in 1958 by their hydrogen glow. One of them, HBC 722, flared in the summer of 2010, brightening about 75 times when the disc round it suddenly poured gas onto it. It has stayed bright since. The small red fan beside it is a nebula that appeared as it brightened, lit by its light.

It has an older cousin at the upper left: V1057 Cygni, the orange star, which Herbig had catalogued in 1958 as an ordinary young star, flared by more than a hundredfold in 1969 and 1970, one of the first stars seen to do it, and has been fading since. It’s a little behind the nebula, about 3,000 light-years away, with a small group of young stars.

Inside the dark cloud, near the right edge, the tiny red knot is HH 953: a shock where a jet from a newborn star hidden in the cloud slams into the gas round it. It’s the northern end of the biggest flow in the nebula, about 7 light-years end to end; its twin at the other end is too faint to show here.

In front

The brightest star in the picture, near the top, is HR 8035, an orange giant about 260 light-years away, a tenth as far as the nebula. The blue star with spikes at the lower left, V2200 Cygni, is about 1,070 light-years away; patches of unusual elements on its surface make it dim very slightly every 26 days as it turns.

The colours

The colours are the Hubble palette: sulphur red, hydrogen’s red line green, and oxygen blue, with hydrogen’s blue line blended in with the oxygen. Across the wall they show how the front is layered. Oxygen needs the hardest light to glow, so its blue fills the space facing the star; hydrogen and sulphur glow together at the front itself, in cream and gold; and behind it the cloud is red-brown, where sulphur’s glow mixes with the dust. The stars are from exposures with no filter, so their colours are their own.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. In front of it, two Antlia dual-band filters each pass two bands 5 nm wide: one hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III, the other sulphur II and hydrogen-beta. The picture is 7 hours 41 minutes through the first and 6 hours 51 minutes through the second, and 30 minutes with no filter for the stars, over five nights in July 2025. It’s 4,114 by 6,216 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, Affinity Photo, and Photoshop.
