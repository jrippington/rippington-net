The Total Eclipse of August 2026

Totality from Soria, Spain

Totality from Soria, Spain, on 12 August 2026, the first total eclipse on mainland Spain since 1905: the Moon's disc, the Sun's golden corona fanning out round it, a great pink prominence over 100,000 km long, and a fainter one, a thin thread, that NASA's spacecraft saw too. With the video of the trip Cal and I took to see it.

This picture is from the total eclipse of the Sun on 12 August 2026, which Cal and I went to Soria, in Spain, to see. It’s from my portable rig, my Nikon D850 and a Sigma 150–600 mm zoom on a small tracking mount, and it’s several exposures, bracketed and blended, because no single exposure can hold both the bright edge of the corona and its faint outer reaches. The Sun has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

The black disc is the Moon, exactly covering the Sun. Round it is the corona, the Sun’s outer atmosphere, golden and streaked with streamers. At the upper left, at about half past ten, is a pink prominence, a cloud of glowing gas standing off the Sun’s edge. Look carefully at about one o’clock and there’s another, a faint, pale thread; and at about half past four, tiny pink patches at the very edge.

The picture isn’t turned to match the sky. The Sun’s own north pole is towards the upper right, at about twenty-five past two, and to see the eclipse as it hung in the sky over Soria you’d turn the picture about 40 degrees anticlockwise, which puts the big prominence on the left. At the Sun’s distance each pixel covers about 1,150 kilometres, so the Earth would be about 11 pixels across.

Totality at Soria

At Soria the Moon covered the Sun completely from 20:29:01 to 20:30:42, Central European Summer Time: 1 minute 41 seconds. The Sun was only 7 degrees up, low in the west-north-west, and totality ended about 43 minutes before sunset, so the Sun set while the Moon was still crossing it. It was the first total eclipse on mainland Spain since 30 August 1905.

The Moon’s shadow raced from northern Siberia, close to the North Pole, across north-east Greenland and the far west of Iceland, where totality was longest, 2 minutes 18 seconds offshore, then across the Atlantic and northern Spain, to end at sunset in the Mediterranean. Spain saw it low in the evening sky everywhere, lower the further east.

The Moon looked 3.4 per cent wider than the Sun that evening: it was only two days past its closest to the Earth, and the Earth was near its furthest from the Sun. So it covered the Sun with about 32 arcseconds to spare on each side, about 23,000 kilometres at the Sun’s distance, enough to hide the lowest prominences. Its disc in the picture is very slightly oval, 1.3 per cent shorter one way than the other: so low in the sky, the air bent the light from the Moon’s lower edge upwards more than from its upper edge, and squeezed it, by just the amount the arithmetic predicts.

The corona

The corona is the Sun’s outer atmosphere, gas at one to two million degrees, far hotter than the Sun’s visible surface, at about 5,500 °C; why it’s so much hotter is still an open question. It’s a million times fainter than the surface, so it’s lost in the bright daytime sky, and it can only be seen when the Moon covers the Sun.

Its streamers follow the Sun’s magnetic field. Here the brightest runs out to the upper left, at about half past eleven, at least a million kilometres above the Sun’s surface, over the Sun’s equator; another fans out to the left. Over the Sun’s poles, at about half past two and eight o’clock, the corona is dark: these are the polar coronal holes, where the field opens out into space.

That shape says where the Sun was in its 11-year cycle. Near the cycle’s maximum the corona is round, with streamers in every direction; near the minimum, its streamers lie along the equator, with dark holes at the poles. The cycle peaked in October 2024, and by August 2026 its sunspots were down to under half the peak: this is a corona on the way down, its polar holes back, its streamers drawn in towards the equator.

The corona looked golden from Spain. NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day put that down to the low Sun’s long path through the air, and smoke from forest fires.

The prominences

A prominence is a cloud of gas held up above the Sun’s surface by its magnetic field, roughly a hundred times cooler and denser than the corona round it. It shines mostly in hydrogen’s red light, mixed with hydrogen’s blue lines and the corona’s white, so it looks pink. Seen against the Sun’s face, the same thing is a dark filament.

The big one at the upper left is about 110,000 kilometres long, more than eight times the Earth’s width, and it stands at least 41,000 kilometres above the Moon’s edge, and perhaps as much as 65,000 above the Sun’s surface hidden behind it. It’s the large prominence on the Sun’s south-east edge that solar telescopes on the ground caught in hydrogen’s light at the moment of totality, and the one NASA photographed from Spain as a pink, mountain-shaped feature on the eclipsed Sun’s left edge.

The faint thread at about one o’clock is a prominence too, about 47,000 kilometres high and pale beside the big one. NASA’s Solar Dynamics Observatory, watching the Sun in ultraviolet, shows a thin, curved thread at that place on the Sun’s north-east edge at 18:30 UT, at a high northern latitude where such threads form. It was faint in hydrogen’s light, which is why it looks so pale.

The tiny pink patches at about half past four are small prominences on the Sun’s north-west edge, low ones, about 10,000 kilometres high; or the pink rim of the Sun’s lower atmosphere, the chromosphere, showing where the Sun’s edge came back into view as totality ended. It may well be both.

The picture

The camera is a Nikon D850, 45.7 megapixels, with a Sigma 150–600 mm zoom, on a Sky-Watcher Star Adventurer GTi, a small tracking mount that followed the Sun: my portable rig. Each pixel of the picture covers about 1.6 arcseconds. The exposures were bracketed, a run of different lengths, because the corona fades by a factor of a thousand or more from the Sun’s edge outwards, and then blended. The picture is a square crop, 3,642 pixels across. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.

The next total eclipses

The next total eclipse in Spain is on 2 August 2027, in the far south: Cádiz, Tarifa and Málaga. The next on the British mainland is on 23 September 2090, at sunset in Cornwall.
