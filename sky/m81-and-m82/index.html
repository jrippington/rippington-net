M81 and M82

Two galaxies after an encounter

Bode's Galaxy and the Cigar Galaxy, about 12 million light-years away, a few hundred million years after they swung past each other: M81's grand spiral, M82's starburst blowing a wind of hot gas out of it, a glowing cloud 38,000 light-years out where that wind strikes, young dwarf galaxies born from the debris, all seen through the faint dust of our own Galaxy; and behind them quasars whose light set out nearly 12 billion years ago. 51 hours over many nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from M81 and M82 left them about 12 million years ago, at the turn of the Middle and Late Miocene, about when ash from a supervolcano in what’s now Idaho buried hundreds of animals at a waterhole in Nebraska, rhinoceroses among them, whose skeletons are still being uncovered where they lay. The light from the farthest quasar that shows, a faint point above M82, is a thousand times older: it set out about 11.9 billion years ago, more than seven billion years before the Sun and the Earth formed.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, 51 hours over many nights in March 2025. It covers 1.4 degrees of sky by 0.9, about three full Moons by two, in Ursa Major. It’s in natural colour, from a filter that passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands. M81 and M82 each have their own gallery, Bode’s Galaxy and the Cigar Galaxy.

What’s in the frame

North is to the upper right, so east is to the upper left, west to the lower right, and south to the lower left. M81, Bode’s Galaxy, is the large spiral below the middle; M82, the Cigar Galaxy, is the edge-on galaxy at the upper right, with a red plume of glowing gas across its middle. Between and around them, filling most of the frame, is a faint smoky haze of brown and grey: the IFN, dust in our own Galaxy, far in front. Upper left of M81 is a ragged blue patch, Holmberg IX, a young dwarf galaxy; in the top left corner is a third galaxy of the group, NGC 3077. And at the far right, close to the edge, is a faint orange-red streak, the cap, where the wind from M82 strikes a cloud 38,000 light-years out. At their distance the frame is about 295,000 light-years across and 180,000 high, and each pixel covers about 50 light-years.

From Oxfordshire they never set. In March they crossed the meridian about 72 degrees up, north of the zenith, and stayed above about 45 degrees all night, through nights of seven to nine hours of full darkness. The Moon was full on 14 March, so the darkest weeks were the first and the last.

The whole scene

M81, M82, and NGC 3077 are the heart of the M81 group of galaxies. M81 and M82 are about 126,000 light-years apart on the sky, and a few hundred million years ago they were much closer. Seen in visible light they look like separate galaxies minding their own business, but radio maps of their hydrogen show bridges of gas about 100,000 light-years long joining all three, torn out of them by gravity: as the astronomer who mapped them put it, you’d never guess from the optical picture alone that the three are strongly interacting.

In a model that reproduces that gas, M82 swung past M81 about 220 million years ago, at 25 kiloparsecs, about 80,000 light-years, and NGC 3077 about 280 million years ago, at 16, about 52,000. The encounters stoked bursts of star formation in both. Young stars, 30 to 160 million years old, now follow the gas bridges, in clumps and dwarf galaxies that formed from the debris: Holmberg IX, Arp’s Loop, BK3N, and the Garland beside NGC 3077. Even M81’s spiral may be the encounter’s doing: one study finds no sign of the slow, steady wave that usually drives spiral arms, and argues for arms raised by its companions’ tidal effects, though that’s still debated, as is whether this is the three galaxies’ first meeting.

M82 and its wind

M82 is a starburst galaxy: the burst of star formation at its centre came in two short episodes, about 10 and 5 million years ago, set off by the encounter. The supernovae of all those young stars heat the gas to 30 to 80 million degrees and drive it out above and below the disc as a superwind, at 1,400 to 2,200 kilometres a second, far faster than the galaxy’s escape speed. In visible light the wind shows as filaments of glowing hydrogen, the red plume across M82’s middle, reaching about 5 arcminutes, 17,000 light-years, each side; its hot gas, seen in X-rays, goes on at least 14 kiloparsecs, about 46,000 light-years, to the north.

On 21 January 2014 Steve Fossey and four of his students at University College London’s teaching observatory in Mill Hill found a supernova in M82, on the lower half of its disc here: SN 2014J, a type Ia, the closest of its kind in decades.

The cap

Far out along M82’s northern wind, about 11 arcminutes from the galaxy, at the far right of the picture just below a bright star, is the cap, a faint orange-red streak: a cloud about 11.6 kiloparsecs, 38,000 light-years, from M82, glowing in hydrogen’s light and in X-rays. Here the wind, travelling at about 800 kilometres a second, has run into a massive cloud in M82’s halo. It drives a slow shock into the cloud, which makes it glow in hydrogen’s light, the part that shows here, and just in front of the cloud the wind piles up into a fast bow shock, invisible to a camera, which shines in X-rays. The cloud’s oxygen, neon, magnesium, silicon, and iron are in the proportions supernovae make: the metals came from M82’s starburst. At 800 kilometres a second, the wind takes about 14 million years to get there.

The arcs left of M82

On the other side of M82, left of its plume, is a thin bluish arc, and its place matches the Southern Arcs, arcs of young stars about 5 kiloparsecs from M82 along its wind. The Hubble telescope’s study of them finds that about 85 per cent of their stars formed between about 150 and 70 million years ago, alongside M82’s own burst, perhaps where the hot outflow met cooler gas in the halo, or in cool clouds the wind carried out. The match here is by its position and its colour; the fainter arcs further left seem to be the dust of our own Galaxy.

M81

M81, Bode’s Galaxy, is a grand spiral about 12 million light-years away, from its Cepheids and the brightest red giants among its stars, about 94,000 light-years across. Its core is a gentle active nucleus round a black hole that the James Webb telescope’s measurements of 2026 put at about 48 million times the Sun’s mass, less than the 70 million of an earlier study. A supernova, SN 1993J, went off in its south-western arm in March 1993, found by Francisco García Díaz, an amateur in Lugo, Spain. And one of its globular clusters, a faint point far out to the left of it, is home to FRB 20200120E, the nearest repeating fast radio burst known outside our own Galaxy, a source of millisecond flashes of radio waves about 40 times closer than any other found beyond it.

Johann Bode found both galaxies on 31 December 1774; Pierre Méchain found them again in 1779, and Charles Messier added them to his catalogue in February 1781.

The IFN: dust in our own Galaxy

The faint haze that fills the frame isn’t in the M81 group at all. It’s Galactic cirrus, thin dust high above the plane of our own Galaxy, lit by the combined light of the Milky Way’s stars: astrophotographers call it IFN, integrated flux nebula. In 1976 Sandage showed that a bright example of it lay over this very field, and the Herschel telescope’s far-infrared maps of the dust match the hydrogen of our own Galaxy, not the M81 group’s. Its colours are a clue too: cirrus is a little bluer than starlight from galaxies. Here it’s strongest in the grey bands across the lower right, and in the brown clouds round Arp’s Loop.

Arp’s Loop, between M81 and M82, looks like a loop of stars pulled out of M81, and it does hold young stars born in the gas bridge, and older ones stripped from the galaxies; but much of its light is the cirrus in front.

The dwarfs

Holmberg IX, the blue patch upper left of M81, has some of the youngest stars of any galaxy near us: it seems to have been born about 130 million years ago, probably from debris pulled out by M82’s last passage, in a burst that has since stopped. At its top edge is a small pink nebula, a bubble about 1,300 light-years across round Holmberg IX X-1, an ultraluminous X-ray source, blown by its jets and wind. BK3N, a faint blue smudge below M81, is probably another young dwarf born in the debris.

KDG 61, the soft yellowish oval left of and below M81, is quite different: an old dwarf spheroidal galaxy, its stars mostly 12 to 14 billion years old. The little knot on its upper edge, KDG 61em, is a spark of star formation in M81’s outer gas, only in line with it. In the top left corner, NGC 3077 still has its hydrogen pulled 4 kiloparsecs away from its stars, and the Garland beside it is a string of young star clusters in its tidal arm.

Far beyond

Behind the group, the frame is full of more distant galaxies and quasars. The most conspicuous cluster of galaxies, round a big orange galaxy at the right edge just below the cap, has light about 2.7 billion years old; the farthest cluster that shows, a small faint smudge right of the middle, about 5.6 billion. A quasar at redshift 1.98, SDSS J0956+6900, shines through M81’s outer spiral arm, its light about 10.5 billion years old.

The farthest thing that shows is DESI J149.4692+69.5409, a faint clean point above M82 and left of it: a quasar whose light set out about 11.9 billion years ago, when the universe was about 1.9 billion years old. SDSS J0955+6842, at the lower left, almost as far, is the brightest of the distant quasars. The farthest catalogued in the frame, PSO J148.4829+69.1812, below and right of M81, is a quasar at redshift 5.84, whose light set out 12.8 billion years ago, when the universe was under a billion years old; but its light has been stretched so far into the infrared that it falls beyond the filter, and it can’t show.

The colours

The colours are close to natural: the filter passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record. M81’s core is yellow with old stars, its arms blue with young ones; M82’s red plume is hydrogen’s light, which the filter’s red band passes; and the cirrus is brown and grey, the colour of starlight scattered by dust.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.87 arcseconds here. The picture is 51 hours through an Altair TRI-RGB filter, over many nights in March 2025. It’s 5,947 by 3,659 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
