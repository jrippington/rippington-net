The Elephant's Trunk

A pillar of dust in IC 1396

The Elephant's Trunk, a pillar of dust and gas about 20 light-years long in the glowing cloud IC 1396, about 3,000 light-years away in Cepheus: its bright rim eaten by the light of the hot star at the top of the frame, a hole in its head blown by a young star, and protostars still forming inside. Two nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from IC 1396 left it about 3,000 years ago, around 1000 BC, in Britain’s later Bronze Age. Sometime between about 1380 and 210 BC, by the dating of the soil beneath it, someone cut the White Horse into the downs above the Vale of White Horse in Oxfordshire, so this light set out within the centuries when it may first have been made.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over two nights in August 2024. It covers 1 degree of sky by 1.5, about two full Moons by three, in Cepheus. It’s from a filter that passes two narrow bands of light, hydrogen’s red line and oxygen’s green-blue, its gold and blue made by working the colours in Photoshop; the stars are from exposures with no filter. IC 1396 has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the right, so east is up, west down, and south to the left. The bright blue star at the top left is HD 206267, the star whose light makes all of this glow. The Elephant’s Trunk is the long dark pillar rising from the lower left, its curled head, rimmed with light, facing up towards that star. Below it, the brightest gold rims belong to IC 1396B, and at the lower right is another dark globule, IC 1396D. The whole glow is IC 1396. At its distance, about 3,000 light-years, the frame is about 51 light-years across and 77 high, and each pixel covers about 810 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 27 times Neptune’s distance.

From Oxfordshire it never sets: in mid-August it crosses the meridian about 1 am, 84 degrees up, just north of the zenith. The sky had only been getting fully dark again since late July, under three hours a night at the start of August and about six by the end.

IC 1396

IC 1396 is a great cloud of glowing hydrogen nearly 3 degrees across, more than five times the full Moon’s width, lit by the hot stars at the heart of the young cluster Trumpler 37, about 4 million years old. Gaia’s measurements of the cluster’s stars put it about 3,000 light-years away; you’ll also read 2,400 or 2,450, older figures. The gas round it lies in a shell about 80 light-years across, expanding at about 5 kilometres a second, and on that shell sit more than 20 dark globules with bright rims, the densest knots left behind. This frame takes in about a quarter of it, its western side, from the central star out to the rim where most of the globules are.

E. E. Barnard found it on a photograph of 1893, a seven-hour exposure. In 1956 Pottasch lettered the most prominent globules from A to H in order of their distance from the star, so the Elephant’s Trunk is IC 1396A.

The Elephant’s Trunk

The Elephant’s Trunk is a cloud of dust and gas of about 200 times the Sun’s mass, a pillar at the edge of the glowing gas, with its head pointed back at the star that’s eating it. Its head is about 5 arcminutes across, about 4 light-years, and the trunk runs down from it about 20 light-years to IC 1396B. The bright rim over the top of the head is about 13 light-years from HD 206267: the edge where the star’s ultraviolet light is boiling the cloud away. It’s in front of the glowing gas, a silhouette against it, and its rim is the brightest of all the globules’, probably because it’s the closest to the star.

In its head is a hole, the elephant’s eye, under a light-year across, with stars showing through. The bright star in it, LkHα 349a, is a young star only about 300,000 to 400,000 years old, spinning near its breaking speed, and its wind seems to have swept the hole clear in about 100,000 years. The fainter star just beside it, LkHα 349c, still has a disc round it. The soft blue glow just below the head is vdB 142, a small reflection nebula, dust lit by a hot star in front of the globule; you’ll often see the name used for the whole trunk.

Stars being born

The trunk is dark in visible light but bright in infrared. The Spitzer Space Telescope’s pictures of it, among its first, released in December 2003, found protostars hidden in it, several very young ones within a few hundredths of a light-year of the rim. In 2012 the Chandra X-ray telescope found more than 250 young stars in and round it, getting younger from the star towards the globule, which suggests the star’s light set off the formation of many of them as it squeezed the cloud. The youngest is IC 1396A-PACS-1, found by the Herschel telescope just inside the rim: a protostar less than 100,000 years old, still wrapped in cold dust and invisible here. Not everyone reads the evidence the same way, and some studies find stars formed in several episodes. Either way the trunk hasn’t long: the star’s light should wear it away in about 2 million years, and the stars forming in it now are probably its last.

More globules

Down the trunk, IC 1396B has the brightest gold rims in the picture, and below it IC 1396C is another dark cloud with a gold edge facing the star. At the lower right, IC 1396D is a big bright-rimmed globule with a young star buried in it, the farthest of these from HD 206267. At the top, Barnard 161 is a small comet-shaped dark cloud with a sharp black head and a tail to the right, just as Barnard described it; its gas moves differently from IC 1396’s, and it’s probably not part of it.

The star that lights it

HD 206267, at the top left, is a system of hot stars at the heart of Trumpler 37. Its brightest part is a pair circling each other every 3.7 days, of about 28 and 18 times the Sun’s mass, both of the hottest kind, type O; it’s at about magnitude 5.6, just visible to the eye from a dark site. One of its companions, further out, shows as a little bump just right of the bright star. Barnard noted it on his discovery plate as the brightest star of the group.

Far beyond

This frame looks along the Milky Way’s disc, through IC 1396’s own dust and much more beyond it, and nothing in it can be confirmed as a galaxy or a quasar. A strong radio source in the upper middle, with nothing visible at its place, may well be a distant galaxy, but no one has identified it. So the farthest things that show are stars far behind the nebula. AS 470, at the left, is about 30,000 light-years away by Gaia’s measurement, ten times further than IC 1396, and LS III +57 10, above the trunk’s head, about 24,000, which makes it very luminous, probably a supergiant. Their light left them in the last ice age.

In front

The second-brightest star at the top, HD 206482, is a foreground star about 335 light-years away. And a star right on the trunk, BD+56 2600B, is about 750 light-years away, well in front of it. The bright star at the bottom, HD 205196, is a hot supergiant about 3,300 light-years away, a little behind the nebula.

The colours

Only two bands of light were taken, hydrogen’s red line and oxygen’s green-blue, so the gold and blue are colour worked in Photoshop. Gold means more hydrogen glow, blue more oxygen. Oxygen needs the hardest light to glow, so the gas nearest HD 206267, at the top, is blue, and the gas further away and at the rims, where fresh gas is being ionised, is gold. The stars are from exposures with no filter, so their colours are their own.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. In front of it, an Antlia dual-band filter passes two bands 5 nm wide, hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III. The picture is 7 hours 56 minutes through it, and 30 minutes with no filter for the stars, over two nights in August 2024. It’s 4,032 by 5,996 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
