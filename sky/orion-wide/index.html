Orion Wide

The Orion molecular clouds in the light of hydrogen

Orion's belt and sword in the light of hydrogen alone, with the stars taken out. Behind the familiar stars are two giant clouds of gas and dust about 1,300 to 1,400 light-years away, the nearest place where stars of every size, the most massive included, are being born, and the glowing gas is where young stars are breaking out of them: the Orion Nebula, the Flame, and the curtain behind the Horsehead.

The light from the clouds and the stars in front of them left between about AD 550 and 780. The Orion Nebula’s set out around 756, a generation after Bede finished his Ecclesiastical History of the English People; Orion A’s, on average, around the 610s, the time of the Sutton Hoo ship burial; and the light from the furthest dust in the frame, in the 500s.

This picture is six frames from iTelescope’s T10, a 130 mm refractor at Siding Spring in Australia, joined into one, taken in December 2022. It covers about 7.4 degrees of sky by 5, fourteen full Moons across and ten high, from Orion’s belt to its sword. It’s made from one narrowband filter, which passes only the red light of hydrogen, 21 hours 30 minutes through it in all, and the stars have mostly been removed, so the gas shows on its own. Three things in it have galleries of their own: the Horsehead Nebula, the Flame Nebula, and the Orion Nebula.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left and east is down, so the belt runs from the top left towards the middle, and the sword lies on its side on the right. The three belt stars were Mintaka, at the top left, Alnilam, and Alnitak, just left of the middle; with the stars removed, only soft smudges mark where they were. The sword hangs from the belt, so it’s to the right of it here, with the Orion Nebula its brightest part. At the clouds’ distance the frame is about 170 light-years across and 115 high, and each pixel covers about a thousand times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

The belt sits almost exactly on the celestial equator, so everyone on Earth can see Orion. From London the belt climbs to about 37 degrees in the south, and it’s due south at midnight in mid-December. From Siding Spring it’s 60 degrees up in the north and, to a northern eye, upside down, with the sword above the belt. In December 2022 it was highest there about 1 am. The Orion Nebula is visible to the naked eye, a misty patch in the sword.

The Orion molecular clouds

Almost everything that glows here is the surface of two vast, cold clouds of gas and dust, Orion A and Orion B, each of the order of 100,000 times the Sun’s mass. Orion A is the long cloud behind the sword, and only its northern end, its head, is in the frame: the rest runs off beyond the right-hand edge. Orion B, east of the belt and below it here, fills the lower half of the picture, from the Horsehead and the Flame down to M78. Together they’re the nearest place where stars of every size are forming now, the most massive included. They’ve made at least 10,000 stars in the last 12 million years, and the Spitzer Space Telescope found nearly 3,500 young stars still wrapped in dust in them.

The stars that formed here earlier make up the Orion OB1 association, in groups of different ages: the oldest, north-west of the belt and beyond this frame, about 8 to 12 million years old; the belt stars and the stars round them; the sword’s stars, a little younger, in front of Orion A; and the youngest of all, in the Orion Nebula, less than 2 million years old. Their winds and supernovae have blown a cavity in the gas about 1,000 light-years across, the Orion-Eridanus superbubble, and Barnard’s Loop, a bright arc on its eastern side, passes just beyond the bottom of this picture.

Gaia and radio telescopes have measured the distances, and the clouds are much deeper than they look. Orion A’s head, round the Orion Nebula, is about 1,270 light-years away, but its tail, off the right-hand edge, recedes to at least 1,530: the cloud is about 300 light-years long, not the 130 it looks, and points almost straight away from us. Orion B is on average about 1,380 light-years away.

You’ll often read 1,344 light-years for the Orion Nebula, from radio measurements of 2007 to 2009, and before that, 1,500; the newer figure is from a larger sample of stars. The belt stars used to have distances from the Hipparcos satellite of 700 to 2,000 light-years; Mintaka and Alnilam turn out to be about 1,250, a little in front of the clouds, and Alnitak’s distance is still uncertain. They and the clouds lie in our own spiral arm of the Galaxy, which is named the Orion Arm after the constellation.

Why hydrogen light

Hydrogen glows red, at 656 nanometres, when ultraviolet light has stripped its electron and it’s recaptured, and only hot, massive stars give out enough ultraviolet to do it. So a picture in hydrogen light is a map of where those stars are eating into the clouds: θ¹ Orionis C, the brightest of the Trapezium stars, lights the Orion Nebula; σ Orionis lights the curtain behind the Horsehead; and a star hidden inside the Flame lights the Flame. Where the dust is thick enough it blocks the glow behind it and shows dark, as the Horsehead does.

The sword

On the right, the brightest thing in the picture is the Orion Nebula, M42, about 24 light-years across, lit by the Trapezium, the young cluster at its heart. Its middle is so bright that it’s a featureless white even here. Just left of it, cut off by a dark lane of dust, is the round disc of M43, lit by a single star. Behind the nebula, in the dense cloud and invisible in this light, a group of massive young stars broke apart about 500 years ago, as we see it, flinging out stars and spraying streamers of gas in every direction.

Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc seems to have been the first to record the nebula, in November 1610, though his note wasn’t found until 1916; Johann Baptist Cysat saw it the next year. Christiaan Huygens drew it in 1656 and was long credited with finding it, Charles Messier added it to his catalogue in 1769, and in 1880 Henry Draper made the first photograph of any nebula, of this one.

Left of M43, the bright patch is the Running Man, lit by a cooler star, 42 Orionis: in 2016 seven young stars near it were found trailing envelopes of evaporating gas, the first such “proplyds” round a star of its kind. Above and to the right of the Orion Nebula, the ring of bright arcs about 40 arcminutes across seems to be the edge of a bubble blown by θ¹ Orionis C’s wind, about 13 light-years across and expanding at 13 kilometres a second. The sword’s tip, further right, is ι Orionis, a pair of stars on a very eccentric orbit, which may have been involved in throwing two other stars out of the young cluster about 2.5 million years ago.

Near the right-hand edge are the marks of young stars’ jets. The small bright knot is NGC 1999, lit by a young star, V380 Orionis; its famous dark patch was long taken for a cloud, until the Herschel space telescope showed in 2010 that it’s a hole, blown by the star’s jets. Nearby are HH 1 and HH 2, shocks where twin jets from a buried star hit the gas round it, found by George Herbig and Guillermo Haro around 1950, which gave all such objects their name. And the thin bright arc left of them is HH 222, the Waterfall, a giant bow shock from V380 Orionis, no more than 28,000 years old.

The Horsehead and IC 434

Left of the middle, the bright curtain is IC 434, gas boiled off the face of Orion B and lit by σ Orionis, about half a degree above it here. σ Orionis is a triple star whose orbits give the most precise distance in the picture, about 1,264 light-years, and round it is a young cluster with brown dwarfs and planets drifting free of any star. Rising out of the curtain’s lower edge is the Horsehead, Barnard 33: a pillar of dust and gas of about 27 times the Sun’s mass, whose head stands about 2 light-years above the cloud. It’s lit from behind, it has two young stars at its tip, and it has perhaps 5 million years left before it’s worn away.

Williamina Fleming spotted it in 1888, on a photographic plate at Harvard, as a semicircular dent in the glowing strip south of Alnitak. The catalogue that first listed the nebula credited the Harvard finds to “Pickering”, and her part was recognised only later. The James Webb Space Telescope photographed its mane in 2024, the sharpest picture of it yet.

Below the Horsehead and to its left, the small glow is NGC 2023, dust lit by a young star at its centre, which William Herschel found in 1785. Further left, the round patch is IC 432, lit by a magnetic star whose spin is measurably slowing.

The Flame

Just below and left of where Alnitak was, the bright branching nebula with a dark lane through it is the Flame, NGC 2024, which William Herschel found on New Year’s Day 1786. It looks as if Alnitak lights it, and many pictures say so, but it’s lit by a star buried inside it, IRS 2b, behind the dark lane. Behind the dust is a cluster of several hundred young stars, the youngest, about 200,000 years old, at the centre, and the oldest, about 1.5 million, at the edges.

The belt and the clouds beside it

The belt stars are all hot, massive, and young, the brightest members of Orion OB1. Mintaka is an eclipsing pair with a third star; Alnilam, a blue supergiant of about 28 times the Sun’s mass, is spinning so fast it’s flattened; Alnitak is a close pair whose distance is still uncertain. Above Alnilam in the picture, the big bright cloud with rims facing it is a set of remnant clouds, leftovers of the cloud the belt stars formed from, which those stars’ light is clearing away. Nearer Mintaka, IC 423 is a small cloud with a bright rim. And in the lower right, below the sword, the bright-rimmed cloud over a dark V-shaped hollow is another of these remnants.

M78 and its neighbours

At the bottom left, M78 is a cloud of dust lit by two hot stars, the brightest of a group of such nebulae, found by Pierre Méchain in 1780. It glows blue in ordinary pictures, so in hydrogen light it’s small and faint. Beside it is NGC 2071, and just right of them, the tiny bright knots are HH 24, five jets from a small group of young stars that has already thrown out one of its members. In 2004 an amateur astronomer, Jay McNeil, found a new nebula beside them, McNeil’s Nebula, lit by a young star flaring up. It doesn’t show in this picture, and its star had faded by 2018.

The picture

T10 is a Takahashi TOA-130, a 130 mm apochromatic refractor, with a focal reducer and an SBIG STX-16803 camera, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. Each of its frames is 2.77 degrees square, so this is six of them, three across and two down, overlapping. The picture is from 21 and a half hours through the Hα filter, across the six panels. Joined, it’s 11,043 by 7,460 pixels, each covering 2.4 arcseconds. I processed it in PixInsight and finished it in Photoshop.

It has only one filter, so it’s in greyscale: brighter means more hydrogen light. With the stars removed, the soft round glows at the belt stars’ places are what’s left of them.
