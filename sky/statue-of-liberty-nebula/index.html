The Statue of Liberty Nebula (NGC 3576), Nyx

Statue of Liberty Nebula

A hidden cluster, and the bubble its stars have blown

January 2023, 9 min read

A giant cloud of glowing gas about 9,000 light-years away in Carina. At its heart is a cluster of young, massive stars so deeply buried in dust that none of them can be seen in visible light. Their winds and light have blown a bubble above the cloud, and in some pictures the bubble and the dark pillars inside it look like the Statue of Liberty.

The light in this picture left the nebula about 9,000 years ago, around 7,000 BC, give or take a thousand years. About then, in what’s now Turkey, people were building Çatalhöyük, one of the first towns, its houses packed so close together that they were entered through their roofs. NGC 3603’s light, just out of the frame, is about 23,000 years old: it set out about 14,000 years before the light in this picture, and arrives with it.

This picture is a patch of sky 37 arcminutes square, a little larger than the full Moon, taken with iTelescope’s T59, a 20-inch reflector at Siding Spring in Australia, in January 2023. It’s made from three narrowband filters, which pass only the light of hydrogen, oxygen, and sulphur, with 3 hours 15 minutes through each. The nebula has its own gallery, and it’s also left of centre in my wide-field mosaic of the region, between the Carina Nebula and the Running Chicken.

What’s in the frame

The nebula is in Carina, about 61 degrees south of the celestial equator, so from Britain it never rises. From Siding Spring, at 31 degrees south, it never sets, and at its highest it’s about 60 degrees above the horizon.

Its distance is less certain than you might expect. Radio measurements of how fast its gas is moving, read against the Galaxy’s rotation, put it at about 9,000 to 10,000 light-years; Gaia, the European Space Agency’s star-mapping satellite, puts the O stars round it nearer 8,000. About 9,000 is a fair round figure, and at that distance the frame is about 96 light-years across. Each pixel covers 0.54 arcseconds, about 1,500 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

The statue

The name is usually credited to Steve Mazlin, of the Star Shadows Remote Observatory, who suggested it in 2009; the astronomy writer Phil Plait said he proposed it because the nebula, though well studied, had no common name. With north up, as here, the figure stands upright. The tall dark pillar on the left is her raised arm, holding the torch at its top, and the rounder dark cloud to its right is her head. The glowing gas below is her gown, and the bright, knotted gas at the bottom, where the light is strongest, is the pedestal.

Six numbers for one nebula

John Herschel found it from the Cape of Good Hope, on 14 and 16 March 1834, and catalogued its brighter pieces separately: they became NGC 3576, 3579, 3581, 3582, 3584 and 3586. Most modern catalogues and papers use NGC 3576 for the whole nebula and put it on the brightest part, the pedestal. But Harold Corwin, whose NGC/IC Project rechecked the catalogue’s identifications against its discoverers’ notes, concluded that Herschel’s NGC 3576 was a small, separate patch about 4 arcminutes south-west of the rest, catalogued since as ESO 129-5. On his reading the brightest part is NGC 3581. The whole complex is also RCW 57A, and with its neighbour NGC 3603 it’s Gum 38.

The hidden cluster

The stars that light the nebula are just south-west of the brightest gas, in a cluster inside the dark cloud. The dust in front of it dims their light by about 16 magnitudes, a factor of about two million, so in visible light nothing of them shows: what you see is gas lit from within. Infrared cameras see through the dust. A survey in 2002 found 315 stars there, and the eight brightest showed no sign yet of a massive star’s normal spectrum: they’re still wrapped in the cocoons they formed in. A later infrared survey found 33 more sources so deeply buried that they’re probably massive stars still forming.

The cluster is one of the youngest massive clusters in the Milky Way, no more than a million or two years old, and at most about 5,400 times the Sun’s mass. It gives out about 1.7 million times as much energy as the Sun, mostly as infrared light from dust it has warmed. That’s about twice what the stars found so far can account for, so some of its hottest stars, worth about four of the O5 type, haven’t been identified. Water, methanol, and hydroxyl masers (natural microwave lasers, which mark where massive stars are forming) cluster at its centre.

The bubble

Above the cloud, the gas is a bubble about 14 arcminutes across, roughly 35 light-years at this distance. The winds and light of the massive stars have blown it out on the side where nothing held them back: infrared pictures show it closed to the south, where the dense cloud is, and open to the north. Radio measurements show the ionised gas flowing out northwards from the core, and NASA’s Chandra X-ray telescope shows the space inside filled with hot gas, at one to eight million degrees.

The bubble holds an older generation of stars too, several million years old, which formed before the hidden cluster. One of them is EM Carinae, above the head: two O stars, 23 and 21 times the Sun’s mass, eclipsing each other every 3.4 days. The most massive stars of that older group have probably already exploded. Near the top of the bubble’s rim, invisible in this picture, is a young pulsar, a spinning neutron star turning 15 times a second, which may be what’s left of one of them. The X-ray astronomers who studied the bubble with Chandra found hard X-rays round the pulsar and through the cavity, and read them as the signature of a supernova inside it.

The torch and the head

The two dark shapes inside the bubble are dense clouds of cold dust and gas, seen in silhouette against the glow behind them, their edges worn away by the radiation filling the bubble. The arm is about 7 light-years long, and the head about 3 light-years across. They aren’t just being destroyed. The European Space Agency’s Herschel Space Observatory (named for John Herschel’s father, William) found compact cores of cold dust at the tip of the arm and in the head, and surveys with NASA’s Spitzer telescope found young-star candidates in both, so stars are probably forming inside them, as they did in the cloud the cluster came from.

Two small nebulae

Right of the pedestal, the small round orange glow is VdBH 47a, a hot star of type B, cooler than the O stars, inside a small nebula of its own. Its Gaia distance is the nebula’s, so it’s probably part of the complex. Orange here means hydrogen and sulphur with little oxygen: the gas round a B star isn’t as highly ionised as the gas round an O star. Below it and a little to the left, the small bright patch is ESO 129-5, Herschel’s original NGC 3576 on Corwin’s reading, and its gas moves with the rest of the complex.

The glowing patch at the right-hand edge looks like part of the same nebula, but it isn’t. Its gas is moving the other way, which puts it much further off, on the far side of the Galaxy’s Carina arm, about 27,000 light-years away.

NGC 3603, just out of the frame

Just beyond the left-hand edge, about 22 arcminutes from the pedestal, is NGC 3603, one of the most massive young clusters in the Milky Way: more than 10,000 times the Sun’s mass, packed into a core about three light-years across, and about a million years old. In the sky the two are neighbours, and together they make up RCW 57. But NGC 3603 is about 23,000 light-years away, more than twice as far, and their gas is moving at different speeds. Looking this way we’re looking along the Carina arm of the Galaxy: NGC 3576 is on its near side, and NGC 3603 is much further along it. In my mosaic they’re side by side.

The picture

T59 is a PlaneWave CDK20, a 0.51-metre reflector at its full focal length of 3.4 metres, with a monochrome CCD camera, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. The picture is from 3 hours 15 minutes each through the Hα, OIII, and SII filters: nine hours and three quarters in all.

The colours are the Hubble palette: sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue. Gold is strong hydrogen and sulphur together, the light of gas that’s ionised but not very highly. Knocking a second electron off an oxygen atom takes ultraviolet light that only the hottest stars give out in quantity, so blue marks the most highly ionised gas: inside the bubble, in the cap over its top, and in the bright knots of the pedestal.

There are no colour frames: the stars come from the same three narrowband filters, so their colours aren’t their own. I processed it in PixInsight.
