The Iris Nebula

A flower of dust in a dark cloud

The Iris Nebula, NGC 7023, about 1,100 light-years away in Cepheus: dust lit blue by a young, hot double star, in a dark cloud full of brown dust lit only by the Milky Way, with red threads where the star's light eats into the cloud, and the first place carbon's football-shaped molecules were firmly found in space. Five nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from the Iris left it about 1,100 years ago, around AD 920. In 912 the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle recorded that Edward the Elder took London and Oxford, the first time Oxford appears in writing, a few miles from where its light was caught.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over five nights in August 2024. It covers 1.5 degrees of sky by 1, about three full Moons by two, in Cepheus. It’s in natural colour, from a filter that passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands. The Iris Nebula has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is up and east is to the left. The Iris Nebula is the blue glow right of the middle and below it, with its star, HD 200775, at its heart. Round it, brown dust fills the right half of the frame: the dark cloud LDN 1174, its edge running down just right of the middle, with a clearer, starrier sky to the left. The orange-red star at the upper left is T Cephei. At the nebula’s distance, about 1,100 light-years, the frame is about 28 light-years across and 19 high, and each pixel covers about 300 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 10 times Neptune’s distance.

It’s in Cepheus, high in the north, and from Oxfordshire it never sets: it crosses the meridian 73 degrees up on the northern side of the zenith, in August 2024 between about 1.20 am at the start of the month and 11.25 pm at the end. The sky had only begun to get fully dark again on the night of 21 July, so there were under three hours of true darkness a night at the start of August and about six by the end.

The Iris

The Iris is a reflection nebula: dust, not glowing gas, lit by the starlight it scatters. Its star, HD 200775, is a young, hot pair of stars of about 10 and 9 times the Sun’s mass, circling each other every 3.6 years or so, only about half a million years old. Their light is blue-rich, but not hard enough to make much of the hydrogen round them glow, so the cloud reflects instead. Dust scatters blue light more than red, the same tendency that makes the daytime sky blue (though with grains of dust, not molecules of air), so the nebula is blue.

The dust belongs to LDN 1174, one of the clouds of the Cepheus Flare, which rise about 14 degrees above the plane of the Milky Way. Gaia’s measurements of the young stars in it put it about 1,100 light-years away; you’ll also read 1,300 or 1,400, older figures (1,400 matches the Hipparcos satellite’s measurement of 1997). The bright blue glow is about 13 arcminutes across here, about 4 light-years.

Red threads and carbon footballs

Look closely just above and right of the star, and below and right of it, and there are thin pinkish arcs: the fronts where the star’s ultraviolet light is eating into the cloud, breaking up its molecules. Hubble photographed the northern one in 2009, and ESA’s caption put its red down to an unknown compound, probably based on hydrocarbons. The glow is called extended red emission, and its source is still unidentified; the arcs here sit just where it’s known to be.

The Iris is a famous laboratory for carbon. In 1983 it was one of three reflection nebulae (with NGC 2023 beside the Horsehead and NGC 2068, which is M78) where astronomers found an infrared glow that ordinary dust couldn’t explain, a step towards our picture of dust that includes very small grains and large carbon molecules. And in 2010, in the Iris, the Spitzer Space Telescope made the first firm detection in interstellar space of neutral C60, buckminsterfullerene: cages of 60 carbon atoms, shaped like a football. Something like a few tenths of a per cent of the carbon here may be in them, and one idea is that the star’s light strips big carbon molecules down to sheets that curl up into cages.

History

William Herschel found it on 18 October 1794: a 7th-magnitude star “very much affected with nebulosity”, spreading, he thought, to a degree all round. But that night, he wrote, “everything about the telescope being very damp, I have suspicion of a deception.” It’s in Dreyer’s New General Catalogue as a star in extremely faint, extremely large nebulosity, and later lists counted its young stars as a cluster, so it’s sometimes catalogued as one; it’s also Caldwell 4. Isaac Roberts photographed it in 1894, and Max Wolf in 1907, whose plate showed a circle half a degree across empty of faint stars: the dark cloud.

The dust and the rest

Away from the star, the faint brown and grey dust that fills so much of the frame is lit by no single star but by the combined light of the whole Milky Way. Amateur astronomers call dust like this integrated flux nebula, after Steve Mandel’s photographs of it in 2004. It dims everything behind it, by about a magnitude even in the frame’s clearer parts. At the bottom, LDN 1172 is a darker knot of it, so dense no stars show through.

Left of the Iris and below it, RNO 131 is a second, tiny reflection nebula, a little fan of dust lit by a young star at its waist. Young stars are scattered through the cloud: in 2013 astronomers found the shocks of their jets, Herbig–Haro objects, up and right of the Iris, though they’re far too faint to show here.

Stars worth a look

T Cephei, the orange-red star at the upper left, is a Mira variable, a pulsing red giant that swings between about magnitude 5.2 and 11 over about 389 days; it’s about 600 light-years away, half as far as the Iris. HD 201731, the white star with spikes left of the middle, is about 915 light-years away, in front of the Iris. And near the left edge is TOI-6034, a red dwarf about 383 light-years away with a planet a little smaller in mass than Jupiter that passes in front of it every 2.6 days, found by the TESS satellite.

Far beyond the dust

The farthest things here are hard to find: the dust hides most of what’s behind it, and nothing in the frame has a redshift measured from a proper spectrum. Gaia picked out three faint quasars, the bright cores of distant galaxies, from their colours and its own low-resolution spectra. The farthest, J2058+6840, a faint dot near the top right, is at a redshift of about 1.17, so its light set out about 8.6 billion years ago, about 4 billion years before the Earth formed; J2104+6842, near the top, is at about 1.09, and J2105+6845, beside it, at about 0.62.

A few galaxies show through the dust too, their distances estimated from their colours. The plainest is an edge-on disc just left of the middle, whose light is about 940 million years old; the farthest that shows, a faint reddish smudge near the bottom left, about 2.6 billion.

The colours

The colours are close to natural: the filter passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record. The nebula is blue with scattered starlight, the outer dust brown, the fronts pink, and T Cephei as red as a cool giant gets.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. The picture is 16 hours through an Altair TRI-RGB filter, over five nights in August 2024. It’s 6,040 by 3,955 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
