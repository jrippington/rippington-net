The Moon, Nyx

Theophilus

Craters on the shore of the Sea of Nectar

June 2024, 8 min read

Theophilus, Cyrillus and Catharina, three great craters in a row on the Moon, with the Sun setting on them: the long cliff of the Altai Scarp, the rim of a basin nearly 900 km across, the Sea of Nectar fading into night, and, as dots in the highlands and on the plain, the places where Apollo 16 and Apollo 11 landed. From my garden in Oxfordshire, through an infrared filter.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, without its corrector, at its full 1,176 mm, with a small ZWO ASI678MC planetary camera and an infrared filter, on the night of 25 June 2024. It covers about 1,000 km of the Moon each way, a little south-east of the middle of the face we see. The Moon has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

Lunar north is up, tilted a little to the right, so east, towards the Sea of Crises, is to the right, west to the left, and south down. Theophilus is the big crater above the middle, its central peaks catching the light. Below it to the left, Cyrillus and then Catharina make a chain of three, each about 100 km across. The long black line curving across the lower left is the Altai Scarp, running down to Piccolomini at the bottom. On the right, the smooth floor of Mare Nectaris, the Sea of Nectar, fades into night, with Fracastorius, a crater half drowned by it, below. The crowded highlands fill the left and the Sea of Tranquillity edges in at the top right, and in them are the places where Apollo 16 and Apollo 11 landed. Each pixel covers about 420 metres.

The Sun was setting there. It was 9 degrees above Theophilus, low in the west, on the left, so every crater’s western wall throws its shadow across the floor, and the line between day and night crosses the frame from the top right to the bottom; Theophilus’s floor would go dark about 18 hours later. From Oxfordshire the Moon rose at a quarter past midnight and never climbed above about 21 degrees before dawn, through the twilight of a midsummer night. It was 80 per cent lit, four days after full.

Theophilus

Theophilus is 99 km across and about 4 km deep, one of the deepest of the Moon’s large craters, with broad terraced walls slumped into steps. Its central peaks rise to about 2.7 km, by one measurement, and their shadows here, with the Sun 9 degrees up, agree with that; they’re made of anorthosite, the nearly pure crystalline rock of the Moon’s ancient crust, thrown up from below by the impact. It’s from the Moon’s Eratosthenian period, a billion years or more younger than the basin it sits in.

Cyrillus and Catharina

Theophilus’s rim breaks into Cyrillus beside it, so it’s the younger of the two, and the three craters get older from north to south. Cyrillus, 3.6 km deep, is worn, with low rounded hills a kilometre high on its floor. Catharina, the oldest, is the most battered of all, its rim broken by later impacts and its central peak buried.

The Sea of Nectar

Mare Nectaris is lava that filled a great impact basin, 339 km across. The basin itself is far bigger: the GRAIL spacecraft’s maps of the Moon’s gravity show rings round it up to 885 km across, and the outermost is the Altai Scarp, a cliff 545 km long facing into the basin. With the Sun setting behind it, it shows here as a long black line. The basin is usually dated to about 3.9 billion years ago, though the date is still argued over, and it gives its name to a whole stretch of the Moon’s history, the Nectarian period, which begins with its debris. In 1968 tracking of the Lunar Orbiter spacecraft found a mascon under it, a concentration of mass that tugged on them as they passed.

On its south shore, Fracastorius, 121 km across, has lost its northern wall to the mare’s lava and opens into it as a bay; the Sun is less than 2 degrees above it, and only its south-eastern rim catches the light. Beaumont was flooded through a gap in its eastern rim. Piccolomini, at the bottom, with terraced walls and a central peak, is where the Altai Scarp begins. And Mädler, the small oblong crater right of Theophilus, shows only its sunlit eastern wall. Isidorus, towards the top right, is at the edge of night, its floor already dark. In the highlands on the left, Kant has a central peak, beside the bright massif of Mons Penck, and Abulfeda a flat floor.

Two Apollo landing sites

Apollo 16 landed on 21 April 1972 in the highlands at the upper left, on plains between the craters Dollond and Zöllner, about 330 km from Theophilus. John Young and Charlie Duke made three moonwalks there, drove 27 km and brought back 96 kg of rock, and its samples are the main evidence for the Nectaris basin’s disputed age. Near the top edge, north of the small crater Moltke, is Apollo 11’s Tranquility Base, where the first people landed on 20 July 1969; Surveyor 5, an uncrewed lander, came down 25 km away in 1967, right on the edge of the frame. At this scale each site is a single dot: one pixel is far bigger than anything left there.

Names

The three great craters are named for three people of Alexandria: Theophilus, its patriarch, who died in 412; Cyril, who followed him as patriarch; and Catherine of Alexandria. Giovanni Battista Riccioli named Cyrillus and Catharina on his map of 1651, and probably Theophilus too. Fracastorius is named for Girolamo Fracastoro, a doctor and astronomer, and Piccolomini for Alessandro Piccolomini, an astronomer and archbishop; Johann Mädler, a German astronomer, named Beaumont, and has a crater of his own.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, used without its corrector at its own focal length of 1,176 mm, f/4.7. The camera is a ZWO ASI678MC, a small planetary camera that records video, and in front of it was an infrared filter, to keep the atmosphere a bit more still: the air’s turbulence blurs infrared light less than visible. I captured it in SharpCap, stacked the sharpest frames in AutoStakkert, sharpened them with wavelets, and finished it in Photoshop. It’s in shades of grey. It’s 2,433 by 2,352 pixels.
