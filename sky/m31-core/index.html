The Core of the Andromeda Galaxy

The nearest big galaxy, close up

The middle of the Andromeda Galaxy, about 2.5 million light-years away, the nearest big galaxy to our own: its bright core, the dust lanes on its near side, a ring of red star-forming clouds, its companions M32 and M110, and hundreds of its globular clusters, looking like faint stars. Six nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from Andromeda left it about 2.5 million years ago, around the start of the ice ages, and the light from M110 about 2.7 million. In East Africa then, the first members of our own genus were living alongside other kinds of early human, and people were making some of the earliest stone tools.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over six nights in October 2025. It covers 1.5 degrees of sky by 1, about three full Moons by two, in Andromeda: the middle half of the galaxy. It’s in natural colour, from a filter that passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, with hydrogen’s own red light added from frames through a second filter, so that the clouds of glowing gas stand out. The Andromeda Galaxy has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left, turned a little up, and east is down. The Andromeda Galaxy’s bright core is left of the middle and a little below, and its disc runs from the lower left to the upper right, tilted so steeply that we see it nearly edge-on. Dark lanes of dust cross the bulge above and left of the core, and the red knots strung along two arcs are clouds of glowing hydrogen where stars are forming. Right of the core is the bright little oval of M32, and in the top left corner the larger, fainter oval of M110, the galaxy’s two brightest companions. At its distance the frame is about 65,000 light-years across and 44,000 high, and each pixel covers about 10.6 light-years.

The whole galaxy is about six full Moons long in a long exposure, and at magnitude 3.4 it’s visible to the naked eye from a dark site, a faint elongated smudge. From Oxfordshire it never sets: it passes about 10 degrees from the zenith, and through October 2025 it was above 24 degrees all night, every night, highest between about 10 pm and midnight.

The Andromeda Galaxy

The Andromeda Galaxy, M31, is the nearest big galaxy to ours, a spiral larger than the Milky Way, with something like a trillion stars and about a trillion times the Sun’s mass, most of it dark matter. Hubble’s measurements of its Cepheid variables, stars whose pulsing gives away how bright they really are, put it about 2.5 million light-years away; you’ll also read 2.537 million, an average of measurements from 2003 to 2005. Its bright disc is about 137,000 light-years across, but its stars reach much further, and a halo of thin gas round it extends for about 1.3 million light-years, halfway to us.

Its disc is tilted 77 degrees from face-on. Which side is nearer was argued over in the 1950s; in 1985 its globular clusters settled it, those on the north-west side being reddened by the disc’s dust in front of them. So the near side is the upper left here, where the dust lanes cross in front of the bulge, while the bulge’s light spreads out smoothly below and right of the core, on the far side. The disc is so deep that the light from the far side of its star-forming ring set out about 64,000 years before the light from the near side that arrives with it.

It’s coming towards us at about 300 kilometres a second, the highest speed anyone had measured when Vesto Slipher found it in 1912; allowing for the Sun’s own motion, it’s closing on the Milky Way at about 100. For decades the two were expected to collide in about 4.5 billion years. In 2025 a new study of their motions gave only about an even chance of their merging in the next 10 billion years; a 2026 reanalysis put it at about 90 per cent. It isn’t settled.

Andromeda in history

The earliest surviving description of it is the Persian astronomer al-Sufi’s, in 964, who called it a “little cloud”. Simon Marius gave the first description through a telescope, in December 1612, and Charles Messier catalogued it in 1764. Isaac Roberts’s photograph of 29 December 1888, a four-hour exposure from near Liverpool, showed its spiral form for the first time; he took it for a solar system in the making.

Then, in October 1923, Edwin Hubble found a Cepheid variable in it on a plate from the 100-inch telescope at Mount Wilson: he’d marked it “N”, for a nova, then crossed that out and wrote “VAR!”. From it he worked out that Andromeda was far beyond the Milky Way, a galaxy in its own right, and his result was announced on 1 January 1925. He put it at about 900,000 light-years, nearly three times too close: there are two kinds of Cepheid, which nobody knew then, and Walter Baade’s work with the Palomar 200-inch roughly doubled the distance in the early 1950s.

The core

The smooth cream glow of the core is the light of old stars, and it hides a lot. In 1993 Hubble showed that the nucleus is double, two bright points about 6 light-years apart: one is thought to be the far end of an elongated disc of old stars orbiting the central black hole, where they linger. The black hole is about 140 million times the Sun’s mass, more than 30 times the Milky Way’s. Here the double nucleus is about half a pixel across.

In August 1885 a supernova appeared just beside the nucleus, S Andromedae, and reached about magnitude 6: the first supernova seen beyond the Milky Way. Nothing of it shows here.

The ring of red knots

The red knots follow two arcs, the near and far sides of a ring of star formation about 10,000 parsecs, 32,000 light-years, from the centre, which holds most of the galaxy’s new stars. Each knot is a cloud of hydrogen lit by young, hot stars; about 3,700 are catalogued in the whole galaxy, and about 2,700 of them fall in this frame. Andromeda makes new stars slowly, a few tenths of the Sun’s mass a year, a fraction of the Milky Way’s rate. One idea for the ring’s shape, offset and split near M32, is that M32 plunged through Andromeda’s disc about 210 million years ago and set it rippling.

The ring’s upper arc ends at the upper right in NGC 206, the brightest star cloud in the galaxy, about 4,000 light-years across: a knot of blue stars wrapped in red clouds, its youngest stars less than 10 million years old. William Herschel found it in 1786. Further out, beyond it, VdB0 is a young cluster of about 25 million years, already rich in red supergiants.

M32

M32, the compact oval just right of the core, is a small elliptical galaxy, about 6,000 to 8,000 light-years across, dense and old, with a black hole of about 2.5 million times the Sun’s mass at its centre. It was the first elliptical galaxy ever found, by Guillaume Le Gentil in 1749. It may be all that’s left of a much bigger galaxy that Andromeda swallowed about 2 billion years ago, its outer stars stripped away; that’s an idea from simulations, not a measurement. It’s probably in front of Andromeda’s disc, since it doesn’t look reddened by its dust, but that isn’t settled either.

M110

M110, the large soft oval in the top left corner, is a dwarf elliptical galaxy about 2.7 million light-years away, so about 130,000 light-years behind Andromeda, though it looks so close to it. Unusually for its kind it has young blue stars and dark dust at its centre, which show here. Charles Messier saw it in 1773 but never put it in his catalogue; Caroline Herschel found it independently in 1783, and it was only given the number M110 in 1966.

Globular clusters

Many of the faint “stars” in the picture are Andromeda’s globular clusters, balls of hundreds of thousands of old stars each: about 435 confirmed clusters fall in this frame. A few stand out. B023-G078, above the core, is perhaps the galaxy’s most massive, about 6 million times the Sun’s mass, with what may be a black hole at its centre; it may be the stripped core of a small galaxy. B037, nearer the core, looks orange-red, the most reddened cluster known in Andromeda, seen through a lane of its dust. B225-G280, at the lower left, is the brightest cluster in the frame. And B158-G213, below the core, holds a neutron star in a tight orbit whose X-rays dip every 2.78 hours.

Behind and in front

Two small things aren’t Andromeda’s at all. Andromeda IV, the faint reddish smudge at the right, was long taken for one of its companions, but it’s a small galaxy about 23 million light-years away, nine times further, receding from us while Andromeda approaches. And the brightest star in the picture, HD 3914, at the right with spikes, is in our own Galaxy, about 162 light-years away.

The colours

The colours are close to natural: the filter passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record. The core is cream, the light of old stars; the outer arms are bluer, with young ones; the dust is brown against the starlight behind it. The red of the knots is hydrogen’s light, taken from 10 hours through the dual-band filter. Continuum subtraction takes away the ordinary starlight that also comes through that filter, leaving hydrogen’s light alone, which I added to the colour picture.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. The picture is 8 hours 30 minutes through an Altair TRI-RGB filter and 10 hours through an Antlia dual-band filter that passes hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III, over six nights in October 2025. It’s 6,146 by 4,125 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, Affinity Photo, and Photoshop.
