How astro cameras work

From the first CCDs to today's cooled CMOS

Every picture on Nyx began as a grid of numbers. Each pixel of the camera counted the electrons that light knocked loose in a sliver of silicon during the exposure, and almost everything about an astro camera comes down to how well it does that: how much of the light it catches, how much noise it adds, and how a sensor that can't see colour still makes colour pictures.

This is a grounding in how that works, for anyone starting out: how a sensor turns light into numbers, the two kinds of sensor (CCD and CMOS) and how one replaced the other, where the noise in a picture comes from, how colour is made, and how to match a camera to a telescope. It’s long. The first three sections are the foundation, and after them each section stands fairly well on its own, so you can skip to the one you need. On Optica’s map, the camera is where the telescopes’ light comes to a focus and becomes data, and every stage along the path after it works on what the camera recorded.

Light into electrons

A useful way to picture a sensor is as a field of buckets left out in the rain. Each bucket is a pixel and each raindrop a photon, a particle of light, and when the exposure ends you go round and measure how much water each bucket caught. A bucket under a bright star fills quickly; one under empty sky catches only a little. The picture is the pattern of those measurements.

The physics behind the buckets is the photoelectric effect. Each pixel is a small patch of silicon, and when a photon strikes it with enough energy, it frees an electron from the silicon’s crystal. An electric field built into the pixel draws the freed electrons into a potential well, the bucket, where they’re held until the camera reads them. Twice as much light frees twice as many electrons, so the count is a direct measure of how much light arrived. Photographic film never behaved that simply, which is one reason electronic sensors replaced it.

Silicon has limits, set by its band gap: the energy a photon has to deliver to free an electron, 1.12 electronvolts. Red photons carry less energy than blue ones, and beyond a wavelength of about 1,100 nanometres a photon doesn’t carry enough. Silicon is blind there, which is why infrared telescopes use detectors made of other materials: the James Webb Space Telescope’s near-infrared cameras use mercury cadmium telluride.

Colour also decides how deep a photon goes before it’s absorbed. Blue light at 400 nm is stopped within about 0.1 micrometres of the surface; green at 500 nm goes about a micrometre, red at 700 nm about five, and near-infrared at 800 nm about twelve. The part of a pixel that collects electrons is only a few micrometres to a few tens of micrometres deep, so the deepest-going light is often absorbed where its electron can’t be collected, or passes straight through. That’s why sensors become less sensitive towards the deep red and the infrared, and why the CCDs in the Rubin Observatory’s camera are made unusually thick, 100 micrometres, to catch more of the red.

The share of photons that end up as counted electrons is a sensor’s quantum efficiency, or QE, and it varies with wavelength. It’s the number that changed most over the history of astro imaging. A photographic plate recorded about 1 photon in 100, or 4 or 5 in 100 after special treatment. Kodak’s KAF-16803 CCD, long a mainstay of amateur and remote observatories and still at work in several of the remote telescopes I use, reaches about 60 per cent at its peak. The best CMOS sensors today reach around 90 per cent, by their camera makers’ estimates (Sony, which makes them, publishes only relative figures).

Two inventions account for most of that gain. In a front-illuminated sensor, light has to get past the electrodes and wiring on the sensor’s surface before it reaches the silicon, and they block or absorb some of it, the blue especially. A back-illuminated sensor is thinned and turned over, so that the light comes in from the back, with the wiring behind the silicon rather than in front of it. And a microlens over each pixel gathers light that would have fallen on its insensitive parts and focuses it on to the part that collects. On early interline CCDs, which gave much of each pixel over to circuitry, microlenses raised the share of the pixel that gathered light from about a quarter to over three quarters.

So the buckets help, but they mislead in places. Not every raindrop is caught, since quantum efficiency is never 100 per cent, and some drops fall straight through. Measuring the water adds an error of its own, which the next section comes to. And a full bucket doesn’t spill evenly on to its neighbours, as the section on overflowing pixels shows.

Electrons into numbers

When the exposure ends, the camera turns each pixel’s electrons into a number, in two steps. An amplifier converts the charge into a voltage, and an analogue-to-digital converter, or ADC, measures the voltage and gives a whole number. Those numbers are often called ADU, for analogue-to-digital units, and a raw frame is nothing more than a grid of them.

How many different numbers the converter can give is its bit depth. A 12-bit converter counts from 0 to 4,095, a 14-bit one to 16,383, and a 16-bit one to 65,535. Most cooled astro cameras today are 16-bit, while DSLRs and mirrorless cameras write 12-bit or 14-bit raw files.

Gain sets how many electrons each step of the converter is worth. Take a typical modern sensor, Sony’s IMX571 in a ZWO camera. At its lowest gain setting, each step is worth about 0.78 electrons, and the whole of a pixel’s capacity, its full well of about 50,000 electrons, fits on the converter’s scale. Turn the gain up and each step is worth fewer electrons, so the same charge reads as a bigger number and small differences are recorded more finely. The cost is that the scale now runs out before the well does. Gain doesn’t make the sensor any more sensitive: the same light frees the same electrons whatever the setting. It changes how they’re counted.

Every reading adds a little random error, the read noise, quoted in electrons. It comes from the amplifier and the electronics round it, and it’s added once per frame, however long the exposure. The KAF-16803 has read noise of about 9 electrons; today’s CMOS sensors manage 1 to 3. Many of Sony’s sensors also have a second, more sensitive way of reading their pixels, a “high conversion gain” mode that switches on at a particular gain setting (gain 100 on ZWO’s IMX571 cameras). From that point the read noise drops from about 3.3 electrons to about 1.5, which is why camera makers’ charts show a sudden step there, and why it’s a popular setting.

The ratio of the full well to the read noise is the sensor’s dynamic range: the span from the faintest signal it can tell apart from its own noise to the brightest it can hold. For the IMX571 at its lowest gain that’s 50,000 to 3.3, about 15,000 to 1, or nearly 14 stops (doublings). Perhaps surprisingly, the KAF-16803’s much bigger pixels, 9 micrometres across, with a full well of 100,000 electrons and 9 electrons of read noise, come out much the same, at about 13.4 stops: big pixels hold more, while small modern ones read more quietly. In its high conversion gain mode, the IMX571 keeps nearly all of its dynamic range, with about 20,000 electrons over 1.5.

One more number goes into every reading. The camera adds a fixed offset, so that an empty pixel doesn’t read as zero. Read noise scatters readings both up and down, and the converter can’t record a number below zero, so without the offset the lower half of the scatter would be cut off and the average dragged upwards. The offset is taken back out in calibration.

CCD and CMOS: two ways to read a sensor

There are two ways to get the charge out of millions of pixels, and they give the two kinds of sensor their names and most of their character.

A CCD, a charge-coupled device, moves the charge itself. Each pixel sits under a set of electrodes, and by switching their voltages in sequence the camera shifts every row’s charge down one row at a time, the way a bucket brigade passes water along a line. The bottom row drops into a separate serial register, which shifts its charges sideways, a pixel at a time, into a single amplifier at its end. Then the next row comes down, and so on until the whole sensor has been read.

The bucket brigade is a good comparison, with two corrections. Only the charge moves: the electrodes stay where they are, and the wells move along under them as the voltages change. And every hand-over loses a tiny amount. A good CCD passes on better than 99.999 per cent of the charge at every step, which matters on a chip where the last pixel’s charge has thousands of steps to make.

One amplifier serving every pixel makes a CCD very even, since every pixel is measured in the same way, but slow. Reading Kodak’s 16.8-megapixel KAF-16803 at a million pixels a second takes about 17 seconds, and reading it faster makes the noise worse. A CCD also goes on collecting light while its charge is being shifted, so a full-frame CCD needs a mechanical shutter to cover it during readout, or bright stars would smear down their columns. Interline CCDs, such as the KAI-11002 in iTelescope T11, avoid the shutter by giving each column a masked twin to shift into, at the cost of light-sensitive area that microlenses then win back.

Moving charge has one real advantage. A CCD can add the charge of neighbouring pixels together before it reaches the amplifier, which is called binning. Binned 2 × 2, four pixels’ charge is read as one, so you get four times the signal with a single helping of read noise, at half the resolution.

A CMOS sensor (for complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor, the process it’s made with) does the opposite: every pixel has its own amplifier, built from a few transistors beside its light-sensitive area. Instead of moving charge across the chip, the camera switches one row of pixels at a time on to wires that run down each column, and every column has its own converter. A whole row is read at once, and the rows follow one another in quick succession. With thousands of converters working side by side, each can work slowly and quietly, so CMOS sensors are fast and low in read noise at the same time: a camera with the 26-megapixel IMX571 reads about three and a half full frames a second, and small regions of it far faster.

CMOS paid for that in other ways, at least at first. Thousands of slightly different amplifiers and converters leave a faint fixed pattern of their own, which calibration removes. Some CMOS sensors glow faintly at an edge or a corner during long exposures, from the heat and light of their own readout circuits, which is called amp glow: Sony’s IMX294 and IMX183 show it, and so does the Panasonic sensor in ZWO’s ASI1600. Dark frames subtract it, and the current generation, including the IMX571 and the full-frame IMX455, is sold as free of it. Because the rows are read one after another, the readout “rolls” down the sensor, each row a tiny fraction of a second behind the last, which makes no difference to exposures of seconds or minutes. And binning on a CMOS sensor can only be done after reading, by adding up the numbers, so a 2 × 2 bin carries four helpings of read noise, twice the noise of one. With CMOS it’s better done later, in processing.

CMOS won mostly because of everything else that uses it. Phones and consumer cameras made CMOS sensors an enormous business, and they improved year after year. By the late 2010s Sony’s back-illuminated CMOS sensors were cheaper, quieter, and faster than CCDs, and nearly as sensitive, and the makers of CCDs announced their end. The next section but one has the dates.

When a pixel overflows

A pixel’s well holds only so many electrons. The bright stars in a deep-sky frame fill their pixels long before the faint nebula round them has registered, and what happens to the overflow depends on the sensor.

In a CCD, the barriers between neighbouring columns are built into the silicon, while the barriers between pixels in the same column are made by the electrode voltages, and are weaker. When a pixel is full, its extra charge spills over the weaker barriers into the pixels above and below, and fills them in turn. The result is blooming: a bright, straight streak running up and down through the star, sometimes the full height of the frame.

An anti-blooming gate prevents it, with a drain beside each column that carries away the charge from any pixel that overfills. The KAF-16803’s drains cope with stars more than a hundred times brighter than it takes to fill a pixel. Anti-blooming takes up some of each pixel’s light-sensitive area, and so costs some sensitivity, and it makes a pixel’s response bend as it nears full, which spoils measurements of brightness. That’s why many research cameras do without it and put up with the streaks: iTelescope uses CCDs without anti-blooming on some of its telescopes for their higher quantum efficiency and deeper wells. The ST-7 above is an amateur camera without it; SBIG sold both kinds.

Blooming is easy to confuse with diffraction spikes, the cross of light that the vanes holding a reflector’s secondary mirror draw on bright stars. Spikes have four or more arms, set by the telescope. A bloom runs only along the sensor’s columns, in one straight line, and grows longer with the exposure.

Modern CMOS sensors rarely bloom. Each pixel can drain its extra charge away through its own reset circuitry, so an overexposed star comes out as a flat white disc rather than a streak. Overexposure still does harm, since a saturated pixel has lost the information about how bright it was, but the damage stays where it happened.

From glass plates to CMOS

For more than a century, astronomers recorded the sky on glass. John William Draper took the first known photograph of the Moon in 1840, and photographic plates did most of astronomy’s recording from the 1880s well into the 1980s: Harvard alone holds some 500,000 of them, and the second Palomar sky survey was still exposing plates in 2000. Plates were large and permanent, but they wasted almost all of the light. A plate recorded about 1 photon in 100, and astronomers baked their plates in hydrogen or nitrogen gas, or chilled them with dry ice, to raise that to 4 or 5. Worse, an emulsion becomes less sensitive the longer it’s exposed to faint light, a failure of reciprocity that the astronomer William Keel calls “the greatest special problem in astronomical photography”.

The charge-coupled device was invented at Bell Labs in October 1969 by Willard Boyle and George Smith, in a discussion that took less than an hour. They meant it as a kind of computer memory and called it a “charge bubble device” at first; within two years their colleagues had made one that took pictures. In 1976 a team from NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory and the University of Arizona put a cooled CCD of 400 × 400 pixels on a 1.5-metre telescope near Tucson and photographed the planets, and its pictures of Uranus, taken in the near-infrared, showed markings on the planet’s disk for the first time. Professional astronomy changed over during the 1980s, and in 1990 the Hubble Space Telescope went up with eight CCDs of 800 × 800 pixels in its main camera. In 2009 Boyle and Smith shared half of the Nobel Prize in Physics for the invention; the other half went to Charles Kao, for optical fibres.

Amateurs followed. SBIG’s ST-4 of 1989 was the first autoguider amateurs could buy, a camera that watches a star and keeps the telescope tracking it, and it could take pictures too. SBIG’s ST-7 and ST-8 of 1994 used Kodak’s KAF-0400 and KAF-1600 sensors. Kodak’s KAF and KAI families went on to fill a generation of cameras from SBIG, FLI, Apogee, and others, alongside Sony’s CCDs, and many remote telescopes still use them. Among the ones I use, iTelescope T8, T9, T10, T14 and T59 have the KAF-16803, T72 the KAF-16200, T31 the KAF-09000 and T11 the interline KAI-11002.

Meanwhile, cameras made for daylight were becoming good enough to point at the sky. Canon’s EOS 20Da of 2005 was a DSLR made for astrophotography, letting through about two and a half times as much hydrogen-alpha light as its everyday sibling. CMOS reached dedicated astro cameras in the 2010s, first in small planetary and guide cameras, then in cooled cameras for deep-sky work: ZWO’s ASI1600MM-Cool of 2016, built round a Panasonic sensor, was the company’s first. Cameras with Sony’s IMX294 followed in 2017, and in 2019 cameras with its full-frame IMX455 were announced, followed by the APS-C IMX571. Those two sensors are now everywhere: in remote telescopes such as T17, T32, T70 and T71, and in the colour camera on my own 250 mm and 150 mm Newtonians.

Then the CCDs stopped. Sony announced the end of its CCD production in 2015 and stopped making the wafers in 2017. ON Semiconductor, which had bought Kodak’s sensor business by way of Truesense Imaging, announced the end of all its CCDs in October 2019. The last orders were taken in March 2020, and the Rochester factory that had made Kodak’s sensors closed that June.

CCDs haven’t vanished from professional astronomy, though. The largest digital camera ever built for astronomy, the Rubin Observatory’s, which took its first look at the sky in 2025, uses 189 CCDs for its 3.2 billion pixels, made thick for their sensitivity to red light, and the Gaia spacecraft has mapped the Milky Way with 106. CMOS is spreading there too, in survey cameras built from dozens of CMOS sensors, and in Hamamatsu’s qCMOS cameras, whose read noise is so low, about 0.3 electrons, that they can count individual electrons.

Noise, and why astro cameras are cooled

What makes a faint nebula hard to photograph is how weak its light is compared with the random scatter in everything else a pixel records. That scatter is noise, and much of what an astro camera is designed to do, and much of what processing does afterwards, is about keeping the signal ahead of it.

Some of the noise is in the light itself. Photons arrive at random, like raindrops, so a pixel that catches 100 on average catches 90 one time and 110 the next. The spread, called shot noise, is the square root of the count: about 10 for 100 electrons, and 100 for 10,000. It grows more slowly than the signal, which is why more light always helps, but no camera can remove it.

The sky adds light of its own, from light pollution, the faint glow of the upper atmosphere, and the Moon, and with it its own shot noise. Processing can subtract the sky’s average brightness, but not its noise, which stays in the picture. Heat adds more: the silicon’s own warmth frees electrons even in total darkness, at a steady rate called the dark current, and they bring their noise with them. Then the read noise is added once, when the frame is read.

Last, there are patterns: hot pixels, whose dark current is far above the rest; amp glow; slight differences in sensitivity from one pixel to the next; and the shadows of dust and the darkening towards the corners that come from the telescope. These are the same in every frame, so they can be measured and taken out, which is what calibration does, with dark frames (taken with the telescope covered), flat frames (of an evenly lit field), and bias frames (the shortest exposures the camera can make).

Random noises add as the square root of the sum of their squares, and since shot noise is itself a square root, for one pixel the signal-to-noise ratio comes to:

SNR=SS+B+D+R2\text{SNR} = \frac{S}{\sqrt{S + B + D + R^{2}}}SNR=S+B+D+R2​S​

where S is the electrons from the target, B from the sky, D from the dark current, and R is the read noise. As a worked example, with illustrative numbers, take a five-minute frame of a faint nebula under a suburban sky, with a modern cooled CMOS camera. The nebula gives a pixel 200 electrons, the sky 1,500, the dark current 0.04, and the read noise is 1.5 electrons. The noise comes to the square root of about 1,702, which is 41 electrons, so the signal-to-noise ratio is about 5: enough to see the nebula, but grainy.

Two things stand out. It’s the sky that sets the noise here; the dark current and the read noise barely register. And the way forward is more light. Stacking frames, which means averaging them, keeps the signal the same while the random noise in the average shrinks by the square root of the number of frames. Four frames halve the noise, and a hundred cut it to a tenth. Thirty-six frames cut it to a sixth, here about 7 electrons, and raise the signal-to-noise ratio to about 29.

Dark current falls steeply with temperature, roughly halving for every 5 to 7 °C of cooling, and for CCDs that mattered a great deal. The KAF-16803 generates about 3 electrons a second in each pixel at room temperature; cooled to −20 °C, that falls to about 0.02, still around 6 electrons in a five-minute frame. A modern CMOS sensor is far better. ZWO quotes about 0.0001 electrons a second for the IMX571 at −20 °C (another maker quotes 0.0005, so they clearly measure differently), well under one electron in a five-minute frame.

So for a modern camera, cooling is less about reducing the dark current than about holding it steady. A thermoelectric cooler, a Peltier device that pumps heat from the sensor to a heat sink and a fan, can keep the sensor at a set temperature, typically up to 30 or 35 °C below the air round it, night after night. The dark current, hot pixels, and amp glow are then the same every time, so a set of dark frames taken once can be used for months. An uncooled camera’s sensor follows the night’s temperature, and warms as it works, so its dark frames have to be taken the same night.

A sensor cooled below freezing would frost over in damp air, so cooled cameras seal it in a chamber with a desiccant to keep it dry (the desiccant needs drying out again every six months to a year), heat the window in front of it, and in some cases fill the chamber with argon.

How long to expose

On any night, the total exposure matters most: more time means more photons and more signal against the noise. The question is how to divide it up. Is one 10-minute frame better than ten 1-minute ones?

The only difference between them is the read noise, which is added once per frame: ten frames carry ten helpings of it, one frame only one. But read noise matters only if it’s a noticeable part of the total, and in the example above it isn’t, because the sky’s shot noise swamps it. So the useful rule is to make each frame long enough for the sky to bury the read noise, and no longer.

Robin Glover, who wrote the SharpCap capture software, put a number on that. If each frame collects about ten times the square of the read noise in sky electrons, the read noise adds only about 5 per cent to the total noise, and longer frames gain almost nothing. Because the rule goes with the square of the read noise, it explains why low-noise CMOS cameras favour short frames, and why CCDs needed long ones. For example:

Read noise 1.5 e⁻, sky 1 e⁻ a second in each pixelAbout 23 seconds
Read noise 1.5 e⁻, a darker sky or a narrowband filter: 0.1 e⁻ a secondAbout 225 seconds
Read noise 9 e⁻ (an older CCD), sky 1 e⁻ a secondAbout 810 seconds

Shorter frames have other advantages. A gust of wind, a satellite, an aircraft, or a guiding error spoils one short frame rather than one long one, and with more frames the stacking software has more to compare, so it can reject the odd bad value. Bright stars saturate less. The costs are more files to store and process, and below the rule’s length, more read noise.

Gain is the other setting to choose. A sensible starting point is where the camera’s read noise drops, at the start of its high conversion gain mode if it has one. For its IMX571 cameras, ZWO recommends either gain 0, for the most dynamic range, or gain 100, where that mode begins.

One more habit helps: move the telescope a few pixels between frames, which is called dithering. Hot pixels and other fixed patterns then fall on different parts of the target in each frame, and stacking rejects them rather than building them up.

Colour from a sensor that can’t see it

Once a photon has freed its electron, nothing records what colour it was: an electron freed by red light is the same as one freed by blue. A sensor counts light, and the colour has to come from filters in front of it.

The most direct way is a mono camera, with no colour filters of its own, and a filter wheel in front of it. You take a set of frames through a red filter, a set through green, and a set through blue, and combine them into a colour picture. Many imagers add a fourth set through a clear luminance filter, L, which passes all three colours at once, about three times as much light as any one of them, and so records the detail and the faint parts of the target more quickly. That works because our eyes take their sense of detail mostly from brightness rather than colour, so the colour frames can be fewer, or softer, without the picture looking it. The page on iTelescope T70 describes a typical set of filters on a mono camera.

For glowing nebulae there’s a better way still. Their light comes from gas made to shine by nearby stars, and each kind of gas glows at only a few exact wavelengths, its emission lines. The four that matter most are:

Hydrogen-alpha (Hα)656.3 nm, deep red
Oxygen III (OIII)500.7 nm (with a weaker line at 495.9), blue-green
Sulphur II (SII)671.6 and 673.1 nm, a deeper red
Hydrogen-beta (Hβ)486.1 nm, blue-green; a quarter to a third as strong as Hα

A narrowband filter passes a band only a few nanometres wide round one line, typically 3 to 7 nm. The nebula’s light gets through almost untouched, while the sky’s background, spread across the whole spectrum, is cut down in proportion to how narrow the band is. That’s why narrowband imaging works under light pollution, and even in moonlight. Moonlight is strongest near OIII, so that’s the filter where narrowness matters most.

Narrowband pictures have to be given their colours, since three bands of red and blue-green don’t make a natural-looking picture on their own. The best-known scheme is the Hubble palette, in which SII is shown as red, Hα as green, and OIII as blue, in order of wavelength. It comes from Jeff Hester and Paul Scowen’s 1995 Hubble picture of the Pillars of Creation in the Eagle Nebula. The colours are a map: the nebula wouldn’t look like that to the eye.

One-shot colour and debayering

A one-shot colour camera puts the filters on the sensor itself, as a mosaic of tiny red, green, and blue filters, one over each pixel, called a colour filter array. Nearly all of them use the pattern Bryce Bayer patented at Kodak in 1976, which repeats a square of four pixels: one red, two green, and one blue. Green gets twice the share because it sits in the middle of the spectrum, where, as Bayer’s patent explains, the eye is most sensitive to fine detail. Phones, DSLRs, and cooled astro cameras such as the Altair Hypercam 26C on my own telescopes all work this way.

So a raw frame from a colour camera is a mosaic. Each pixel has measured only one colour, and zoomed in, an unprocessed raw frame shows a fine checkerboard. To make a colour picture, software estimates the two missing colours at every pixel from its neighbours, which is called debayering, or demosaicing. The simplest way averages the nearest pixels of each colour. That works well on smooth areas, but leaves zigzags and false colour along sharp edges, so better methods follow the edges rather than averaging across them. Some suit stars particularly well: Siril, the free processing software, uses one called RCD by default for that reason.

That has consequences for processing. Calibration has to be done before debayering, on the raw mosaic, because darks and flats correct each pixel on its own, and have to be applied to the values it actually measured. There are also ways round the estimating. Superpixel debayering turns each square of four into one full-colour pixel, measured rather than estimated, at half the width and height. And with enough frames, each shifted a little by dithering, every part of the sky eventually falls on a red, a green, and a blue pixel, so the stack can be built from measurements alone. That’s Bayer drizzle, or CFA drizzle, and its main benefit is cleaner noise.

What colour costs shows most in narrowband. In ordinary light each colour reaches its share of the pixels, a quarter for red and for blue, and half for green, and the result is a good colour picture in one go. But the light of hydrogen-alpha falls almost entirely on the red pixels, a quarter of them, with a little leaking into the green. A colour camera therefore collects roughly a third to a quarter as much Hα as a mono camera with the same sensor behind an Hα filter, and at lower resolution. OIII does better, since its blue-green light falls on the green and blue pixels, three in every four.

The same sensor often comes in both versions, which makes the comparison direct. ZWO sells the IMX571 as the ASI2600MM, mono, with a peak quantum efficiency of about 91 per cent, and as the ASI2600MC, colour, at about 80.

Colour cameras can do a kind of narrowband imaging too, with dual-band filters that pass Hα and OIII together in two narrow bands, such as Optolong’s L-eXtreme (7 nm) and Antlia’s ALP-T (5 nm). The Hα lands on the red pixels and the OIII on the green and blue, so one set of frames records both gases, and processing software can separate them again afterwards. The page on my 250 mm Newtonian describes a pair of such filters on a colour camera.

So which should you choose? Mono is the more sensitive and the sharper, especially in narrowband, and it’s what most remote telescopes use: of the iTelescope telescopes I use, only T20 has a colour camera. But it needs a filter wheel and a set of filters, and a picture isn’t finished until every filter has had its share of clear sky, which can take several nights. A colour camera records everything at once, needs no wheel, costs less, and is simpler to process. I chose one-shot colour because it lets me finish pictures more quickly with the variable weather in the UK. Under reliably clear skies, or for narrowband work above all, mono repays the extra effort.

Ordinary cameras under the stars

Many people start with the camera they already have, and a DSLR or mirrorless camera can do a lot: a large sensor, low read noise, raw files, and interchangeable lenses. My own Nikon D850, unmodified, is the camera on the portable rig I take on trips. Underneath, the silicon is much the same as in an astro camera. The differences are in what’s built round it for daylight.

The biggest is the filter in front of the sensor. To make daylight colours look right, it blocks ultraviolet and infrared light, and with them much of the deep red, including hydrogen-alpha at 656 nm, the light in which most emission nebulae shine brightest. How much of that gets through varies a great deal from camera to camera: measurements of older models range from about a quarter down to almost none. Camera makers have made astro versions with a different filter, such as Nikon’s D810A (2015) and Canon’s EOS 20Da (2005), 60Da (2012), and EOS Ra (2019), which let through two and a half to four times as much Hα. Many astrophotographers have their own cameras modified instead, with the filter replaced or removed, which lets through 90 per cent or more of the Hα. The catch is that a modified camera’s daylight pictures turn red unless a correcting filter goes back on; Nikon says the D810A isn’t recommended for general photography.

Next, a DSLR isn’t cooled. Its sensor sits at the temperature of the night, and warms as the camera works through a long sequence, so its dark current changes through the night and from one season to the next. Dark frames have to be taken in the same session, at the same temperature, to match. Most cameras offer long-exposure noise reduction, which takes a dark frame straight after every exposure and subtracts it. It works, but it halves the time spent collecting light, so many astrophotographers turn it off and take their dark frames separately.

And raw isn’t always quite raw. Some cameras clean up their raw files before saving them, with filters meant to remove hot pixels that can remove small stars as well. On some of Sony’s cameras this became known as the “star eater”, and some Nikons have filtered raw files too, though less harshly. It’s worth finding out how a particular model behaves before relying on it.

Raw files are also 12-bit or 14-bit rather than 16, though with the low read noise of modern sensors that rarely limits a picture. For wide fields, the Milky Way, eclipses, and travel, a DSLR or mirrorless camera on a small tracking mount does very well.

Matching the camera to the telescope

How much sky each pixel sees depends on the size of the pixel and the focal length of the telescope. It’s called the pixel scale, in arcseconds a pixel (an arcsecond is 1/3,600 of a degree):

pixel scale=206.265×pixel sizefocal length\text{pixel scale} = 206.265 \times \frac{\text{pixel size}}{\text{focal length}}pixel scale=206.265×focal lengthpixel size​

with the pixel size in micrometres and the focal length in millimetres. The IMX571’s 3.76-micrometre pixels behind my 250 mm Newtonian, at a focal length of 882 mm, see 0.88 arcseconds each; the same pixels behind the 135 mm lens of iTelescope T70 see 5.8.

Which is better depends on the sky. The air above a telescope blurs every star into a small disc, and the width of that disc, measured across its brightness at half the peak (its FWHM), is called the seeing. At the best professional sites it can be under an arcsecond; amateurs more often see 2 to 4 arcseconds, and the size of a star in a picture also includes any error in focus or guiding. The usual advice is to spread a star’s FWHM over about two or three pixels, which in seeing of 2.5 arcseconds means about 1 arcsecond a pixel. Sources disagree on the exact figure, and some suggest coarser.

With pixels much coarser than that, the picture is undersampled: stars fall into a pixel or two and come out square, and fine detail is lost. With pixels much finer, it’s oversampled, which records no more detail, since the seeing has already blurred it, but gives each pixel less of the light, and so more noise. Oversampled data can be binned, which on a CMOS camera is best done in processing; T17 and T32 bin their full-frame CMOS sensors 2 × 2 to suit their long focal lengths. Undersampled data can be drizzled: the frames are dithered, so the stars fall on different parts of the pixels each time, and the stack is rebuilt on a finer grid. Drizzle was developed for the Hubble Deep Field. Wide-field instruments such as T70 are coarse by design, giving up detail for sky.

The sensor’s size decides the field of view, the patch of sky the whole frame takes in, in degrees:

field of view≈57.3×sensor widthfocal length\text{field of view} \approx 57.3 \times \frac{\text{sensor width}}{\text{focal length}}field of view≈57.3×focal lengthsensor width​

with both in millimetres. Astro sensors come in much the same sizes as other cameras’ sensors: the so-called 1-inch type (Sony’s IMX533 is a square 11.3 mm across), Four Thirds (the IMX294, 19.1 × 13.0 mm), APS-C (the IMX571, 23.5 × 15.7 mm), full frame (the IMX455, 36 × 24 mm), and medium format (the IMX461, 44 × 33 mm). The same APS-C sensor takes in 1.5° × 1.0° at 882 mm and 2.8° × 1.85° at 486 mm, the focal lengths of my 250 mm and 150 mm Newtonians. A bigger sensor takes in more sky but asks more of the telescope, which has to give sharp, evenly lit stars right out to the corners.

The other cameras, and what a camera hands on

The camera taking the picture isn’t the only one on a telescope. A guide camera, small, sensitive, and usually mono, watches a single star, through a small guide scope or through the main telescope with an off-axis guider, and tells the mount how to correct its tracking every few seconds. SBIG’s ST-4 of 1989 was the first amateurs could buy; a ZWO ASI120MM guides both of my Newtonians.

Planets need something different. They’re small and bright, and the atmosphere’s blurring changes many times a second, so planetary cameras have small pixels and fast readout, and record video, often of just a small region of the sensor, at tens or hundreds of frames a second. Software then keeps the sharpest frames, taken in moments of steady air, and stacks them, which is called lucky imaging. Sony’s small-pixel sensors made for security cameras, such as the IMX585 and IMX678, are popular for it.

Meteor and all-sky cameras use small, inexpensive CMOS sensors behind wide lenses, running all night: the Global Meteor Network’s stations, for instance, record 25 frames a second through a 4 mm lens. At the far end, research cameras count light a photon at a time: electron-multiplying CCDs, which amplify the charge before it’s read, and the qCMOS cameras mentioned earlier.

Whatever the camera, what it hands on is a file. Astronomy cameras write FITS files (for Flexible Image Transport System), a format published in 1981 and endorsed by the International Astronomical Union in 1982. A FITS file starts with a header of plain-text lines recording the exposure time, the sensor’s temperature, the gain, the offset, the binning, the filter, and much else, followed by the image itself, usually as 16-bit whole numbers: the counts. DSLRs and mirrorless cameras write their own raw formats instead.

Those counts still carry the camera with them: its offset, its dark current and hot pixels, the small differences between its pixels, and the shadows of dust and the darkening towards the corners that come from the telescope. The next stop on Optica’s path, calibration, takes them back out.
