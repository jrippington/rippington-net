Vela Supernova Remnant

A star's blast wave, 11,000 years on

The debris of a star that exploded about 11,000 years ago, as seen from here, about 940 light-years away: one of the nearest supernova remnants known. Its blast wave has spread across 8 degrees of sky, and where it runs into gas, the gas glows, oxygen blue and hydrogen red. Behind it are two more remnants, Vela Junior and Puppis A.

The light in this picture left Vela about 940 years ago, around the 1080s: in England, the time of the Domesday survey of 1086. People were already watching other supernovae then: one in 1006 in Lupus, and the one that made the Crab Nebula in 1054. The supernova’s own light, if the pulsar’s age is right, reached the Earth about 11,000 years ago, just after the end of the last ice age, about when people in what’s now Turkey were building the stone enclosures of Göbekli Tepe. Behind the remnant, NGC 2660’s light set out about 9,000 years ago.

This picture is four frames from iTelescope’s T8, a wide-field refractor at Siding Spring in Australia, joined into one, taken in December 2022. It covers about 7 degrees of sky by 8.5, fourteen full Moons across and sixteen high. It’s made from two narrowband filters, which pass only the light of hydrogen and of oxygen, 17 hours 20 minutes through the first and 18 hours 40 minutes through the second, and the stars’ colours were made from the same two. The remnant has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is up and east is to the left. Nearly everything with fine structure, the blue filaments and the stringy red ones, is the Vela remnant, and it almost fills the frame. At its distance it’s about 130 light-years across, and each pixel covers about a thousand times the distance from the Earth to the Sun. Its X-rays reach further still: it bulges out about two and a half degrees beyond the right-hand edge.

It’s far south, 45 degrees below the celestial equator, and from London none of it ever rises: you’d have to go south of about 44 degrees north, to Madrid, say, to see it at all, and even there it only clears the horizon. From Siding Spring it climbs to 76 degrees. It’s best from January to April; in December, when these frames were taken, it was highest in the small hours.

What a supernova remnant is

A supernova is a star exploding, and there are two main kinds. A star born with at least about eight times the Sun’s mass runs out of fuel and its core collapses, and the energy released blows the rest of the star apart, leaving a neutron star, or sometimes a black hole. Or a white dwarf, the dense core left by a smaller star, grows too heavy, usually with help from a companion, and blows itself apart in a runaway nuclear explosion, leaving nothing. Vela has a pulsar at its heart, so it was the first kind.

The debris flies out at thousands of kilometres a second, and as it sweeps up the thin gas between the stars it builds a blast wave in front of it. At first the debris coasts. After a few hundred years the gas it has swept up outweighs it, and the blast wave slows; after tens of thousands of years it has slowed enough for the gas behind it to cool and shine, and in a million years or more it merges into the gas round it and is gone. Vela is in its middle age.

The filaments in the picture are where the blast wave meets clouds of gas. The shock heats the gas, and as it cools it glows. Oxygen glows in its OIII line, blue here, where the gas is cooling from about 100,000 degrees, which takes a fast shock, of roughly 100 kilometres a second or more; hydrogen glows red behind it, where it’s cooler still. So in several places in the picture the thin blue line of oxygen runs along the outside of a band of red, as on the Pencil Nebula. Not all the red is the remnant, though: the broad, smooth red clouds are mostly gas lit by hot stars, not shocked by the blast.

Green’s catalogue lists 310 supernova remnants in our Galaxy, and a core-collapse supernova happens in it about once every 60 years, though only about nine have been seen with the naked eye in the last 2,000. Remnants shape the Galaxy round them. They scatter the elements made in stars into the gas that new stars form from, and they compress that gas and set off star formation. Most of the cosmic rays that reach the Earth are thought to come from them too: in 2013 the Fermi gamma-ray telescope found the signature of protons accelerated in two of them.

The Vela remnant

The best measure of its distance is the parallax of its pulsar, its tiny shift in position as the Earth goes round the Sun, measured with radio telescopes in 2003: about 940 light-years. For decades it was thought to be about 1,600 light-years away, and older papers still use that. It’s one of the nearest supernova remnants known.

The pulsar’s slowing spin gives its age: about 11,000 years, as seen from here. That’s the conventional figure, but other ways of reckoning give up to about 30,000. The star that exploded was probably fairly modest, eight to ten times the Sun’s mass, near the lowest mass that can end this way, and it may have been two stars that had merged. Nobody knows how bright the supernova was, but a typical one at this distance would have reached about magnitude −9, about 75 times as bright as Venus at its brightest, and been visible by day. You’ll sometimes read that it was as bright as the full Moon; that’s unlikely. Nor has any record of it in rock art or legend been confirmed, despite several claims.

The pulsar, and what you can’t see

Just above and right of the middle is where the Vela pulsar is: the neutron star the explosion left, a ball of neutrons the size of a city, spinning eleven times a second. Astronomers at the University of Sydney found it in 1968, and with the Crab pulsar it was the first direct evidence that supernovae make neutron stars. It’s moving north-west, up and to the right, at about 60 kilometres a second, so it was born a little below and left of where it is now. At magnitude 23.6 it’s far too faint for this picture, and so is Vela X, the cloud of fast particles its wind has filled below it, which shines in radio, X-rays, and gamma rays.

The Pencil Nebula and the eastern edge

On the left-hand edge, the brightest, straightest filament in the picture is the Pencil Nebula, NGC 2736, about 7 light-years long: a sheet of shocked gas seen edge on, its sharp blue edge facing out, with red hydrogen and a fan of fainter filaments trailing behind it, which the European Southern Observatory likened to a witch’s broom. John Herschel found it from the Cape of Good Hope on 1 March 1835. Its shocks are moving at about 100 to 180 kilometres a second.

It marks the edge of something you can’t see here. In 1995 astronomers using the ROSAT X-ray satellite reported six fragments of the exploded star, nicknamed shrapnel, flying out beyond the main blast wave, several with a shock wave in front of them like a bullet’s. The Pencil lies along the outer edge of one of them, shrapnel D, which is rich in oxygen, neon, and magnesium made in the star, where it’s ploughing into a cloud. Above the Pencil, the remnant’s eastern edge is a braided rope of blue filaments, with red hydrogen just inside it.

The middle

The middle of the remnant is a tangle of loops. Left of centre, a big arched loop of blue filaments looks like a bubble seen side on, and below the pulsar is a set of nested round shells. Nobody seems to have studied these loops on their own. The usual explanation for such shapes in an old remnant is that the shocked gas forms thin, rippled sheets, and they look like sharp filaments and rings wherever we see a sheet edge on. Across the top, the remnant’s northern edge is a hood of fine blue filaments over a bright tangle of red and blue.

Vela Junior

Below and left of the middle, overlapping Vela, is a second supernova remnant you can’t see: Vela Junior, found in ROSAT’s X-ray survey in 1998, a near-perfect circle 2 degrees across, as big as four full Moons. It’s younger, perhaps 1,600 to 5,100 years old, and its distance is disputed, between about 1,600 and 4,600 light-years. It barely glows in hydrogen or oxygen, because its fast shocks are running into an almost empty cavity, blown before it exploded by its star’s own wind. The blue filaments in that part of the picture are Vela’s, in front of it. A claim that it was only a few centuries old and very close, from a gamma-ray line of radioactive titanium, hasn’t held up.

Puppis A

At the top right is a third remnant, Puppis A, about 4,500 years old and four or more times further away than Vela, so behind it. It’s much brighter in X-rays, but here it shows only as small red knots, and as a pure blue knot of oxygen that’s probably its fast-moving filament of the exploded star’s own oxygen, racing out at about 1,500 kilometres a second.

Clouds and clusters behind it

The Vela remnant lies inside a much bigger glow, the Gum Nebula, 36 degrees across, whose own origin is still argued over: an old supernova remnant, or gas lit by hot stars, or both. And behind the remnant, along the plane of the Milky Way, which crosses the top left of the picture diagonally, are clouds where stars are forming now, thousands of light-years further away.

The big round red cloud at the top left is Gum 17, hydrogen lit by a pair of hot young stars at its centre, about 3,000 light-years away; Gum 15, with a dark lane of dust across it, is cut off by the top edge. On the left-hand edge, RCW 36 is a cluster only about a million years old blowing a two-lobed bubble in a cloud called Vela C, part of the Vela Molecular Ridge. At the bottom left, the bright pink knot with dark lanes is RCW 38, one of the youngest and most deeply buried massive clusters studied, with more than 2,000 stars, and in the corner, the small round nebula is Gum 25.

Several star clusters show through too. The richest and most compact, below the middle, is NGC 2660, about a billion years old, which is old for an open cluster, and about 9,000 light-years away. Below it, IC 2395 is a loose group of young blue stars about 2,300 light-years away. At the top, NGC 2671 is a tidy compact cluster beside Gum 17. The brightest star in the picture, below the middle, is b Velorum, a yellow supergiant thousands of light-years behind the remnant, so it must be enormously luminous.

The picture

T8 is a Takahashi FSQ-106ED, a 106 mm refractor built for photography, with a monochrome CCD camera, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. Each of its frames is about 4 degrees square, so this is four of them, two by two, overlapping. The picture is from 17 hours 20 minutes through the Hα filter and 18 hours 40 minutes through OIII, across the four panels: 36 hours in all. Joined, it’s 7,456 by 8,711 pixels, each covering 3.5 arcseconds. I processed it in PixInsight and finished it in Photoshop.

Hydrogen is red and oxygen blue. Narrowband filters don’t give stars their own colours, so here the stars’ colours were made from the same two filters: they’re an approximation.
