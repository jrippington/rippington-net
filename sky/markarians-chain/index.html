Markarian's Chain

The heart of the Virgo Cluster

Markarian's Chain, the curve of galaxies at the heart of the Virgo Cluster, about 54 million light-years away, with the giant M87 and its black hole below it: colliding galaxies, galaxies being stripped of their gas, and, far behind, clusters of galaxies and a quasar whose light set out 12 billion years ago. From my portable 150 mm Newtonian, in my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from these galaxies left them about 54 million years ago, in the early Eocene, among the warmest times since the dinosaurs. The London Clay was being laid down in a warm sea over south-east England then, and its fossil plants suggest a tropical coast.

This picture is from my portable 150 mm Newtonian, set up in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken in January 2024. It covers 1.6 degrees of sky by 2.3, about three full Moons by four and a half, in Virgo. It’s in natural colour, from the colour camera with no filter at all, and drizzled, a way of combining the frames that recovers finer detail, to one and a half times the camera’s own resolution. The Virgo Cluster has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is up and east is to the left. Markarian’s Chain is the curve of galaxies across the middle: it starts with the two big round galaxies at the right, M84 at the edge and M86, runs left through the pair called the Eyes, then curves up through NGC 4461, NGC 4473, and NGC 4477 towards the top left. The big round galaxy at the lower left is M87, the heart of the whole cluster. Nearly every smudge in the picture is a galaxy. At their distance, about 54 million light-years, the frame is about 1.5 million light-years across and 2.1 million high, and each pixel covers about 260 light-years.

From Oxfordshire the field climbs to about 51 degrees in the south. In January it rises late in the evening and is highest before dawn, and the Moon was new on 11 January 2024.

The Virgo Cluster

The Virgo Cluster is the nearest large cluster of galaxies, centred on M87: an extended catalogue lists 1,589 galaxies in it. Measurements of how grainy the starlight of its galaxies looks, which gives their distances, put it about 54 million light-years away, and about 8 million light-years deep from front to back. Its mass is about 600 to 700 trillion times the Sun’s, or about 1.2 thousand trillion by other methods; most of it is dark matter.

Markarian’s Chain

In 1961 the Armenian astronomer Benjamin Markarian drew attention to this curve of bright galaxies and called it a physical chain. Straight across it’s about 1.3 million light-years on the sky. But modern measurements suggest it’s mostly a line of sight: the galaxies’ speeds away from us spread over about 2,500 kilometres a second, and their distances run from about 50 to 60 million light-years. Within it, though, some pairs are really interacting.

Charles Messier catalogued M84, M86, and M87 on a single night, 18 March 1781, with several other galaxies of the cluster; William Herschel found the rest of the chain.

M86 and M84

M86, the big oval right of the middle, is coming towards us, one of the very few galaxies that is, at about 220 to 300 kilometres a second, and it’s moving at about 1,550 kilometres a second relative to M87. Its hot gas is being stripped off behind it in a tail at least 1.2 million light-years long, seen in X-rays, and faint filaments of glowing hydrogen join it to NGC 4438, the lower of the Eyes: the two seem to have collided. Neither shows in a picture like this one.

M84, at the right edge, is about 5 million light-years behind M86. It’s a radio galaxy, with twin radio jets, and a black hole of about 850 million times the Sun’s mass.

The Eyes

NGC 4438 and NGC 4435, near the middle, are the Eyes. NGC 4438, the lower, is a spiral torn apart: about 100 million years ago NGC 4435 passed within about 16,000 to 33,000 light-years of its centre, and the encounter drew out the bluish tidal tails that show here, one reaching up past NGC 4435 and one down to the right. NGC 4438 is probably on its first plunge through the cluster’s centre, and the cluster’s hot gas has stripped away its hydrogen. NGC 4435, above it, is a smooth barred lenticular galaxy.

More of the chain

Below M86 and to its right, the edge-on spiral NGC 4388 has an active nucleus and is being stripped too: a cloud of its hydrogen about 360,000 light-years long trails behind it, visible only to radio telescopes. Above M86, NGC 4402, another edge-on spiral with a dust lane, is losing its gas the same way. Up the chain, NGC 4458 is small and round, with a core that rotates differently from the rest of it, and NGC 4473 has two sets of stars orbiting in opposite directions.

M87

M87, at the lower left, is a giant elliptical galaxy about 55 million light-years away, at the cluster’s heart, and home to the first black hole ever photographed: the Event Horizon Telescope’s image, published on 10 April 2019, of a black hole of about 6.5 billion times the Sun’s mass. It has about 13,000 globular clusters, against the Milky Way’s 150 or so, though they’re far too faint to show here. In 1918 H. D. Curtis found a straight jet coming out of its core, about 5,000 light-years long; here it only shows when the picture is sharpened, a faint bump on the upper right of the core.

Far beyond Virgo

Behind the cluster are galaxies much further away. The pair NGC 4446 and NGC 4447, near the top, are about 345 million light-years away, six times further than Virgo. The galaxies of the cluster Abell 1552 spill into the bottom of the frame, their light about 1.2 billion years old. Near the top, the central galaxy of another cluster is about 4.2 billion light-years away, and below the middle a clear pinkish dot, SDSS J1228+1240, is a galaxy about 5.6 billion. The farthest galaxy that shows, SDSS J1225+1244, a faint red smudge at the right, is about 6.2 billion. And beside NGC 4477 is a cluster of galaxies, XMMU J1230.3+1339, whose light set out about 7.8 billion years ago, before the Sun formed; it’s too faint to show.

The quasars, the bright cores of distant galaxies lit by gas falling into huge black holes, look like faint stars. SDSS J1227+1356, near the top, is 11.9 billion light-years away. The farthest thing that shows is SDSS J1226+1321, a yellowish-white point right of the middle and above it, unremarkable among the stars: a quasar whose light set out about 12.0 billion years ago, when the universe was about 1.8 billion years old, lit by a black hole estimated at about 10 billion times the Sun’s mass, larger than M87’s. The farthest catalogued in the frame, DESI J187.26+14.15, right at the top edge, is a quasar a little further, too faint to show.

In front

The brightest star in the picture, HD 108614, near the bottom, is an orange star of our own Galaxy, about 830 light-years away. The star just above M87 is another, HD 108915, about 630 light-years away.

The colours

The camera’s own colours, with no filter: the elliptical galaxies yellowish with old stars, NGC 4438’s torn tails bluish with younger ones.

The picture

The telescope is a Sky-Watcher Quattro 150P, a 150 mm Newtonian reflector, with its coma corrector, at about 519 mm and f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera; its pixels cover 1.49 arcseconds on this telescope, and drizzling at 1.5 times makes each pixel of the picture about 1.0 arcsecond. The picture is 5 hours 20 minutes with no filter, in January 2024. It’s 5,820 by 8,245 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
