The Horsehead and the Flame

Close up on Orion's belt

The Horsehead Nebula and the Flame, about 1,300 light-years away in Orion, close up: a pillar of dust standing out of the Orion B cloud against the glowing curtain of IC 434, the Flame lit by a star hidden in its own dust, and Alnitak, the belt star, shining in front of them all. One night, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from the nebulae left about 1,300 years ago, around AD 720, around the time that, by tradition, Frideswide founded her minster at Oxford, the beginnings of the city; she’s said to have died in 727. The light from HD 37805, the nearer star on the ridge, left around 1705, the year building began on Blenheim Palace, at Woodstock in Oxfordshire.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken on one night in January 2026. It covers 1.4 degrees of sky by 0.9, nearly three full Moons by two, in Orion, just south of the belt’s eastern star. It’s from two filters that each pass two narrow bands of light, one hydrogen’s red line and oxygen’s green-blue, the other sulphur’s red and hydrogen’s blue, in a warmer palette than my other pictures, because I felt like a change. The stars are from exposures with no filter. The Horsehead and the Flame have galleries of their own, the Horsehead’s and the Flame’s, and Orion Wide shows this patch in its setting, the clouds of Orion round it.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left and east is down, so west is up. The Horsehead rises out of the dark ridge right of the middle, against the gold curtain of IC 434, which fills the top and middle of the picture. The brilliant blue star left of the middle is Alnitak, the eastern star of Orion’s belt; below it, in the bottom left corner, is the Flame, and right of that the blue glow of NGC 2023. The star near the top edge is σ Orionis, whose light makes IC 434 glow. At their distance, about 1,300 light-years, the frame is about 32 light-years across and 21 high, and each pixel covers about 350 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

From Oxfordshire the Horsehead never climbs higher than about 36 degrees, low in the south. In January it crosses the meridian at about 11 pm at the start of the month and about 9 pm at the end, and it’s above 30 degrees for only about four hours of full darkness.

The Horsehead

The Horsehead is a pillar of dust and gas, about 27 times the Sun’s mass, standing out of the dark Orion B cloud, which fills the bottom of the picture below the bright ridge. It’s on the near side of the cloud and lit from behind, so we see it in silhouette against the glowing gas. Its head is about 1.5 light-years across and stands about 2 light-years above the cloud. Its nose points left, to the north, towards Alnitak.

The thin bright rim round the top of its head is the mane: the edge where σ Orionis’s ultraviolet light meets the cloud, breaking up its molecules and stripping its atoms. In 2024 the James Webb Space Telescope looked at it at very high resolution and found that edge astonishingly sharp, the layer between the stripped gas and the molecular cloud less than 100 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun thick, and what may be dust being carried off the edge in the flow of gas towards the star.

On the crown of the head is a faint point of light, B33-1: a young star still forming, one of two at the tip of the pillar, where the light presses hardest. Their position fits the idea that the light, squeezing the cloud, set them off. Gaia’s measurement of B33-1 puts it about 1,330 light-years away, the Horsehead’s own distance. Inside the dark head, invisible here, are two dense cores of gas; the one in the horse’s throat may yet collapse to form a star. Without that, the Horsehead has about 5 million years left before it’s worn away.

IC 434 and σ Orionis

IC 434 is gas boiling off the face of the Orion B cloud, ionised by σ Orionis and flowing back towards it, faster as it goes: about 35 kilometres a second by the time it nears the star. Look closely at the curtain above the Horsehead and there are faint streaks running up from the ridge towards σ Orionis. They seem to be that flow made visible. Along the ridge are smaller pillars and knobs, also facing the star, like the small pillar left of the Horsehead.

σ Orionis is a triple of hot stars, the most precisely measured distance in this part of the sky: about 1,264 light-years, from the orbits of its stars. Round it is a young cluster, two or three million years old, in which the Euclid space telescope, whose picture of 2023 took in the Horsehead, has found objects drifting free of any star down to about four times Jupiter’s mass.

In 1946 Nils Cederblad’s catalogue of bright nebulae credited Alnitak with lighting both IC 434 and the Flame, and you’ll still read it. Neither is true: σ Orionis lights IC 434, and the Flame has its own star.

The Flame

The Flame, NGC 2024, fills the bottom left corner, gold and white, branching, with a dark lane of dust through it. It’s lit by IRS 2b, a hot star hidden behind that lane, which was only identified in 2003. Alnitak is just 15 arcminutes away in the sky, and its glare spills over the nebula, so it looks as if it does the lighting; but it’s probably a little in front. Behind the dust is a cluster of about 800 young stars, the youngest, about 200,000 years old, at its centre, the oldest, about 1.5 million, at its edges. In pictures it took in 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope found objects there down to two or three times Jupiter’s mass, and whether there’s a lower limit to how small such objects can be is still argued. William Herschel found the Flame on New Year’s Day 1786, and the old catalogue’s description of it ends “black spaces included”.

NGC 2023

The blue glow below and left of the Horsehead is NGC 2023, dust reflecting the light of the star at its centre, HD 37903, a young hot star about 1,300 light-years away. It’s blue for the same reason the sky is: the dust scatters blue light more than red. Round the star is a small young cluster of 20 or 30 stars, some of them firing jets: right of the nebula, the faint V-shaped knot is HH 249, one of a few small shocks where jets from young stars hit the gas round them. William Herschel found NGC 2023 on 6 January 1785. In 1983 it was one of three reflection nebulae in which astronomers found an infrared glow that ordinary dust couldn’t explain, a step towards our picture of dust that includes very small grains and large molecules.

Alnitak

Alnitak, ζ Orionis, the brilliant blue star left of the middle, is a hot supergiant with two companions, and much nearer than the nebulae, though how much nearer isn’t settled: one measurement puts it about 960 light-years away, another about 1,260. Its four long spikes aren’t real: they come from the four thin vanes in my telescope that hold its small secondary mirror, which spread a bright star’s light into a cross. σ Orionis and the other bright stars have them too.

The bright star on the ridge just left of the Horsehead, HD 37805, is nearer still, about 320 light-years away, in front of the whole scene.

The colours

The filters pass hydrogen’s red and blue lines, oxygen’s green-blue line, and sulphur’s red one, and I mapped them into a warmer palette than usual, so the hydrogen glow of IC 434 is gold. The hydrogen-beta line one of the filters passes is the same one observers use to see the Horsehead with their eyes: their hydrogen-beta filters are often sold as “Horsehead filters”. The stars are from exposures with no filter, so their colours are their own: Alnitak, σ Orionis, and the young stars blue, the cooler foreground stars yellow and orange.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. In front of it, two Antlia dual-band filters each pass two bands 5 nm wide: one hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III, the other sulphur II and hydrogen-beta. The picture is 2 hours 6 minutes through each, and 20 minutes with no filter for the stars, all on one night in January 2026. It’s 5,755 by 3,757 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, Affinity Photo, and Photoshop.
