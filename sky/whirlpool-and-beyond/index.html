The Whirlpool and the Galaxies Beyond

A spiral, its companion, and the galaxies far behind

The Whirlpool Galaxy, M51, about 25 to 28 million light-years away in Canes Venatici, with its companion NGC 5195 and the faint shell of stars their encounter pulled out; and behind them, a group of galaxies four times as far, clusters of galaxies billions of light-years away, and quasars whose light set out 12.2 billion years ago. Three nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from the Whirlpool left it about 25 to 28 million years ago, in the Oligocene. At the farther figure, it set out about when the La Garita caldera in Colorado erupted, one of the largest explosive eruptions known, about 28 million years ago. The light from the group of galaxies behind it left them about 120 million years ago, in the Early Cretaceous, when dinosaurs walked the Earth.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over three nights in April 2025. It covers 1.5 degrees of sky by 1, about three full Moons by two, in Canes Venatici. It’s in natural colour, from a filter that passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands. The Whirlpool has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left, a little up, so east is down, west up, and south to the right. The Whirlpool is the spiral at the left, face on to us, and NGC 5195, its companion, is the smaller galaxy at the end of one of its arms, left of it. Round them both is a faint brownish shell: the fan of stars rising up and to the left from NGC 5195, and a fainter skirt below and right of the Whirlpool. Across the right half are the galaxies of a group far behind: the round glow of NGC 5198 right of the middle and below it, and higher up NGC 5173, NGC 5169, and the thin IC 4263. Among the stars are smaller smudges, and nearly all of them are galaxies too. At the Whirlpool’s distance the frame is about two-thirds of a million light-years across, and each pixel covers about 100 to 120 light-years.

From Oxfordshire the Whirlpool never sets, and it crosses the meridian 85.5 degrees up, almost overhead, so through April it was high all night. Full darkness shrank from about seven hours a night at the start of the month to about four at the end, and the Moon was full on 13 April and new on 27 April.

The Whirlpool

The Whirlpool, M51, NGC 5194, is a grand-design spiral: two strong, clean arms wound round its core. Its distance has been measured two ways, by the brightest red giants among its stars, about 28 million light-years, and by its Cepheids and a supernova, about 24.5 million; the two disagree by more than their errors, so about 25 to 28 million light-years is fair. NASA’s 31 million is further than both. It’s about 80,000 to 91,000 light-years across.

Charles Messier found it on 13 October 1773, while he was following a comet, as a very faint nebula. In April 1845 Lord Rosse, at Birr Castle in Ireland with his 72-inch telescope, saw that it was a spiral, the first spiral ever recognised, and that June he showed his sketch to the British Association in Cambridge.

Its core is active, a Seyfert nucleus, but the black hole there is hidden behind so much gas that even most of its X-rays can’t get out. Three supernovae have been seen in it: 1994I, 2005cs, and 2011dh, found by four amateur astronomers independently in 2011; the last two have been used to measure its distance. In 2019 a fainter outburst, AT 2019abn, peaked between a nova and a supernova in brightness. And in 2020, a brief dip in the X-rays from one of its star systems was put forward as the first candidate for a planet in another galaxy.

NGC 5195

NGC 5195, the companion, was found by Pierre Méchain on 21 March 1781, and Messier listed the pair together, which is why M51 sometimes means the pair. It’s at the end of the Whirlpool’s northern arm, about 4.4 arcminutes from its core, about 31,000 to 36,000 light-years on the sky, but it’s behind the arm: in my picture the arm’s dark lanes of dust cross in front of its bright core. It has an active nucleus of its own, so the pair have two active nuclei, both feeding their black holes very slowly. A supernova, 1945A, appeared in it in April 1945.

The two have met. NGC 5195 swung past the Whirlpool within the last few hundred million years, perhaps more than once, and simulations of encounters like that raise two strong arms like the Whirlpool’s: its spiral is probably the encounter’s doing. Whether NGC 5195 passed once or twice is still debated.

The shell

The faint brownish shell round the pair looks like dust, but it’s starlight. Deep pictures of 2015 show it as streams of stars pulled out of both galaxies by the encounter, old stars spread very thin. The fan rising up and to the left from NGC 5195 is the northern of its western streams, curving at its top; the broad glow just left of NGC 5195 is a three-pronged “crown”; the glow below NGC 5195 is its eastern streams; and the skirt below and right of the Whirlpool is a tail drawn from the Whirlpool’s own disc, a little bluer than the rest. From the tip of the fan to the edge of the skirt the shell is about 20 arcminutes, about 140,000 to 160,000 light-years, nearly twice the Whirlpool’s own width.

It looks brown because old stars are redder than the young ones of the Whirlpool’s arms, and yellow-orange light this faint, on a black sky, reads as brown; processing may deepen it at the faintest levels. The real dust is in the reddish-brown lanes that thread the Whirlpool’s arms and cross NGC 5195’s core. Further out still, the Whirlpool’s hydrogen gas stretches far beyond these stars in a long tail, but with no stars in it, it’s invisible here.

The galaxies beyond

The galaxies across the right half are a group of their own, unrelated to the Whirlpool: they’re moving away from us about five times as fast, so they’re about 115 to 130 million light-years away, four to five times as far. The biggest, NGC 5198, is a smooth elliptical galaxy about 70,000 light-years across. NGC 5173 is another, round, with a bright core. NGC 5169 is a barred spiral tilted steeply to us, its arms knotted even at that angle, and IC 4263 is a thin, knotty spiral seen edge on; an old radio measurement put IC 4263 at the Whirlpool’s speed, but that was probably the Whirlpool’s own gas in the radio telescope’s wide beam. MCG+08-25-006, a small spiral just below a bright star whose spikes cross near it, and a few smaller galaxies belong to the group too.

Others only look close to the Whirlpool. IC 4277, a strikingly thin, straight needle of a galaxy just left of its shell, is probably about 650 million light-years away, though that’s only an estimate from its colours. IC 4278, the compact galaxy below NGC 5195, is about 240 million. And a small galaxy that seems to sit inside NGC 5195’s western stream is about 80 times further away than the stream: its light is about 2.1 billion years old.

Far beyond

Further still, clusters of galaxies. At the lower left, a knot of a dozen small orange galaxies is a cluster whose light set out about 2.1 billion years ago. At the upper right, a sprinkle of faint orange dots round a small elongated galaxy is the richest cluster in the frame, its light about 4.1 billion years old: it left when the Earth was only about 400 million years old. The farthest single galaxies that show are two faint dots, indistinguishable from stars, whose light is about 7 billion years old, about 2.4 billion years older than the Sun.

The quasars, the bright cores of distant galaxies lit by gas falling into huge black holes, go further. About 190 confirmed quasars are in the frame, and about 150 of them show as faint dots, among them 47 of the 60 whose light set out more than 10.5 billion years ago. The brightest, B3 1330+476, at the lower left, is a radio source too, its light about 6.3 billion years old.

The farthest thing in the frame, and the farthest that shows, is SDSS J1332+4714, a faint, warm-coloured dot near the bottom edge, below and left of the Whirlpool: a quasar whose light set out about 12.2 billion years ago, when the universe was about 1.6 billion years old. The second farthest, SDSS J1332+4701, is a still fainter orange dot below the Whirlpool, almost as far. Both are warmer in colour than the nearer quasars, which look bluish, probably because the hydrogen in the space between the galaxies has taken out their blue light on its way to us. You may find a quasar here listed further, at a redshift of 4.12, but that’s a bad fit; its spectra put it at 3.03.

In front

The brightest star in the picture, HD 117815, at the lower left, is a white star of our own Galaxy about 370 light-years away, some 70,000 times nearer than the Whirlpool.

The colours

The colours are close to natural: the filter passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record. The Whirlpool’s disc is a little blue with young stars, NGC 5195 yellower with old ones, and the shell brownish, faint old starlight.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. The picture is 14 hours 26 minutes through an Altair TRI-RGB filter, over three nights in April 2025. It’s 6,013 by 3,969 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
