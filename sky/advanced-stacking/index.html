Stacking beyond the average

Every frame's own blur, and ImageMM

An ordinary stack is an average, and an average blends the sharp frames taken in moments of steady air with the soft ones taken while it boiled, so the stack is never as sharp as its best frames. Newer methods keep each frame's own blur and solve for the one sharp sky that explains them all. This is what an average gives away, the ways of getting some of it back, how ImageMM works, and what amateurs have found with it so far.

This is the third article at Optica’s stacking stop, after Aligning and stacking and Drizzle, and it goes further than either. The ordinary stack, an average of registered frames with the outliers left out, works very well, and nothing here replaces it as the place to start. But it gives something away, and over the last few years, and especially since a paper in 2025, there’s been a lot of interest among amateurs in getting it back. Some of what follows is well established and some is new enough that the verdict isn’t in, and the text says which is which. As before, the last section sets the same ideas out as mathematics.

What an average gives away

The air above a telescope never keeps still, and how much it blurs a star changes from moment to moment and from one frame to the next: a breeze gets up, the target sinks lower, the focus drifts as the night cools. So each frame has its own blur. The shape a single point of light makes in a frame, its star image, is called the frame’s point-spread function, or PSF, because it shows how the frame spreads out a point. A sharp frame’s PSF is narrow; a soft one’s is wide.

Averaging frames averages their PSFs too. Every star in the stack is the average of that star in every frame, so its shape is a mixture: a narrow core from the sharp frames, sitting on broad wings from the soft ones. If a third of the frames have stars 2 pixels across at half their peak (their FWHM), a third 3, and a third 4, the stack’s stars come out about 2.6 pixels across, better than the frames’ average of 3, but with wings as wide as the worst frame’s, and well short of the best frame’s 2.

Sharpening the stack afterwards can win some of that back. The usual way is deconvolution, which works out what sharp picture, blurred by a known PSF, would give the picture you have. But deconvolving a stack works from the mixed PSF, measured from the stack’s own stars. By then it can’t tell which detail came from the sharp frames and which from the soft ones, because the average has already blended them. Every method in this article is a way of using the frames’ differences before the average blends them away.

Choosing and weighting

The simplest answer is to leave out the softest frames. Grading, described in Aligning and stacking, measures every frame’s stars and can set the worst aside. The cost is light: every frame left out is signal not used, and the stack is noisier for it. One imager on AstroBin found that cutting the worst half of their frames by FWHM improved their stack’s FWHM by only about 0.1 arcseconds, a small gain for half the light.

The next answer is to weight the frames by sharpness as well as by noise. PixInsight’s PSF Signal Weight, introduced in 2022, rewards frames whose stars are smaller as well as those with more signal and less noise, and it’s the default in its batch processing script; Siril can weight by a measure of star width it calls weighted FWHM; Astro Pixel Processor offers weighting by star shape, which its developer says sometimes gives a stack whose stars are more than 10 per cent narrower. Not everyone agrees that it’s wise. John Murphy, who wrote PixInsight’s NormalizeScaleGradient script, argues that the only weights that maximise the signal-to-noise ratio are based on it alone, and that weighting by FWHM gives a noisier result. Both are right about what they’re optimising: one wants the least noise, the other a balance of noise and sharpness.

Some imagers do both: they make the usual stack from everything, and a second, sharper one from the best 10 to 30 per cent of the frames, and blend the sharp one into the places where detail matters most, such as a galaxy’s core. Either way, the result is still an average, and its PSF is still a mixture, only one that leans towards the sharper frames.

Lucky imaging

Planetary imagers take choosing to its limit. The air’s turbulence is effectively frozen in an exposure shorter than about 5 to 15 milliseconds, so a planetary camera records video, thousands of frames, and in a few of them the air happens to be steady. Software keeps the sharpest few per cent and stacks only those, which is called lucky imaging. AutoStakkert!, the standard program, and PlanetarySystemStacker go further, with multi-point alignment: they lay a grid of alignment points over the planet and choose the best frames separately for each point, since the air can be steady over one part of the image while it blurs another. Professional astronomers use the same idea on large telescopes: in 2006, Nicholas Law, Craig Mackay, and John Baldwin improved the resolution of the 2.56-metre Nordic Optical Telescope by 2.5 to 4 times in poorer seeing.

Deep-sky imagers have tried the same thing since low-noise CMOS cameras made very short exposures affordable: frames of a few seconds to perhaps half a minute, with the softest culled. With frames thousands of times longer than the air’s frozen moments, this picks the calmer spells of seeing rather than freezing it, so it’s more like strict grading than true lucky imaging, and one practitioner advises not to expect miracles. Still, with an 8-inch telescope and 10-second frames, keeping the best half, Robert Majewski reached stars 1.9 arcseconds across on M51 before any sharpening.

Using every frame’s own blur

In 2015, two astronomers, Barak Zackay and Eran Ofek, asked what the best way to combine survey images really is, and showed that the ordinary weighted average isn’t it. For finding faint objects and measuring their brightness, each frame should first be filtered by its own PSF, which favours the detail it records best, and only then added up, weighted by its transparency and noise. A second step evens out the result so that its noise is uniform at every scale. They called it proper coaddition. Its stars are typically as narrow as the best frame’s, or narrower; it keeps all the information the frames had; and, unlike deconvolution, it never divides by small numbers, so it can’t blow up. They measured gains in survey speed, a measure of how quickly faint objects can be found, of between a few per cent and 25 per cent over a weighted average.

Proper coaddition is still linear, an adding up of frames with clever weights, and it doesn’t try to remove the blur. It shows, though, that there’s information in the frames’ differences that an average discards, and the methods that go furthest try to use all of it.

Solving for the sky

Every frame is the end of a chain: the sky as it is above the air; blurred by that frame’s PSF; recorded on the camera’s pixels; and with noise added. Multi-frame restoration, or multi-frame deconvolution, runs the chain backwards for all the frames at once. It asks what single sharp sky, put through each frame’s own blur, would best match every frame recorded, and improves its guess step by step until the answer stops changing. The sharp frames then constrain the fine detail, because their blur preserved it, while the soft frames still add their light at larger scales.

The idea isn’t new. William Richardson and Leon Lucy described the classic way to deconvolve a single picture in 1972 and 1974, and Timothy Schulz extended it to many frames with unknown blurs in 1993. In 2011, Michael Hirsch and colleagues published a version that takes the frames one at a time, so thousands can be used, and they showed it on amateur equipment: a 12-inch Meade telescope, and 2,350 video frames of the crater Copernicus through a Celestron 14.

Deconvolution of any kind has the same risks. It amplifies noise if run too long, so it has to be stopped at the right point; it draws dark rings round bright stars, called ringing, when the PSF is slightly wrong or the data are noisy; and it’s only as good as its PSF. Multi-frame methods add a heavier version of the last: they need a good PSF for every frame.

ImageMM

ImageMM is the method that brought this to amateurs’ attention. It was published in The Astronomical Journal on 29 September 2025 by Yashil Sukurdeep, Tamás Budavári, and Fausto Navarro of Johns Hopkins University and Andrew Connolly of the University of Washington, after a first version on arXiv that January. The MM in its name stands for majorize-minimize, the mathematical method it uses.

It takes many registered frames of the same field, each with its own PSF measured from the stars in it, and solves for one sharp, positive picture of the sky that best explains them all, weighting every pixel of every frame by how noisy it is. It starts from a flat or average picture and multiplies it, pixel by pixel, by a correction worked out from all the frames together: where the frames show more light than the current guess predicts, the pixel is raised, and where less, lowered. In the paper, each correction is limited to at most doubling or halving a pixel in one step, which the authors found speeds it up. There’s no smoothing rule, as many deconvolution methods have; the only constraint is that no pixel may be negative, so, with the sky’s own level taken off first, the empty sky between the stars and galaxies is driven to zero and the light is gathered into the objects.

Majorize-minimize is what makes each step safe. Finding the best picture directly is hard, so each step replaces the problem with an easier one: a simpler curve that lies everywhere on or above the real measure of misfit and touches it at the current guess. Its lowest point is easy to find, and since the simpler curve is never below the real one and touches it where the step began, the real misfit can only go down. Repeated, the guess walks downhill to the best answer.

Two refinements matter for real data. A robust version weighs down any pixel that disagrees strongly with the rest, such as a satellite’s trail or a cosmic ray, inside the fit itself rather than by rejecting it beforehand; in the paper’s test it removed a satellite trail that the plain version left partly in. And a super-resolution version solves for a picture on a grid finer than the camera’s pixels, as drizzle does, but removing the blur at the same time.

The authors tested it on 33 frames from Hyper Suprime-Cam, the wide-field camera on the 8.2-metre Subaru Telescope, and compared it with the survey’s own stack of the same frames. Their measure of sharpness rose by about 13 per cent, and faint galaxies became visible that the stack doesn’t show, more so with super-resolution. On a graphics card, a field of 1,000 by 1,000 pixels took under 12 seconds.

Some of the headline numbers need care. The paper reports the sky’s noise as about ten thousand times lower than the stack’s, but that’s largely because the method sets the empty sky to zero, not a like-for-like reduction in noise; and the authors note that the faintest sources, beyond about magnitude 25, come out systematically too bright, because light from the noisy background gets gathered into them. The paper doesn’t compare ImageMM with the stack deconvolved, which is the comparison an amateur would most like to see, and the authors haven’t released their code. Press coverage said the method makes pictures from the ground “as clear as those taken from space”; the paper itself makes no such comparison.

What a simulation shows

A simulation shows what the method does, and what it doesn’t. Twelve frames of a small patch of sky with stars and a galaxy, their seeing ranging from 1.8 to 5 pixels across, each with its own noise, are combined four ways: the sharpest frame alone; the plain average; ImageMM’s update over all twelve; and the same update applied to the average alone, with the average’s own blur, which is ordinary deconvolution done the same way.

Both restorations brought the stars from 2.8 pixels across in the average back to about 1.16, the sharpness of the simulated sky itself. Restoring from all twelve frames got there in about half as many steps, 40 against 80, and left a grainier background; deconvolving the average left a smoother one. Run much longer, both got worse, as the noise was amplified. So in this idealised case, where every blur is known exactly and the noise is simple, most of the gain comes from deconvolution itself, and solving from every frame adds less than the pictures in the press suggest. That fits the theory, which puts the extra information in the frames’ differences at a modest amount, and it fits what amateurs have reported. Real data are another matter: blurs that vary across the field and must be estimated, frames that aren’t perfectly aligned, and noise that isn’t simple. That’s where the methods may differ most, and it’s what the comparisons now being made will show.

ImageMM for amateurs

Three days after the paper appeared, Franklin Marek of Seti Astro released an implementation in Seti Astro Suite Pro, a free processing program for Windows, macOS, and Linux, where it’s called MFDeconv, for multi-frame deconvolution. It runs on graphics cards from NVIDIA, Apple’s own chips, or other Windows graphics cards, or more slowly on the processor. It adds engineering of its own to cope with amateur data: masks that limit how much bright stars’ cores can push the result, to reduce ringing; noise maps for each frame; a normal stack with rejection first, whose rejection maps then tell the restoration which pixels to ignore; and smaller, gentler steps. It also offers super-resolution, though its own notes say drizzle probably gives better results for the computing time. It needs a lot of memory: Seti Astro recommends at least 32 gigabytes for practical work and 64 for serious use.

It’s the only ready-made way for an amateur to use the method so far. A PixInsight script by another author handles only a single picture, not many frames; PixInsight’s developers were reported on a forum in October 2025 to be evaluating it as a standard process, a report that comes only second-hand; and Siril, up to 1.4.4, doesn’t offer it.

What amateurs report is mixed. In the first weeks, users on Cloudy Nights saw dark rings round stars, more noise, and long runs that strained powerful computers; one careful comparison found ImageMM’s raw output sharper than a conventional stack’s, and ImageMM followed by BlurXTerminator (a sharpening tool applied after stacking) better than the stack followed by it, but the gain overall “comparable to conventional deconvolution”. Others have called their results the sharpest they’d made. Most of the critical reports date from before the program’s masks, noise maps, and rejection were added, and it has changed constantly since, so the early verdicts may not hold. Nobody seems yet to have published a careful comparison of the current version.

Trying it fairly is simple to describe: stack the same frames both ways, apply the same later steps and the same stretch to both, and compare. Saving the intermediate steps shows where ringing starts. And it needs the frames themselves, calibrated and registered, not just the finished stack, so it’s worth keeping them.

Where this leaves stacking

An average of registered frames with the outliers rejected remains the foundation, and for most pictures it’s enough. Weighting already takes some account of sharpness, drizzle recovers what large pixels lose, and deconvolution after stacking recovers much of what the air took. Methods that use every frame’s own blur are the frontier: sound in principle, with real gains shown on professional data, but young in amateur hands, demanding of computers, and still to prove by how much they beat stacking and then deconvolving.
