Darks, flats, and bias

Taking the camera and the telescope out of the picture

Every raw frame carries the camera's and the telescope's fingerprints along with the sky: an offset the camera adds, the warmth of its silicon, the darkening towards the corners, and the shadows of dust. Calibration frames record those fingerprints on their own, so they can be taken back out. This is what bias frames, dark frames, and flat frames each record, how to take them well, and why the newest CMOS cameras often don't need darks at all.

This follows on from How astro cameras work, which explains the offset, dark current, hot pixels, and noise that turn up here, and it’s the next stop along Optica’s path. The idea is simple, and the details are where pictures go wrong, so it’s worth taking slowly. Everything is explained in words first; the last section sets the same ideas out as mathematics, for anyone who wants them.

What calibration is for

Every pixel of a raw frame of the target (a light frame, in the jargon) holds more than the light from the sky. Four things are mixed together in it:

  • the offset the camera adds to every reading, with a faint pattern from its readout electronics;
  • the dark signal: electrons freed by the silicon’s own warmth, fastest in hot pixels, and in some cameras a glow from the readout circuits (amp glow);
  • the light from the sky and the target, but not quite as it arrived: the telescope dims it towards the corners (vignetting), dust casts shadows in it, and each pixel’s sensitivity differs slightly from its neighbours’;
  • and random noise, from all of these.

The first two are added to the light. The third changes it in proportion: a dust shadow that blocks 5 per cent of the light takes 5 units from a faint patch of sky worth 100, and 500 from a star worth 10,000, so no single amount could be subtracted to fix both. Calibration is therefore a subtraction and a division. Take away the offset and the dark signal, divide by the telescope’s response, and what’s left is the light from the sky and the target, with its noise.

Each kind of calibration frame records one of those fingerprints on its own, with no light from the target. Bias frames record the offset. Dark frames record the offset and the dark signal together. Flat frames record the telescope’s response. Many of each are taken and averaged into a master frame, so that the master is almost free of noise, and the masters are then applied to every light frame.

What calibration can’t do is take out the random noise. It’s different in every frame, so no frame taken in advance can record it; only more light, and stacking more frames, reduces it. Calibration takes out what’s the same every time.

Bias frames: the camera’s starting point

A bias frame is the shortest exposure the camera can make, with no light reaching the sensor: the telescope capped, or the camera covered. In so short a time almost no dark signal can collect, so all a bias frame records is what the camera adds to every reading: its offset, and the faint, fixed pattern of its readout, often a texture of slightly different columns or rows.

The bias frame’s main job is to take that offset out of the flats. A flat is used by dividing by it, so it has to be a picture of the telescope’s response and nothing else. Left in, the offset adds the same amount to every pixel of the flat, bright or dim, which makes the vignetting and the dust shadows look shallower than they really are, so the flat corrects them too little, and faint traces of both remain. Bias frames are also needed if darks are to be scaled to a different exposure time, since only the dark signal grows with time; the offset doesn’t.

They’re quick to take, in daylight or under cloud, as long as the gain and offset are set as they will be for the frames the bias corrects. Most guides suggest a few dozen, averaged into a master bias.

Some CMOS cameras are an exception. Their sensors work differently for very short exposures, so a bias frame doesn’t show the same offset as a longer exposure does. The best-known case is the Panasonic sensor in ZWO’s ASI1600, whose bias frames shorter than about a third of a second show gradients and glow that its longer exposures don’t. Diffraction Limited, who make the capture software MaxIm DL, advise against bias frames with CMOS sensors altogether, and recommend dark flats instead: darks taken at the flats’ exposure, which come up again below. Sony’s sensors, such as the IMX571 and IMX455, don’t seem to share the problem, and with sensors whose offset is very even, Siril can subtract a single number in place of a master bias.

Dark frames: the camera’s warmth

A dark frame is an exposure made exactly like a light frame, at the same exposure time, sensor temperature, gain, offset, and binning, but with no light reaching the sensor. It records everything the camera adds over that time: the offset; the dark signal, built up as the silicon’s warmth frees electrons; the hot pixels, where that happens much faster; and amp glow, if the sensor has it. Subtracting a master dark from each light frame takes all of those out in one go, the offset included, which is why a calibration that uses darks for the lights doesn’t need bias frames for them as well.

What a dark can’t remove is the dark signal’s noise. Dark current, like light, arrives at random, so a dark frame records only its average and its fixed pattern, and the random part in each light frame stays where it is. Each dark frame also has random noise of its own, which subtracting it adds to the light. In the worst case, subtracting a single dark frame from a single light frame raises the noise by about 40 per cent, but averaging many darks into a master shrinks the master’s own noise until it hardly adds anything. Most guides suggest at least 20 darks, and 50 or more is better.

Darks have to match the lights closely. A different temperature changes the dark current, which roughly doubles for every 5 to 7 °C warmer, and a different gain or offset changes everything. With a cooled camera that holds a set temperature, that’s easy: a set of darks for each combination of exposure, temperature, gain, and offset that you use can be kept as a library and reused for months, refreshed perhaps twice a year as the sensor ages. Darks don’t need a clear sky, so they can be taken indoors or under cloud. Light must be kept out completely, though. Some plastic caps let infrared light through, so a metal cap or a layer of foil is safer, and a master dark that’s brighter in the middle, or has a gradient across it, has had light get in.

Uncooled cameras, such as DSLRs, are harder. Their sensors follow the night’s temperature, and warm as the camera works, so their darks have to be taken in the same session, as close to the lights’ temperature as possible. Most cameras offer long-exposure noise reduction, which takes a dark straight after each exposure. It matches well, but it halves the time spent collecting light, so most astrophotographers turn it off and take a set of darks during the session instead.

Software can also scale a master dark to a different exposure time, since the dark signal grows in proportion to time: with the offset taken out first, a 600-second master dark multiplied by a tenth stands in for a 60-second one. It works for sensors whose dark signal behaves simply, but not for those with amp glow, which doesn’t grow in a straight line, and some CMOS cameras don’t scale well at all, so darks taken at the lights’ own exposure are the safer choice.

Why the newest cameras often skip darks

For a long time, darks were part of every astrophotographer’s routine. The latest generation of CMOS sensors, such as Sony’s IMX571, IMX455, IMX533, and IMX585, has changed that for many people. Cooled, their dark current is tiny, somewhere between a ten-thousandth and a hundredth of an electron per pixel per second, depending on whose measurements you believe and how cold the sensor is, so a five-minute frame collects between a few hundredths of an electron and a few electrons of dark signal. They’re also made without amp glow. iTelescope says of T70, whose camera has the IMX571, that its dark current is so low that it makes dark frames “virtually redundant”. Robin Glover, who wrote the SharpCap capture software, makes the same point, and describes SharpCap’s removal of hot pixels as an alternative to subtracting darks rather than an addition to it.

Skipping darks doesn’t mean skipping calibration. The offset still has to come out of every light frame, so a master bias, or with a sensor whose offset is even a single number, takes the dark’s place for the lights. The flats still need their dark flats or bias. Hot pixels are still there, but there are other ways to deal with them. Dithering moves the telescope a few pixels between frames, so that each hot pixel falls on a different part of the target each time, and stacking rejects it. Cosmetic correction finds pixels that stand out from their neighbours and replaces them with an estimate from those neighbours.

It isn’t a rule, though, and opinions differ. Darks are still worth taking with cameras whose sensors glow, such as ZWO’s ASI294, ASI183, ASI178, and ASI1600; with uncooled cameras and DSLRs, especially on warm nights; with older CCDs, whose darks carry column patterns of their own; with long exposures; and with sensors that have many warm pixels. Many astrophotographers take them anyway, on the grounds that a library of darks costs nothing once it exists, and that a master made from 50 frames or more adds almost no noise. If in doubt, calibrate the same frames both ways and compare.

Flat frames: the telescope’s fingerprints

A flat frame is a picture of nothing in particular: an evenly lit, featureless field, photographed through the telescope exactly as it’s set up for the night. Because the light going in is the same everywhere, any difference between one pixel and another in a flat comes from the optical system, and that’s what it records.

Three things show up in it. Vignetting is the light falling off towards the edges and corners of the frame, because the telescope, the focuser, a reducer, or a filter cuts off some of the light heading for them; on some systems the corners get a third less light than the middle. Dust shadows are the out-of-focus shadows of specks on the filters, the camera’s window, or the reducer. And each pixel’s sensitivity differs slightly from its neighbours’, usually by less than 1 per cent.

Dust shadows have a shape worth understanding. The light reaching the sensor is a cone, converging from the whole aperture to a focus, so a speck in the cone shades a patch of the sensor rather than a point, and its shadow is the shape of the cone where the speck sits. In a reflector, or any telescope with a central obstruction, that cone is hollow, so the shadow is a ring, the familiar dust donut. The further the dust is from the sensor, the wider the cone at that point, and the bigger and fainter the ring: its width is about the dust’s distance from the sensor divided by the focal ratio. At f/5, a speck on a filter 15 mm from the sensor casts a ring about 3 mm across, some 800 pixels on a sensor with 3.76-micrometre pixels; one on the camera’s window 2 mm away casts a ring about 0.4 mm across, around 100 pixels. That’s a useful way of tracking dust down. Dust on the sensor itself can’t be corrected, since almost no light reaches the pixels it covers.

All three effects scale the light that reaches each pixel, multiplying it by a little less than one, which is why flats are divided out rather than subtracted. A master flat is scaled so that its average is one, and dividing a light frame by it lifts each pixel by exactly as much as it was dimmed: the corners, the dust rings, and the pixels’ small differences all disappear. Flats can’t help with light that’s been added rather than dimmed, such as gradients from light pollution, reflections, or light leaking into the camera. Those are dealt with later in processing.

Taking a good flat

The first rule is that nothing changes between the lights and the flats. Flats record the optical system exactly as it was, so the camera mustn’t be rotated, the focus mustn’t be changed, the filter mustn’t be swapped, and nothing should be taken off or put back. That’s why flats are usually taken at the end of the night, before anything is touched, or at the start, as long as nothing is touched afterwards.

What’s needed is light that’s the same all across the aperture, and there are three common ways to get it.

Twilight sky is the original. The sky just after sunset or before dawn is bright and fairly even, most of all near the zenith, a little away from the side the Sun is on. It’s free, but the window is short, perhaps 20 minutes, its brightness changes all the time, and stars begin to show, so it’s best to move the telescope slightly between frames, so that stacking can reject them.

The T-shirt method is the cheapest way to make light even. A plain white T-shirt, stretched tight over the front of the telescope with no folds or seams across the aperture, makes a diffuser. Pointed at the twilight sky or an evenly lit wall, it spreads the light so evenly that its weave hardly shows, especially if it’s turned a little between frames, and two layers do better than one.

Warning

Some guides suggest pointing a T-shirt-covered telescope at the Sun to take flats. Don’t. A telescope pointed at the Sun can destroy a camera in moments if the cover slips, and it’s a serious danger to anyone’s eyes. Use bright sky well away from the Sun, or a light panel.

A light panel laid over the front of the telescope gives even light at any time, indoors or out, so flats can be taken at the end of a session without waiting for twilight. Dedicated flat panels, such as Optec’s Flip-Flat, are made for the job, and some double as a dust cap. Cheaper ones do very well too. I use an A3 LED tracing panel, the kind artists trace drawings on, which cost about £20 on Amazon. An A3 panel’s lit area, about 31 by 43 centimetres, covers the front of a 250 mm telescope with room to spare.

Cheap panels have two quirks worth knowing about. Most dim their LEDs by switching them on and off very quickly (pulse-width modulation), far too fast for the eye to see. A CMOS camera reads its rows one after another, so in a short exposure, a fraction of a second, each row catches a different number of flashes, and the flat comes out banded. The cure is a longer exposure, a few seconds, in which every row catches hundreds or thousands of flashes; turning the panel down, or laying a sheet or two of plain paper or opal acrylic over it, makes room for that. And a cheap panel isn’t perfectly even across its whole area, so it’s worth keeping the telescope’s opening over its middle.

The colour of the light doesn’t matter much. Panels and twilight are often bluish, and that’s fine for a mono camera, because each flat is scaled to an average of one anyway. With a one-shot colour camera, the red, green, and blue pixels need scaling separately, or the corrected pictures take on a colour cast; processing software such as Siril does that for you (it calls it equalising the CFA, the colour filter array).

How bright, and how many

A flat has to be bright enough for the offset and read noise to be small beside it, and dim enough to stay well clear of the top of the sensor’s range, where its response stops being a straight line and finally clips. Between those limits there’s plenty of room. Most guides put the peak of the flat’s histogram between a third and a half of the way up; SharpCap suggests a little higher, and N.I.N.A.’s flat wizard aims for half by default. Siril’s documentation puts the upper limit bluntly: “an overexposed flat is the guarantee of a failed calibration”.

My own preference is to put the peak at about 40 per cent, some 26,000 on a 16-bit camera. The histogram of a good flat is narrow, since the light is meant to be even, with a tail to the left from the darker corners.

The exposure time follows from the brightness of the light and the filter; a narrowband filter needs far longer than a clear or colour one. Very short exposures are best avoided with CMOS cameras, both because of panel flicker and because some sensors behave oddly below half a second or so, and a few seconds is comfortable. Capture software can work the exposure out for you: N.I.N.A.’s flat wizard and SharpCap both adjust it until the histogram sits where you ask.

Take flats at the same gain and offset as the lights. That’s the usual advice, and the safe one. Not everyone agrees that the gain has to match exactly (SharpCap’s author says it makes little difference to flats), but it matters that the camera stays in the same gain mode, since sensors with a high-gain mode behave differently in it. As for how many: the master flat’s noise ends up in every calibrated frame, so it pays to take plenty. Twenty is a common minimum, and 30 to 50 is better.

Dark flats

Flats have an offset too, and a little dark signal, and both have to come out before a flat can be used, for the reason above: it has to be a picture of the telescope’s response and nothing else. That’s done with dark flats, also called flat darks: dark frames taken at the same exposure, gain, and temperature as the flats, with the telescope capped. A master dark flat, subtracted from each flat before the flats are averaged, takes out the offset and whatever dark signal and glow the flats picked up. A master bias does the same job for sensors whose bias frames behave, but dark flats are the safer choice with CMOS cameras, and they take only a minute or two.

Flats that go wrong show it in the calibrated pictures. If the corners come out brighter than the middle, or the dust shadows have turned into bright rings, the flats are over-correcting. The usual causes are an offset left in the lights (when neither darks nor bias have been taken out of them), light leaking into the camera while the flats were taken, flats exposed so brightly that they’d stopped being linear, or dew on the optics. If faint dark rings remain, or the corners are still a little dark, the flats are under-correcting. An offset left in the flats does that, and so do flats that simply don’t match the lights, because the camera was turned, a filter moved, or the dust shifted after they were taken.

When flats need to be retaken

Flats describe one particular setup, so they need taking again whenever it changes, and in practice that means every session. Flats from one night shouldn’t be used for another unless the telescope stays in its observatory and nothing at all has been touched, and even then dust moves. These are the changes that call for new flats:

The camera is rotatedDust on the camera’s side, on its window and filters, turns with it, while the telescope’s own vignetting and dust stay put, so the two no longer line up. With a rotator, take flats at each angle you use.
A filter is changedEach filter has its own dust, and often slightly different vignetting, so every filter needs its own flats. A filter wheel or a filter drawer may not put a filter back in exactly the same place.
The focus is changed by muchThe dust rings change size. The small adjustments of a night’s focusing are usually fine.
The camera, reducer, or flattener comes off and goes back onNothing will be quite where it was.
Dust has moved, or there’s new dustSpecks shift with handling and with changes of temperature, and old flats then leave rings behind.
Dew forms on the opticsThe flats must match the optics as they were.
It’s another night, and the setup was taken downAll of the above at once.

A meridian flip, in which the mount swings the telescope over to the other side partway through the night, doesn’t call for new flats, as long as the camera stays fixed to the telescope: the whole optical train turns together, so nothing moves relative to anything else.

The recipe

Put together, calibration runs in this order. The dark flats, or bias, are averaged into a master and taken out of each flat. The flats are averaged into a master flat, scaled to an average of one. The darks, or a master bias if darks aren’t used, are averaged into a master dark. Then every light frame has the master dark subtracted and is divided by the master flat. Any hot pixels left are dealt with by cosmetic correction and by stacking. With a one-shot colour camera, all of this happens on the raw mosaic, before debayering, because calibration corrects each pixel on its own.

Processing software such as Siril, PixInsight, and DeepSkyStacker does all of this automatically once it knows which frames are which. The care goes into taking them.
