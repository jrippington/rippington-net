A giant galaxy and the trail of its jet

Centaurus A, NGC 5128, the nearest radio galaxy, about 12 million light-years away: a giant galaxy made by a merger, a warped lane of dust across its face, and, up and to the left, a chain of pink knots where the jet from its black hole stirs the gas 30,000 light-years out. From a 20-inch telescope in Chile, with hydrogen's light added to show the jet's trail.

The light from Centaurus A left it about 12 million years ago, in the middle of the Miocene. In what’s now Bavaria, about 11.6 million years ago, lived Danuvius, an ape whose bones, found in a clay pit near Pforzen, suggest it may have walked upright.

This picture is from iTelescope’s T72, a 20-inch reflector at Deep Sky Chile, which I used remotely in March 2023. It covers 25 arcminutes of sky by 20, a little less than the full Moon, in Centaurus. It’s in natural colour, from luminance, red, green, and blue filters, with hydrogen’s own light added from frames through a hydrogen-alpha filter, its starlight taken away, to show the faint trail of the galaxy’s jet. Centaurus A has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is up and east is to the left. Centaurus A fills the frame with its soft golden glow, and across its middle, from the lower left to the upper right, runs its dark, twisted lane of dust, edged with pink and blue, with the bright nucleus inside it. Up and to the left, beyond the galaxy’s bright body, is a small chain of pink-red knots: the inner filament, the trail of the jet from the galaxy’s black hole. The bright orange star at the right, HD 116466, the white one below the galaxy, and the pair at the left are all in our own Galaxy. At Centaurus A’s distance the frame is about 91,000 light-years across and 72,000 high, and each pixel covers about 21 light-years.

Centaurus A

Centaurus A, NGC 5128, is a giant galaxy, usually called a peculiar elliptical, and the nearest radio galaxy to us. Several ways of measuring its distance, from Cepheids to the brightest red giants among its stars, average about 12.5 million light-years; the red giants alone give 11.7 million in a recent catalogue, the Cepheids 11.2. You’ll read anything from 10 to 13 million. Its bright body is about 90,000 light-years across, but its faint halo of stars reaches out to about 150 kiloparsecs, nearly half a million light-years. Within 300,000 light-years of its centre it weighs about a trillion times the Sun.

Most of its stars are about 12 billion years old, but a fifth to a third formed only 2 to 4 billion years ago, and simulations show a single great merger of two galaxies, about 2 billion years ago, can explain them, its shape, and the faint shells of stars round it, which don’t show here. The disc of gas and dust across its middle seems to have settled more recently, within the last few hundred million years, after a gas-rich galaxy fell in.

The dust lane

The dust lane is a warped disc of gas, dust, and young stars, seen nearly edge on. Here it’s about 12 kiloparsecs long, about 40,000 light-years, and only about 200 parsecs thick, holding about 16 million times the Sun’s mass of dust. It’s so opaque that a supernova in it in 2016 was dimmed by more than six magnitudes. The pink knots along it are clouds of glowing hydrogen round newborn stars, and the brightest, Sersic 13, just above the nucleus, is the largest such cloud in the galaxy, lit by a pair of young clusters, each at least as luminous as R136, the cluster at the heart of the Tarantula Nebula. At the lane’s right-hand end, blue light from young stars lines its upper edge.

Two supernovae have been seen in the lane: SN 1986G, found by the amateur Robert Evans in New South Wales on 3 May 1986, a type Ia in the dust left of the nucleus; and SN 2016adj, just right of it, found by Peter Marples and Greg Bock in February 2016.

The jet

At the galaxy’s heart is a black hole of about 55 million times the Sun’s mass, and it fires a jet. In 2021 the Event Horizon Telescope published an image of the jet’s very base, sixteen times sharper than any before; the whole of it spans less than a thousandth of one pixel of this picture. The jet itself shines in X-rays and radio waves, not visible light: it runs up and to the left, north-east, for at least 4 arcminutes, about 15,000 light-years, into a pair of radio lobes about 5 kiloparsecs each side of the nucleus, and beyond them the galaxy’s radio glow spreads into giant lobes about 8 degrees long on the sky, fifteen full Moons.

None of that shows here. What does is the inner filament, a chain of pink-red knots of glowing hydrogen up and to the left, along the jet’s line, 7.6 to 9.5 arcminutes from the nucleus, about 27,000 to 34,000 light-years out. It’s about 2 minutes of arc long, about 7,000 light-years. At its tip, the lower right end, are stars only 1 to 4 million years old: the gas here is forming stars, and something, perhaps radiation from the nucleus beamed along the jet, perhaps shocks driven by it, lights it; astronomers still argue which. The star-like point just above the chain is HGHH92-C7, not a star but one of the galaxy’s globular clusters, about 7.8 million times the Sun’s mass. A second filament, the outer one, lies just beyond the top edge.

Globular clusters

About 670 of Centaurus A’s globular clusters are catalogued within this frame, and at this distance they look like stars. Two of the most massive are here: HCH99-18, just up and to the left of the nucleus, the most massive known in the galaxy, about 14 million times the Sun’s mass, ten times more massive than the biggest in our neighbourhood of galaxies; and HGHH92-C23, further out, up and to the left.

History

James Dunlop found it on 29 April 1826, from his house in Parramatta, New South Wales, with a reflector he’d made himself; misled by the dark band, he thought it two nebulae side by side. John Herschel, at the Cape in 1834, drew it with “a faint line in the dark lane”. In 1947 and 1948 the radio astronomers John Bolton, Gordon Stanley, and Bruce Slee picked up a strong radio source in Centaurus from the cliffs at Dover Heights in Sydney, and in 1948 Bolton matched it to NGC 5128. Walter Baade called the galaxy “a freak”.

Far beyond

Behind Centaurus A, seen through its halo, are galaxies far further away. The farthest with a measured spectrum, AAT109567, a faint orange point near the top right, is a galaxy whose light set out about 2.7 billion years ago; HH004, a compact orange smudge at the top edge, is about 1.55 billion. The farthest thing that shows is probably a faint bluish-white point near the top left, a quasar and an X-ray source whose light set out about 9.3 billion years ago, if the redshift from Gaia’s low-resolution spectrum is right. Gaia’s catalogue also lists “quasars” here as far as redshift 6, but they’re star clusters and star-forming knots in Centaurus A itself that fooled its classifier.

In front

The brightest star, HD 116466, at the right, is an orange giant about 2,090 light-years away. The white star below the galaxy, HD 116647, is about 1,530; and the pair at the left, two matching white stars 5 arcseconds apart, is a true double, about 515 light-years away, first measured by John Herschel in 1837. CD−42 8389, on the galaxy’s glow just below the nucleus, is a star in front of it, about 1,100 light-years away.

The colours

The colours are natural: red, green, and blue filters give the colours, and a fourth, luminance, which passes all visible light, the detail. Hydrogen’s light is added from a filter that passes only its red line: that filter also passes some starlight, so a scaled broadband image is subtracted from it to leave the hydrogen alone (continuum subtraction), which brings out the filament and the pink knots. The galaxy’s old stars are golden, its young ones along the dust lane blue.

The picture

T72 is a PlaneWave CDK20, a 0.51-metre reflector, with an FLI ML16200 CCD camera, at Deep Sky Chile in the Río Hurtado Valley. The picture is 6 hours 40 minutes through luminance, 1 hour 10 minutes each through red, green, and blue, and 40 minutes through hydrogen-alpha: 10 hours 50 minutes in all. It’s 4,239 by 3,342 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
