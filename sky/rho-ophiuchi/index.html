The Rho Ophiuchi Clouds (IC 4604), Nyx

Rho Ophiuchi

Coloured dust round Antares, and four globular clusters beyond it

The Rho Ophiuchi clouds, about 450 light-years away, one of the two nearest places where stars are being born, beside the red supergiant Antares. Their dust glows blue, yellow, and red, depending on the stars that light it, and through it, much further away, are four globular clusters, the nearest of them only 6,000 light-years off.

The light in this picture spans a long stretch of history. The clouds’ light left around 1570, about when Tycho Brahe’s supernova appeared in 1572, and Antares’s about a century earlier. M4’s set out about 6,000 years ago, when farming was reaching Britain. NGC 6144’s left at the height of the last ice age, and M80’s about 33,700 years ago, while people were painting the walls of the Chauvet cave in France. The two galaxies by M80 sent their light about 350 million years ago, before the dinosaurs, when four-legged animals were spreading across the land and the great coal forests were growing.

This picture is four frames from iTelescope’s T8, a wide-field refractor at Siding Spring in Australia, joined into one, taken in April 2023. It covers about 6 degrees of sky each way, twelve full Moons across. It’s in ordinary colour, from red, green, and blue filters and a clear one, 2 hours 10 minutes through each. The clouds have their own gallery.

What’s in the frame

The picture is upside down by the usual convention: north is at the bottom and east is to the right. At the clouds’ distance the frame is about 50 light-years across, and each pixel covers about 490 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

Most of the frame is in Scorpius: Antares, the brightest star in it, is the scorpion’s heart. Ophiuchus, the serpent-bearer, has only the lower right-hand quarter, but that’s where the star ρ Ophiuchi and the darkest cloud are. The constellation boundary runs across the picture just above the dark cloud.

From London it’s a struggle. Antares never gets more than about 12 degrees above the southern horizon, and the region is best placed at the end of May, when the sky over London never gets fully dark. From Siding Spring it passes almost overhead.

The Rho Ophiuchi clouds

The dark, tangled cloud below the middle of the picture is the main part of the Rho Ophiuchi cloud complex, catalogued as Lynds 1688. Radio telescopes measuring the parallax of young stars inside it put it at about 450 light-years, and Gaia agrees. Together with the Taurus clouds, at much the same distance, it’s one of the two nearest places where stars are forming now, and it holds the richest cluster of newborn stars within about a thousand light-years: more than 300 young stars, most of them hidden behind the dust. The clouds hold a few thousand times the Sun’s mass of gas and dust.

They aren’t alone. Antares, σ and τ Scorpii, and ρ Ophiuchi itself all belong to Upper Scorpius, part of the Scorpius-Centaurus association, the nearest group of young massive stars to the Sun. Upper Scorpius is somewhere between 5 and 11 million years old, depending on which study you believe, and its winds and supernovae have blown a bubble whose edge has reached the clouds. That pressure may well have helped set off the star formation going on in them now. The clouds also seem to have been seeded with radioactive aluminium from those supernovae, much as the young Solar System was. And they sit on the wall of the Local Bubble, a larger cavity round the Sun blown by about 15 supernovae over the last 14 million years; nearly all the star-forming clouds near us lie on its surface.

Why so many colours

Each glow here is the colour it is because of the star lighting it. Only the hottest stars give out enough ultraviolet to strip hydrogen of its electron, and hydrogen that’s been stripped glows red as it recombines. Cooler stars’ light is just scattered by the dust round them, and dust scatters blue light more than red, as the daytime sky does, so a hot blue-white star’s dust glows blue. A red star’s dust glows too, but yellow.

The picture shows all of it. The two hottest stars in the frame, σ and τ Scorpii, have red glows. The slightly cooler blue-white stars, ρ Ophiuchi and 22 Scorpii, have blue ones. And Antares, a cool red supergiant, sits in a vast yellow one. Where the dust is thick enough, it blocks everything behind it and is black; where it’s thinner, it dims and reddens the light behind it and comes out brown.

Rho Ophiuchi and the blue nebula

Near the bottom, a little left of centre, the blue-white star in the blue glow is ρ Ophiuchi, two hot stars only 3 arcseconds apart, far too close to separate here, with two fainter companions just below and just left of it in the glare. The blue glow round it is the Rho Ophiuchi Nebula, IC 4604, dust at the edge of the cloud reflecting their light. The dark lanes across it are denser dust in front. E. E. Barnard found it, and IC 4603 and IC 4605, in the early 1880s.

The dark cloud and its hidden cluster

Above ρ Ophiuchi, the darkest ground in the picture is the main cloud. Visible light can’t get out of it, but infrared telescopes see a cluster of more than 300 young stars inside, their median age only about 300,000 years. At its left-hand end is the white glow of IC 4603, round a hot star sitting so deep in the dust that its own light is dimmed and reddened.

Just right of IC 4603, a small fan of light opens downwards and to the right from a faint orange star. That’s the light of the young star S1 escaping from the cloud, and in July 2023, three months after these frames, the James Webb Space Telescope’s first-anniversary picture looked at this very patch, a few arcminutes across, and found about 50 young stars in it.

From the main cloud, long brown streamers of dust run off to the right, to the edge of the frame, a little further away than the cloud itself. Hidden inside the first of them is IRAS 16293−2422, a pair of stars still forming, where in 2012 radio telescopes found the simplest sugar, glycolaldehyde, round a young star like the Sun. And beside a star on the cloud’s edge, the small, sharp blue streak is DG 143, one of the smallest nebulae in the picture.

22 Scorpii

Right of centre, between the dark cloud and Antares, the blue-white star in a smaller blue glow is 22 Scorpii, about 410 light-years away, a little in front of the cloud. Its nebula is IC 4605, the same blue reflection as round ρ Ophiuchi.

Antares

Above the middle, the brightest star in the picture is Antares, a red supergiant about 700 times the Sun’s diameter: put in the Sun’s place, its surface would reach beyond the orbit of Mars. It’s about 12 times the Sun’s mass, and near the end of its life: it’s expected to explode as a supernova. Its distance is uncertain, about 550 light-years give or take a hundred, because it’s too bright for Gaia to measure.

The great yellow glow round it is the Antares Nebula, dust lit by Antares, yellow because the light it reflects is a red star’s. It’s often listed as IC 4606, but the catalogue’s description doesn’t fit it, and that number may really belong to the globular cluster NGC 6144, inside the glow. Antares has a hot blue companion, Antares B, only 2.7 arcseconds away and lost in the glare here. It lights a tiny pocket of Antares’s own wind, which professional papers call the Antares Nebula too.

Sigma Scorpii and its red nebula

Left of centre, the blue-white star in a red cloud is σ Scorpii, a hot multiple star whose brightest member pulsates. Its ultraviolet makes the hydrogen round it glow red: that’s Sharpless 2-9, brightest along its left-hand edge, where the starlight is eating into denser clouds. Below it, the yellowish star with no nebula is ο Scorpii, a white giant star made yellow by the dust its light comes through.

Tau Scorpii and the glow across the top

At the top right, the bright blue-white star is τ Scorpii, the hottest star in the picture. It’s a puzzle: its magnetic field is unusually tangled for a hot star, and it looks younger than the stars round it; one explanation, argued in 2019, is that it’s two stars that merged. The faint red glow across the top of the picture, round it and towards Antares, is RCW 129, a region of glowing hydrogen about 3 degrees across. Its catalogue says only that it contains τ Scorpii, which is probably the star lighting it.

Seen through that red glow, left of τ Scorpii, are two galaxies, ESO 452-7, a spiral seen nearly edge on, and ESO 452-5, about 190 million light-years away.

Four globular clusters

Behind the clouds and their stars, much further away, are four globular clusters: ancient, dense balls of stars that orbit the centre of the Galaxy.

The big one left of Antares is M4, the nearest globular cluster to the Sun, about 6,000 light-years away and about 12 billion years old, with about 90,000 times the Sun’s mass in stars. Its light comes through the edge of the Ophiuchus dust, which tints it. Philippe Loys de Chéseaux found it in 1745 or 1746, and Charles Messier, cataloguing it in 1764, was the first to see a globular cluster as stars. Hubble found its first extensive sequence of white dwarfs in 1995, the cooled cores of dead stars, and they’ve been used to date it. Near its centre is PSR B1620−26, a pulsar with a white dwarf partner and a planet of about two and a half times Jupiter’s mass, which NASA announced in 2003 as the oldest planet known.

Below and left of Antares, faint inside its yellow glow, is NGC 6144, about 26,600 light-years away. William Herschel found it in 1784. In the bottom left corner, the compact bright ball is M80, about 33,700 light-years away, the densest of the four. Messier found it in 1781, and in May 1860 a nova in it, T Scorpii, briefly outshone the whole cluster.

The fourth is easy to miss: in the top right corner, the faint knot of stars right of τ Scorpii is ESO 452-SC11, about 24,000 light-years away in the Galaxy’s bulge, only about 7,000 times the Sun’s mass. It’s the lowest-mass cluster yet found with signs of more than one generation of stars.

And beside M80, the two tiny smudges are galaxies, IC 4596 and IC 4600, about 350 million light-years away, the furthest things identified in the picture.

The picture

T8 is a Takahashi FSQ-106ED, a 106 mm refractor built for photography, with a monochrome CCD camera, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. Each of its frames is about 4 degrees square, so this is four of them, two by two, overlapping. The picture is from 2 hours 10 minutes through each of four filters, luminance, red, green, and blue, across the four panels: 8 hours and 40 minutes in all. The red, green, and blue frames give the colours; the luminance filter, which passes all visible light, adds detail. Joined, it’s 6,347 by 6,623 pixels, each covering 3.5 arcseconds. I processed it in PixInsight and finished it in Photoshop.
