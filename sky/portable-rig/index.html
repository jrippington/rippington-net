The telescopes, Optica

Portable DSLR rig

A Nikon D850 on a Star Adventurer GTi, for trips

April 2024, 3 min read

On this page

  1. The camera and the mount
  2. The lenses
  3. In numbers

My portable rig is a Nikon D850 and a set of lenses on a Sky-Watcher Star Adventurer GTi, a small tracking mount, on the carbon-fibre tripod from my 150 mm Newtonian. Cal and I travel together all over the world, and this is the rig that comes with us: it all goes in a suitcase, and the mount runs on batteries, so there are no power packs to worry about on flights.

The camera and the mount

The camera is a Nikon D850, a full-frame DSLR with a sensor of 8256 by 5504 pixels (45.7 megapixels), each 4.35 µm. It isn’t only for the sky: it’s the camera I use for a lot of my landscape and wildlife photography, along with Cal’s D500. It’s unmodified, so the filter in front of its sensor blocks much of the deep red light of hydrogen-alpha, which is what makes emission nebulae glow; a modified camera, with that filter replaced, records far more of it, and I’d like one some day.

On the rig, the camera rides on a Sky-Watcher Star Adventurer GTi, a small equatorial mount that turns with the sky, so stars stay still in the frame through a long exposure, and that can find targets by itself (GoTo). A counterweight on its bar balances the camera and lens. It all stands on the carbon-fibre tripod I also use for the 150 mm Newtonian.

A tracking mount only follows the sky properly if its axis is lined up with the Earth’s, pointing at the celestial pole; that’s polar alignment, usually done by sighting the pole star at night. I do it with the PS Align Pro app on my iPhone, held in a custom-built adapter, which works in daylight and twilight. It’s not as precise as aligning on the stars, but it’s fine for the focal lengths I typically use, where a small error doesn’t show.

The lenses

I use a variety of lenses with it, notably a Sigma 35 mm f/1.4 and a Nikon 20 mm f/1.8: fast, wide lenses, the kind that suit the Milky Way and wide star fields. On the D850 the 20 mm takes in 84° by 62° of sky, and the 35 mm 54° by 38°.

In the photograph at the top it carries a Sigma 150-600 mm zoom with a homemade solar filter, ready for the total solar eclipse of August 2026 in Soria, Spain, which will have an article of its own. At 600 mm the frame is 3.4° by 2.3°, plenty of room round the Sun, which is about half a degree across.

Warning

Never point a camera or lens at the Sun without a solar filter on the front. It will fry the camera, and if you look through the viewfinder it will blind you instantly. The filter comes off only during totality itself, and goes back on before the Sun begins to reappear.
