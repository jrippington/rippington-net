Aristarchus

A bright young crater in the Ocean of Storms

Aristarchus, one of the brightest places on the Moon, on a dark plateau of volcanic glass with the Moon's largest winding valley beside it, and the Ocean of Storms south to Gassendi: Kepler's rays, the domes of the Marius Hills at sunrise, the place Surveyor 1 landed, and night along the bottom. From my garden in Oxfordshire, through an infrared filter.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, without its corrector, at its full 1,176 mm, with a small ZWO ASI678MC planetary camera and an infrared filter, on the night of 18 June 2024. It’s a strip down the western side of the face we see, about 1,750 km from end to end, from the bright crater Aristarchus to Gassendi, which has a picture of its own, taken the same night. The Moon has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

The picture is on its side: lunar north is to the left, so east, towards the Sea of Crises, is up, south is to the right and west is down. Aristarchus is the small, brilliant crater towards the left, on the dark, rumpled block of the Aristarchus plateau, with Herodotus just below it and the winding valley of Vallis Schröteri to their left. Above them are the hills of Montes Harbinger. Above the middle, Kepler throws bright rays across the plain, with Encke to its right. Below the middle, Marius sits among the low domes of the Marius Hills, with Reiner further right, at the edge of night. Most of the frame is the grey lava of Oceanus Procellarum, the Ocean of Storms. Towards the right are the place Surveyor 1 landed, the drowned crater Letronne, Mons Hansteen below it, and Gassendi, near the right edge. Along the bottom it’s still night, and the very corner at the bottom left is beyond the Moon’s edge. Each pixel covers about 670 metres.

The Sun was rising over these plains, in the lunar east, which here is at the top, so shadows fall downwards, and inside each crater the shadow lies along the top of its floor. The line of sunrise curves along the bottom of the frame; just above it only rims and hilltops catch the light. The Sun was 19 degrees above Kepler, 9 above Aristarchus, and over the Marius Hills it had only just risen. From Oxfordshire the Moon was low, never more than 17 degrees up, in the twilight of a midsummer night, and it was 90 per cent lit, three days before full.

Aristarchus

Aristarchus is only 40 km across, but it’s one of the brightest places on the Moon. Partly that’s because it’s young, perhaps 280 million years old by one count of the small craters on its melted floor, so its rock hasn’t yet been dulled by space weathering, the slow darkening of anything left exposed to the solar wind and the rain of tiny meteorites. And partly it’s because it dug down into pale rock: the Clementine spacecraft found two patches in its middle with the signature of nearly pure anorthosite, the light, crystalline rock of the Moon’s first crust. It’s about 3 km deep (sources differ), and here the shadow of its eastern wall covers the upper half of its floor, ending at its sunlit central peak.

Aristarchus also has a long record of odd sightings. On 19 April 1787 William Herschel saw three glowing spots in the dark part of the Moon, and wrote them up as “three volcanos”. He didn’t say where they were, but later writers usually take the brightest to have been Aristarchus, which is bright enough to stand out in earthshine, the faint light of the Earth on the Moon’s night side. Aristarchus and its plateau have more reports of “transient lunar phenomena”, brief glows and colours, than anywhere else on the Moon, by one tally, though many are probably tricks of the telescope or the eye. Something real did turn up in 1971. Apollo 15, passing overhead, detected radon gas over Aristarchus, and since radon decays within days, it must have seeped out of the ground only recently.

The plateau and its valley

Aristarchus sits on the south-eastern corner of the Aristarchus plateau, a raised block of crust about 200 km across, tilted so that its south-eastern edge stands more than 2 km above the plain. It was probably lifted and cracked by the impact that made the Imbrium basin. It looks darker than the lava round it because it’s blanketed in volcanic glass: beads of molten rock sprayed out by fire fountains, which in the Moon’s low gravity, with no air to slow them, flew far enough to cover the whole plateau rather than piling up into cones.

Across it winds Vallis Schröteri, Schröter’s Valley, the largest of the Moon’s sinuous rilles, the winding channels left by lava. It’s about 160 km long (sources give anything from 140 to 185) and up to 11 km wide. It begins just north of Herodotus, at a wide pit called the Cobra Head, thought to be the vent: a flood of very fluid lava poured from it and cut or built the channel. In the picture it runs left from beside Herodotus, then turns and heads down. Herodotus itself, 36 km across, has a smooth, dark floor, because lava flooded it after it formed.

Above the plateau, Montes Harbinger, the harbingers, were named because their peaks catch the sunrise before Aristarchus does; they’re east of it, above it here. Between them and Aristarchus is Prinz, a crater whose rim survives only in part.

Kepler and the Ocean of Storms

Kepler, 30 km across, is another young crater, and its rays, pale streaks of rock splashed out by the impact, fan across the plain for about 300 km. They’re the brightest streaks in the picture. Encke, beside it, is about the same size but low and shallow. Lunar Prospector found radon seeping out near Kepler too.

All of this is in Oceanus Procellarum, the Ocean of Storms, the largest of the Moon’s dark plains, about 2,600 km across and the only one called an ocean. Most of the seas fill the basins of giant impacts, but this one’s edge is different: the GRAIL spacecraft’s maps of the Moon’s gravity show a rough rectangle of ancient rift valleys round it, filled with lava. And some of the youngest lava on the Moon is here. Counting craters on the plains south of Aristarchus, between its rays, gives ages from about 3.6 billion years down to about a billion. Chang’e 5 brought back samples from the north of the Ocean, beyond the left edge of this picture, and at about 2 billion years old they’re the youngest lunar lava yet dated in a laboratory.

Marius and its hills

Marius, 40 km across, has a floor flooded with lava, and round it are the Marius Hills, a volcanic field of a few hundred low domes and cones spread over about 200 by 250 km. On this night the Sun was just rising over them, so every dome throws a shadow; domes this low only show at all when the Sun is low. In 2009 Japan’s Kaguya spacecraft found a pit among them, 65 metres across, probably a skylight into a lava tube. Here it would be a tenth of a pixel.

Further right, only the eastern rim of Reiner is in sunlight. Just below it, still in the night, is Reiner Gamma, a bright swirl on the dark plain. Swirls like it sit on patches of magnetised crust, which probably shield the ground from the solar wind, so it stays pale. NASA’s Lunar Vertex mission plans to land there.

Towards Gassendi

Right of the middle, a faint ring of low ridges about 110 km across is Flamsteed P, a crater almost buried by the lava. Inside it, north of the small crater Flamsteed, Surveyor 1 landed on 2 June 1966, the first American spacecraft to land softly on the Moon; its pictures helped prepare for Apollo. Letronne, 118 km across, has been drowned, all but its southern wall, the hills to its right. Below it, between the craters Hansteen and Billy, is Mons Hansteen, the Arrowhead, a dome of pale, silica-rich lava, far stickier than the runny basalt of the seas. And at the right is Gassendi, 111 km across, with its cracked floor and central peaks, on the northern shore of the Sea of Moisture; it has its own page.

Names

Aristarchus of Samos, who lived from about 310 to 230 BC, is the first person known to have put the Sun at the centre, with the Earth turning once a day and going round it once a year. That book is lost, and we know of it only from Archimedes. His one surviving work tries to measure how much farther away the Sun is than the Moon, from the angle between them at half Moon: he got 18 to 20 times, when it’s really about 400, but the method was sound. Seleucus of Seleucia, who argued for the Sun at the centre about a century later, has a crater in this frame too, across the line of sunrise from Aristarchus, still in the night.

The story goes that Giovanni Battista Riccioli, whose map of 1651 gave the Moon most of its names, and who didn’t accept that the Earth goes round the Sun, put Copernicus, Kepler and Aristarchus out in the Ocean of Storms. Marius is named for Simon Marius, who saw Jupiter’s four big moons at about the same time as Galileo and gave them the names they still have, Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto; Galileo accused him of copying, and a Dutch enquiry in 1903 cleared him. Herodotus is named for the Greek historian.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, used without its corrector at its own focal length of 1,176 mm, f/4.7. The camera is a ZWO ASI678MC, a small planetary camera that records video, and in front of it was an infrared filter, to keep the atmosphere a bit more still. I captured it in SharpCap, stacked the sharpest frames in AutoStakkert, sharpened them with wavelets, and finished it in Photoshop. It’s in shades of grey. It’s 2,484 by 1,094 pixels.
