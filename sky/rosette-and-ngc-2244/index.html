The Rosette and NGC 2244

A ring of gas round a young cluster

The Rosette Nebula, a ring of glowing gas about 110 light-years across and about 4,700 light-years away in Monoceros, round the young cluster NGC 2244, whose hottest stars light it and have blown a hollow in its middle: with dark trunks and tiny globules being eaten away round its edge. Two nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light in this picture left the Rosette about 4,700 years ago, around 2,700 BC: after the first ditch and bank were dug at Stonehenge, around 3,000 BC, and before its great stones were raised, around 2,500 BC.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over two nights in January 2026. It covers 1.3 degrees of sky by 0.9, about two and a half full Moons by two, in Monoceros. It’s from two filters that each pass two narrow bands of light, one hydrogen’s red line and oxygen’s green-blue, the other sulphur’s red and hydrogen’s blue, and the stars are made from the same frames rather than from separate exposures. The Rosette has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is up and east is to the left. The Rosette Nebula fills the frame, its ring trimmed a little at the top and bottom, and in its hollow middle is the young cluster NGC 2244. The brightest star, just left of the middle and below it, is 12 Monocerotis, which isn’t part of the cluster at all. The dark trunks and globules at the upper right are the north-western trunks, and the darker, dustier lower left looks towards the giant molecular cloud the Rosette is pressing into. At the Rosette’s distance, about 4,700 light-years, the frame is about 110 light-years across and 75 high, and each pixel covers about 1,270 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 42 times Neptune’s distance.

It’s inside the Winter Triangle, about a third of the way from Betelgeuse to Procyon and 2 degrees south of the line between them. From Oxfordshire it climbs to about 43 degrees in the south, crossing the meridian just before midnight at the start of January, and it’s above 30 degrees for about six hours of each night. The cluster is a naked-eye smudge from a dark site and easy in binoculars; the nebula is faint to the eye.

The Rosette

The Rosette is a cloud of hydrogen lit by the cluster’s hottest stars, about 10,000 times the Sun’s mass of glowing gas. We see it as a ring, a thick shell round a hollow. Gaia’s measurements of the cluster’s stars put it about 4,700 light-years away; you’ll often read 5,200, which comes from Gaia’s earlier data and older methods. At that distance the bright ring is about 110 light-years across, not the 130 you’ll also read.

Its NGC numbers tell how slowly it was seen. John Flamsteed recorded the cluster in 1690, William Herschel catalogued it in 1784, and his son John recorded it again in 1830 with its position a little out, so it got a second number, NGC 2239; neither Herschel noticed the nebula. The first to see any of the nebula was Albert Marth, from Malta in 1864 with William Lassell’s 48-inch reflector: a small bright knot on the west side, NGC 2238. Lewis Swift called attention to the large western part, NGC 2237, publishing it in 1884 after Edward Barnard came across it while sweeping for comets, and found another patch on the north-east rim, NGC 2246, in 1886. Only Barnard’s photographs, in the early 1890s, showed the whole ring. Today the nebula usually goes by NGC 2237, and the cluster by NGC 2244.

The hollow

The cluster’s hottest stars have cleared the middle with their winds and light, and the hollow is filled with gas at millions of degrees, seen in X-rays. But it’s a puzzle. The hollow is too small for the cluster’s age: by its size it would be less than 110,000 years old, while the cluster is about 2 million. In 2018 astronomers at Leeds suggested why: if the cloud the cluster formed in was a thin sheet rather than a ball, the winds would escape out of its sides instead of inflating a big bubble. And there’s far less hot gas in the hollow than the winds should have made; the rest seems to have leaked out.

NGC 2244

NGC 2244 is about 2 million years old, with something like 1,500 to 3,000 stars and about 1,000 times the Sun’s mass. Most of the ultraviolet that lights the Rosette comes from two stars of about 50 times the Sun’s mass, about 11 light-years apart: HD 46150, above the middle and right, the brightest of its hot stars, and HD 46223, below and left, the hottest, which seems to have been thrown out of the cluster about 1.7 million years ago. Their winds blow at about 2,800 kilometres a second, fast enough to cover the distance from the Earth to the Sun in under 15 hours. And the cluster is flying apart: Gaia shows its stars moving outward, faster the further out they are, so it may never have been held together by its own gravity.

Left of them, the bright orange star is 12 Monocerotis, the brightest star in the picture. It isn’t a member. It’s an orange giant about 460 light-years away, ten times nearer, though the old catalogues described the cluster by it, “12 Mon”; its warm colour among the cluster’s blue-white stars is the giveaway. Near it, V578 Monocerotis is a pair of hot stars that eclipse each other every 2.4 days, which in 2000 gave the first direct measurement of the cluster’s age and distance from one of its own members.

Trunks, globules, and globulettes

At the upper right, a chain of dark trunks and globules runs round the inside of the ring, their edges lit by the cluster. George Herbig drew attention to the small dark globules here in 1974 and suggested they’d broken off the shell. Many are tiny. In 2007 astronomers catalogued 145 globulettes in the Rosette, clouds a few thousand times the Earth–Sun distance across, most of them with less than 13 times Jupiter’s mass. They’re moving outwards with the shell at about 22 kilometres a second, about 80,000 kilometres an hour, and some may collapse into brown dwarfs or planets drifting free of any star. The biggest, marked here, is about two thirds of the Sun’s mass, a dark blob near the top.

Lower down the right-hand side, the tangle of dark clouds round NGC 2238 is what Hubble photographed for its 35th anniversary in April 2025. In it, the Seahorse is a globule with a young star forming inside, firing an outflow.

The eastern side

On the left, a long pillar points in from the east towards the cluster. A small group of young stars at the tip of a pillar on that side is moving with it, away from the cluster, the best case in the Rosette for stars whose birth its light set off; this is probably that pillar. Lower down, the dark, dusty lower left is the Monoceros Ridge, where the nebula presses into the Rosette Molecular Cloud, a cloud of more than 100,000 times the Sun’s mass beyond the lower left corner, with clusters of young stars of its own. Whether the nebula set off the star formation there is still argued.

The orange star at the left-hand edge, HD 46612, is another giant in the foreground, about 900 light-years away.

The colours

The colours are the Hubble palette: sulphur red, hydrogen’s red line green, and oxygen blue, with hydrogen’s blue line blended in with the oxygen. So the hollow and the inner ring are lavender-blue, where the cluster’s hardest light makes oxygen glow, and the outer edges are gold and orange, where the gas meets the cold cloud and sulphur and hydrogen glow together. The stars were made from the same narrowband frames, so their colours aren’t their own.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. In front of it, two Antlia dual-band filters each pass two bands 5 nm wide: one hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III, the other sulphur II and hydrogen-beta. The picture is 2 hours 21 minutes through the first and 2 hours 10 minutes through the second, over two nights in January 2026. It’s 5,486 by 3,760 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, Affinity Photo, and Photoshop.
