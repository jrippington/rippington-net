M13 and NGC 6207

The Great Globular Cluster, and the galaxy beside it

The Great Globular Cluster in Hercules, M13, about 24,200 light-years away, an ancient ball of stars about 484,000 times the Sun's mass and about 12 billion years old, at which a radio message was aimed in 1974; with the spiral galaxy NGC 6207 beside it, about 50 million light-years beyond, and quasars whose light set out nearly 12 billion years ago. From my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from M13 left it about 24,200 years ago, at the height of the last ice age. The ice sheet over Britain and Ireland was nearing its greatest, about a thousand years later; the sea was so low that Britain was joined to the continent, and Oxfordshire, beyond the ice, was frozen tundra or polar desert, probably with no people at all.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken in May 2024. It covers 1.3 degrees of sky by 0.9, about two and a half full Moons by two, in Hercules. It’s in natural colour, from the colour camera with no filter at all. M13 has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left, turned a little up, so east is down and west up. M13 is the great ball of stars a little right of the middle. The spiral galaxy at the lower left is NGC 6207, and the little galaxy between them is IC 4617. The bright orange star below the cluster is HD 150998, and the blue-white one right of the cluster HD 150679, both in the foreground. At M13’s distance the frame is about 555 light-years across and 390 high, and each pixel covers about 6,400 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 210 times Neptune’s distance.

From Oxfordshire, M13 crosses the meridian 75 degrees up, nearly overhead, and it barely sets. In May 2024 that was between about 3 am at the start of the month and 1 am at the end, and after about 21 May the sky never got fully dark. Edmond Halley, who came upon it by chance in 1714, wrote that it “shews it self to the naked Eye” on a clear, moonless night.

M13

M13, NGC 6205, is one of the brightest globular clusters in the northern sky: an ancient ball of stars about 484,000 times the Sun’s mass, often said to hold several hundred thousand stars. Gaia’s measurements, with other methods, put it about 24,200 light-years away; you’ll also read 25,000. Its stars are about 12 billion years old, formed when the Milky Way was young, with about a fortieth of the Sun’s share of iron. As it shows here it’s about 20 arcminutes across, about 140 light-years, and the stars resolve right into its middle, orange giants among blue-white stars burning helium in their cores.

It’s quieter than its neighbour M3 in one way: where M3 has about 240 RR Lyrae variables, M13 has only eight. Its brightest star, V11, is a red giant that varies over about 92 days. Barnard 29, the blue-white star just above and right of the centre, was once thought a young star the cluster had captured; it’s an old one, shedding its outer layers on its way to becoming a white dwarf. Nine millisecond pulsars have been found in the cluster by their radio pulses, and the Fermi telescope sees gamma rays from it. At the eyepiece, observers often see three dark lanes in the cluster, “the propeller”, first recorded at Birr Castle around 1850; it doesn’t show convincingly here.

Its history

After Halley, Charles Messier catalogued it on the night of 1 to 2 June 1764, as a round nebula without a star, with two stars near it, one above and one below; those were probably HD 150998 and HD 150679, the only bright stars that close. William Herschel first saw it in 1779, resolved it into stars in 1783, and on 16 May 1787, looking at it with his 20-foot telescope, found NGC 6207 the same night.

On 16 November 1974, at the dedication of its upgrade, the Arecibo radio telescope sent M13 a message of 1,679 bits, designed by Frank Drake and others, with Carl Sagan. At the speed of light it arrives around AD 26,200. So far it has gone about 52 light-years, a fifth of a per cent of the way, and by the time it arrives the cluster will have moved about 3 arcminutes from where we see it.

Far beyond the cluster

M13 is high above the Milky Way’s disc, so the view past it runs a long way. NGC 6207 is a spiral galaxy about 50 million light-years away by the best measurements (you’ll read 30 million, a figure with no traceable source), and about 45,000 light-years across. In January 2004 Koichi Itagaki found a supernova in it, SN 2004A, just above its centre here, and older Hubble pictures showed the star that exploded: a red supergiant of about 9 times the Sun’s mass.

IC 4617, between NGC 6207 and the cluster, looks like M13’s neighbour but is a spiral whose light left it about 513 million years ago, more than 20,000 times further away; it has an active nucleus, and E. E. Barnard found it. The background is full of galaxies like it. Z 197-2, in the top left corner, is about 430 million light-years away; LEDA 2088062, a compact orange galaxy in the upper left, about 1.4 billion, the farthest that plainly looks like a galaxy. Fainter smudges near the top and at the bottom right are the brightest galaxies of two clusters of galaxies whose light is about 4.5 and 4.7 billion years old.

Many of the faint “stars” are quasars, the bright cores of distant galaxies lit by gas falling into huge black holes: 33 of the 119 catalogued in the frame show clearly, nine of them more than 10 billion light-years away. SDSS J1641+3643, near IC 4617, is easy to see, at 10.6 billion light-years. The farthest thing that shows, SDSS J1642+3605, a faint point at the lower right, is a quasar whose light set out about 11.8 billion years ago, when the universe was about 2 billion years old; by coincidence, that’s about when M13’s own stars formed. The farthest catalogued in the frame, DESI J250.16+36.23, a quasar 12.2 billion light-years away, is too faint for my picture.

In front

HD 150998, the orange star below the cluster, is about 1,190 light-years away, and HD 150679, the blue-white one, about 450: both a long way in front of M13.

The colours

The camera’s own colours, with no filter: the cluster’s giants orange-yellow, its hot stars blue-white, NGC 6207’s arms bluish with young stars, and the distant cluster galaxies orange.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to about 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover about 0.87 arcseconds here. The picture is 3 hours 1 minute with no filter, in May 2024. It’s 5,465 by 3,845 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
