The Seagull Nebula

A gull of glowing gas, wings spread

The Seagull Nebula, IC 2177, about 3,700 light-years away on the border of Monoceros and Canis Major: a head of glowing gas round a young star, wings about 200 light-years long at the edge of a great cavity, clusters of newborn stars, and a runaway star pushing a bow shock ahead of it. Nearly eight full Moons across, from a small refractor in Australia, in the Hubble palette.

The light from the Seagull left it about 3,700 years ago, around 1,700 BC. At Stonehenge, people were digging the last of its prehistoric works, two rings of pits round the stones, and carving bronze axe-heads into the sarsens.

This picture is from iTelescope’s T8, a 106 mm refractor at Siding Spring Observatory in Australia, which I used remotely in January 2023. It covers 3.9 degrees of sky square, nearly eight full Moons across, on the border of Monoceros and Canis Major. It’s in the Hubble palette, from three filters that each pass the light of one element: hydrogen, oxygen, and sulphur. The Seagull Nebula has its own gallery, and Thor’s Helmet is just beyond the bottom right corner.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left, so east is down, west up, and south to the right. The Seagull’s head is the bright round cloud at the upper left, with a dark lane down it, and its wings are the gold and blue sweep across the upper half, from the head to a bright knot at the right-hand end. At the top of the wing, in a dark cloud, is NGC 2327, where new stars are forming, and above it at the top edge are two small round nebulae. The blue arc above the middle is a bow shock round a star that seems to be running away. Fainter gas curves down the left side and along the bottom, with a round shell at the lower left, and the bright knot in the bottom left corner, Sh2-294, is far behind it all. At the nebula’s distance, about 3,700 light-years, the frame is about 255 light-years square, and each pixel covers about 4,000 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

The plane of the Milky Way crosses the frame diagonally, from the left edge to the bottom edge, and the border between Monoceros and Canis Major runs almost straight down it: the head is in Monoceros, the wingtip in Canis Major.

The Seagull

The Seagull is three connected clouds of glowing hydrogen, catalogued by Sharpless in 1959: the head, Sh2-292; the wings, Sh2-296; and the wingtip, Sh2-297. IC 2177 is the name used for the whole bird now, though Isaac Roberts, who photographed it in 1898, meant only the head. Its common name comes from its likeness to a gull in flight; who first used it isn’t recorded.

Gaia’s measurements of its stars put it about 3,700 light-years away, between about 3,600 and 4,100. Older studies gave about 3,200, and some much less. The wings are about 3 degrees long, about 200 light-years at that distance; you may read 100.

It’s lit by the young stars of Canis Major OB1, an association of massive stars, and Canis Major R1, a group of young stars that light the dust round them, found by van den Bergh in 1966. Gaia’s measurements show the group is about 10 million years old and still forming stars.

The head

The head is about 20 arcminutes across, about 23 light-years, lit by HD 53367, the star at its heart. It’s a young massive star, about 700,000 years old, still wrapped in the gas it formed from, with a disc of glowing gas round it; a 2022 study of its orbit finds it about 12 times the Sun’s mass, with a companion of about 5 that circles it once a year. Older studies gave it 20. The dark lane is a chain of dense globules of dust, running down from just beside the star.

The wings

The wings are gas at the edge of a great cavity, about 200 light-years across. They’re lit mainly by three hot O stars: HD 54662, left of the middle, a pair of stars whose ultraviolet makes the gas round it glow blue with oxygen; HD 53975, at the right; and HD 54879. But a 2019 study, counting HD 53975 and HD 54879, found they supply at most about a third of the ionisation that’s measured, and proposed hot, X-ray-emitting gas filling the cavity for the rest.

What made the cavity has been argued since 1977, when Herbst and Assousa proposed that a supernova about 500,000 years ago had swept up a ring of gas about 3 degrees across, the wings with fainter nebulae to the north and east, and set off the stars’ formation; the fainter gas that curves down the left and along the bottom here seems to be part of it. Later studies found most of the stars too old for that, 5 to 10 million years, and winds from the stars could have done the sweeping. The 2019 study proposes three supernovae, about 6, 2, and 1 million years ago, in a cluster near the cavity’s centre, which flung out stars that are still running away from it with bow shocks ahead of them, but concludes that they played only a minor part in starting the stars’ formation. HD 54662 has long been called one of those runaways, though a 2018 study couldn’t confirm it.

FN Canis Majoris and its bow shock

FN Canis Majoris, above the middle, is the brightest star in the picture, a hot giant that pulses every three hours. The blue arc round its upper left side, the north-west, about 100 arcseconds ahead of it, is a bow shock, where its wind meets the gas it’s moving through. It seems to be running away from the centre of the cavity, perhaps flung out when a companion exploded.

Newborn stars

At the top of the wing, NGC 2327 is a small bright nebula tucked into a big dark cloud whose edge is lit by the hot stars nearby. Two clusters of very young stars are buried in it, about 1.5 million years old. William Herschel found it on 31 January 1785, the same night he found Thor’s Helmet. Round it are the stars of vdB 92, the cluster at the heart of Canis Major R1, about 4 to 7 million years old. Z Canis Majoris, the bright star just right of it, is two very young stars a tenth of an arcsecond apart, both still feeding from discs and firing jets; in 2008 it brightened by about two magnitudes, its largest outburst in 90 years. Its jet is too faint to show here.

Sh2-297, the bright knot at the right-hand end of the wing, is lit by the young star HD 53623, and the stars forming in it are about a million years old. A dark cloud presses against its west side, just above it here, and star formation seems to be spreading into it.

The two round nebulae at the top edge, Sh2-293 and Sh2-295, are each lit by a hot star at its centre; Sh2-295’s, FZ Canis Majoris, is a pair of stars that eclipse each other every 1.27 days. A 2026 study suggests Sh2-295’s expansion set off a younger generation of small stars round it.

The shell, and what’s in front and behind

The round shell at the lower left, about 12 arcminutes across, about 14 light-years, has a hot star, HD 55439, near its centre, and it’s brightest on its east and south sides, below and to the right here. It doesn’t seem to have a catalogue name.

Three star clusters William Herschel found on 10 January 1785 are in the frame, NGC 2335, NGC 2343, and NGC 2353, but they’re 100 million years or more older than the Seagull’s stars, and not part of it; NGC 2335 is about 1,500 light-years behind it. Berkeley 76, a faint knot of stars right of FN Canis Majoris, is an old cluster about 16,800 light-years away, on the far side of much of the Milky Way’s dust. K 1-8, the small round orange glow just above the head, was once catalogued as a planetary nebula, but it’s probably a small cloud of glowing gas; PHR J0705-0924, a faint green-blue smudge near the left edge, is a real one, the shell thrown off by a dying star like the Sun. W Canis Majoris, right of the middle and a little up, is a cool carbon star about 2,200 light-years away, in front.

Sh2-294, the bright knot in the bottom left corner, is a small cloud of glowing gas round a young cluster, three or four times farther away than the Seagull, between about 10,600 and 15,700 light-years.

Far beyond

Through the dust of the Milky Way’s plane, and through filters that pass only three narrow slivers of light, no galaxy shows here at all. The farthest thing catalogued in the frame is IGR J07072-1227, a galaxy with an active nucleus found by the INTEGRAL satellite in hard X-rays, whose light set out about 975 million years ago; it’s invisible.

So the farthest things that show are stars. ALS 254, a small bluish star at the lower left, is a hot star about 21,600 light-years away, about six times as far as the Seagull, out in the Galaxy’s outer disc. [M81] I-47, near the right edge, may be farther still, about 25,000 light-years, though less precisely measured. The nearest is RX J0705.1-1008, a faint orange red dwarf at the upper left, only 56 light-years away: its light left it in the late 1960s.

The colours

In the Hubble palette, sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue. Most of the Seagull is gold, hydrogen and sulphur together; it’s blue only where the hottest stars’ ultraviolet is strong enough to strip two electrons from oxygen, round HD 54662 and FN Canis Majoris.

The picture

T8 is a Takahashi FSQ-106ED, a 106 mm refractor built for photography, with an FLI MicroLine 16803 CCD camera, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. The picture is 3 hours 50 minutes through the hydrogen-alpha filter, 3 hours through oxygen III, and 3 hours 40 minutes through sulphur II: 10 hours 30 minutes in all. It’s 4,019 by 4,017 pixels, each covering 3.5 arcseconds. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
