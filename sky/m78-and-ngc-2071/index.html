M78 and NGC 2071

The cosmic bridge

M78 and NGC 2071, clouds of dust lit blue by young stars, about 1,300 light-years away in Orion B, the cloud of the Horsehead: dark lanes, the jets of stars being born, the place where McNeil's Nebula came and went, and, probably, the red edge of Barnard's Loop. About 50 hours over December and January, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from M78 left it about 1,300 years ago, around AD 700, about when the Lindisfarne Gospels were being made in Northumbria.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, about 50 hours taken between December 2024 and January 2025. It covers 1.5 degrees of sky by 1.2, about three full Moons by two, in Orion. It’s in natural colour, from a filter that passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands. M78 has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the upper left, so east is to the lower left, west to the upper right, and south to the lower right. M78 is the large blue fan of light just right of the middle, under a dark arch; left of it is the second blue nebula, NGC 2071. In the dark cloud at the right, a small patch of white-yellow light cut by red streaks is HH 24, where young stars fire jets into the cloud. The little blue comet at the left, above the middle, is DG 82, and the red glow along the left edge is probably Barnard’s Loop. At M78’s distance, about 1,300 light-years, the frame is about 35 light-years across and 27 high, and each pixel covers about 400 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 13 times Neptune’s distance.

From Oxfordshire, M78 crosses the meridian 38 degrees up in the south, around 1 am at the start of December and about 11 pm at the start of January, and it was above 20 degrees for about seven and a half hours of full darkness a night. The full Moons of 15 December and 13 January were unusually high, in Taurus and Gemini, only about 30 degrees from M78: the Moon was near its major standstill, which comes round every 18.6 years.

M78

M78, NGC 2068, is a reflection nebula: dust, lit by the light of young stars, that scatters blue light more than red. It’s the brightest of a little group of them, with NGC 2067 and NGC 2064, at the two ends of the bright band above the arch, and NGC 2071. Its light comes from a pair of hot B stars, HD 38563 A and B, only 2 arcseconds apart, a single bright star here at the top of the fan. The dark arch over it is part of LDN 1627, in the darkest class of Beverly Lynds’s catalogue of 1962. M78 is about 8 by 6 arcminutes, about 3 light-years across.

Radio measurements of its young stars, and Gaia’s, put it about 1,300 light-years away; you’ll also read 1,500 and 1,600, but 1,600 is about a fifth too far. Pierre Méchain found it in 1780, and Charles Messier catalogued it on 17 December that year. Lord Rosse listed it as a spiral nebula; O’Meara, writing in Astronomy, suggests he was seeing the arcs of dust that curl round its stars.

NGC 2071 and the cluster

NGC 2071, 14.5 arcminutes from M78, about 5.6 light-years, is another reflection nebula, lit by the star at its centre; William Herschel found it on 1 January 1786. Between the two, and round them, is one young cluster, NGC 2068/71, about 2 million years old, in which nearly four in five of the stars still have discs round them. About 4 arcminutes north of NGC 2071, hidden in the dust, is NGC 2071 IRS, a cluster seen only in the infrared, driving a pair of outflows about 15 arcminutes from end to end.

They’re in the northern part of Orion B, the cloud of dust and gas that also holds the Horsehead and the Flame, about 3 degrees away: my picture of those shows the cloud’s other end.

The jets

The dark cloud at the right is busy. HH 24, white-yellow light from a cavity in the cloud, cut by red jets, is a small group of at least seven young stars firing five jets; the group is unstable, and one member, a borderline brown dwarf, was thrown out about 5,800 years ago. Hubble photographed its jets in 2015. Round it, the pink and red knots of HH 19 to 21 and HH 25 and 26 are where other jets from young stars slam into the gas round them; the filter passes the red light of hydrogen and sulphur that they glow in. Over 60 such Herbig–Haro objects are catalogued in this frame.

McNeil’s Nebula

On 23 January 2004 Jay McNeil, an amateur in Kentucky, found a new nebula here with a 3-inch telescope he’d just bought: a young star, V1647 Orionis, had flared and lit the dust round it. It had done the same before (Evered Kreimer photographed it in 1966), and it flared again in 2008. Then it faded, and in November 2018 the British Astronomical Association’s observers reported the nebula gone. It doesn’t show here: there’s no star at its place, just a faint reddish wisp beside it.

The comet and the loop

DG 82, the blue comet-shaped nebula at the upper left, is a small reflection nebula catalogued as possibly cometary, its star at its lower right; but Gaia puts that star about 800 light-years away, well in front of the cloud, so what lights it is a puzzle. Below it, NGC 2071-North is a small yellow nebula round a young star, at the heart of another knot of the cloud where stars are forming.

The deep red glow along the left edge is probably the inner fringe of Barnard’s Loop, a great arc of glowing hydrogen about 14 degrees across round Orion, lit by its brightest stars; observers put its brightest stretch just north-east of M78, which is the way the left edge faces.

Far beyond the cloud

Through the cloud’s thinner outskirts, galaxies show. Eight catalogued quasars and active galaxies are in the frame, and all of them show as small points or smudges. The farthest, SDSS J0546+0059, a faint point near the top left corner, is a quasar whose light set out about 9.4 billion years ago, about 4.8 billion years before the Sun formed; SDSS J0545+0039, near the top, is about 7.7 billion. Two galaxies show their shapes: an inclined disc near the top, whose light is about 1.1 billion years old, and a round, soft galaxy near the top, further left, about 0.7 billion. You’ll find redshifts as high as 7 catalogued here, but they’re young stars of the cloud, and blank patches of sky, that a survey’s software mistook for quasars.

In front

The brightest star in the picture, HD 38856, at the left edge in the red glow, is a hot blue giant about 1,100 light-years away, a little in front of the cloud.

The colours

The colours are close to natural: the filter passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record. The reflection nebulae are blue with scattered starlight, the dust brown and black, the jets’ knots pink and red, and Barnard’s Loop deep red.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera. The picture is about 50 hours through an Altair TRI-RGB filter, between December 2024 and January 2025, and resampled, so that each of its pixels covers about 0.97 arcseconds. It’s 5,640 by 4,360 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
