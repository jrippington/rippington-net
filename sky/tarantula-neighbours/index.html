The Tarantula and Its Neighbours

A corner of another galaxy

The Tarantula Nebula and the Large Magellanic Cloud round it, about 162,000 light-years away, in a frame about 7,500 light-years across: bubbles and shells blown by generations of massive stars, a ridge of star-forming clouds, the wrecks of supernovae, and in the lower left the soft glow of the Cloud's bar, the light of countless old stars too faint to see one by one.

The light in this picture left the middle of the Cloud about 162,000 years ago, and the Tarantula’s side, which is nearer, about 160,000. People of our own species were living in Africa: at Pinnacle Point, on the south coast of South Africa, they were gathering shellfish and using red ochre around 164,000 years ago. Britain, as far as anyone can tell, was empty of people, and from Britain the Cloud never rises anyway.

This picture is a single frame from iTelescope’s T10, a 130 mm refractor at Siding Spring in Australia, taken in November 2022. It covers 2.7 degrees of sky each way, about five full Moons, and takes in the Tarantula Nebula and a broad patch of the Large Magellanic Cloud round it. The nebula is from three narrowband filters, which pass only the light of hydrogen, sulphur, and oxygen, 4 hours 40 minutes, 4 hours, and 4 hours 30 minutes through each; the stars’ colours are from half an hour each through red, green, and blue. The Tarantula has its own gallery, and Tarantula Wide shows its middle, the patch round its heart, in far more detail.

What’s in the frame

North is to the right and east is up, so south is to the left and west is down. The Tarantula is the big cyan cloud at the upper left. The soft, pale glow across the lower left is the Cloud’s bar, and the other nebulae are scattered over the rest: N70’s round bubble alone at the top right, a chain of them down the right-hand side, and N44 at the lower right. At the Cloud’s distance the frame is about 7,500 light-years across and about the same high, and each pixel covers about 1.8 light-years, more than 100,000 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

The Large Magellanic Cloud is about 69 degrees south of the celestial equator, so it never rises anywhere north of about 20 degrees north, which rules out all of Britain and Europe. From Siding Spring it never sets. This part of it climbs to about 53 degrees above the southern horizon at its highest, and at its lowest, below the pole, it’s still about 10 degrees up. In November 2022 it was highest at about 4 am, local time, at the start of the month, and about 2 am by the end.

The glow of the bar

The glow in the lower left isn’t gas. It’s starlight: the combined light of the Cloud’s bar, a band of stars too faint and too crowded to see one by one, whose brightest members just show as a fine grain. It’s a warm, neutral colour, unlike the gold and cyan of the glowing gas, and it fades out about halfway along the bottom of the picture. The bar has no gas of its own, so in pictures that show only glowing gas, it vanishes.

Only a corner of the bar is in the frame. It’s about 14,000 light-years long, though estimates of its length differ by up to a factor of two, and its axis crosses only the lower-left corner here; its eastern end is beyond the left-hand edge. Its centre, the densest part of it, is just inside that edge, about three quarters of the way down. Most of its stars are between about 1 and 10 billion years old. Hubble’s pictures of a field at its centre suggest it formed in a burst of star formation between 4 and 6 billion years ago, about when the Sun did, and had another between 1 and 2 billion years ago.

It’s an odd bar. In most barred galaxies the bar is at the centre, but the Cloud’s is off-centre: something like 1,300 to 2,800 light-years from the middle of its outer disc, depending on who measures it, and about 3,000 from the centre of its rotating hydrogen. It’s tilted a little to the rest of the disc, and was once thought to float in front of it. And it turns very slowly, if at all. Astronomers don’t even agree where the Cloud’s centre is: its stars put it inside this frame, near the bar, while the hydrogen’s rotation puts it just beyond the bottom edge. The Small Magellanic Cloud seems to have collided with the Large one about 150 million years ago, and that may be what knocked the bar askew.

The Tarantula

The Tarantula, 30 Doradus, is the biggest and brightest star-forming region in our Local Group of galaxies, and Tarantula Wide is its account: R136, the cluster at its heart, which holds the most massive stars known; its generations of older clusters; and the debris of its supernovae. Here the whole nebula is the cyan cloud at the upper left, with its heart the white patch inside it, and the things that page describes are small. SN 1987A, the first supernova visible to the naked eye since 1604, is a faint star in the glow below the Tarantula and a little left, and NGC 2100 is the rich cluster above it.

It isn’t at the end of the bar, where many barred galaxies gather gas and form stars. It’s more than 3,000 light-years north of the bar’s eastern half, and the bar has no gas to gather. But the Cloud’s hydrogen is piled up on this side, round the Tarantula and the clouds south of it, and two ideas compete to explain that. One is that the Cloud’s eastern side, leading as it moves through the hot gas round our Galaxy, is being squeezed. The other is that the encounter with the Small Cloud set streams of hydrogen colliding here, and that the collision triggered R136 itself. Neither is settled.

The Tarantula’s side of the Cloud is also nearer to us. The Cloud’s disc is tilted, with its north-east side nearest, so the Tarantula is about 2,000 light-years closer than the Cloud’s centre.

The molecular ridge

Left of the Tarantula, along the left-hand edge, is a row of bright nebulae, which on the sky run south from it: N158, N160, and N159, between about 1,100 and 1,900 light-years from R136. The N numbers are Karl Henize’s, from his catalogue of 1956, which listed 415 glowing nebulae in the Large Cloud. These three are the northern end of the molecular ridge, a chain of cold clouds of molecular gas that runs south from the Tarantula for thousands of light-years and holds nearly a third of the Cloud’s molecular gas. Only this end is forming massive stars. The rest, beyond the left-hand edge, is strangely quiet, apparently because its gas isn’t dense enough.

N158 is a bubble full of stars with a sharp cyan rim, and below it and to the left is a bright knot, NGC 2074, which Hubble photographed in August 2008 to mark its 100,000th orbit, in the same palette as this picture. Further left, the brightest of a group of cyan knots is the Ghost Head, NGC 2080, in N160, nicknamed for its look in Hubble’s picture of 2001, in which two hot blobs make its eyes: one lit by a single massive star that has blown a bubble round itself, the other by several stars still hidden in dust. Here the eyes merge into a single bright knot.

At the edge, N159 is still wrapped in the cloud it’s forming from. The first protostar seen in another galaxy, a star still gathering its mass, was found here in 1981. In 2015 the ALMA telescope found filaments of gas there that seem to have collided and set off the birth of a massive star. Among N159’s knots is the Papillon, a nebula that Hubble resolved in 1998 into a butterfly, papillon in French, its wings about 2 light-years apart: far too small to show here. In N159 too is LMC X-1, a black hole of about 11 times the Sun’s mass and a giant star of about 32, circling each other every 3.9 days: one of the first X-ray sources found in another galaxy.

Bubbles, superbubbles, and shells

Massive stars blow bubbles in the gas round them, first with their winds and then with their explosions, and where a whole association of them goes off together, the bubbles merge into superbubbles hundreds of light-years across. Generations of them make supergiant shells, thousands of light-years across. This frame has all three.

Two supergiant shells are in it. The long arcs of filaments across the top left are probably the rim of one, LMC 2, south-east of the Tarantula; and the dark middle of the picture, full of stars and laced with gold loops, is the inside of another, LMC 3, west of it. The right-hand edge runs along the southern rim of a third, LMC 4, whose middle is beyond the edge. Several of the superbubbles are on these rims.

The top: N70 and CAL 83

Alone in the dark at the top right, N70 is about as round as a superbubble gets, about 340 light-years across. It was blown by the winds of the stars in its middle, which are about 5 million years old, and probably by at least one recent supernova, since it’s expanding faster, and its gas is hotter, than their winds alone can explain. Its rim is unusually bright in sulphur’s light, which is why it glows gold and orange here. ESO’s Very Large Telescope photographed it in 1999.

Near the top edge, between N70 and the Tarantula, a faint teal haze round a star marks CAL 83: a white dwarf burning hydrogen on its surface as it feeds on a companion star, which gives out soft X-rays. They light a small nebula round it, the only one known round a star of its kind.

The right-hand side

Down the right-hand side, north of the Tarantula on the sky, a chain of nebulae lies along LMC 4’s rim. At the top, the bright cyan cloud crossed by dark lanes is NGC 2035, “sometimes nicknamed the Dragon’s Head”, as ESO put it when it released a picture of it in 2013. Just above it, the orange loop of filaments is DEM L241, the remnant of a massive star’s explosion. Inside it is LMC P3, a massive star and a companion that’s probably a neutron star, orbiting each other every 10.3 days; the companion is perhaps what the explosion left. LMC P3 was the first binary star found to give out gamma rays outside our Galaxy, and it’s the most luminous in gamma rays known.

Below them is the pair Hubble photographed for its 30th anniversary, in April 2020, as the “Cosmic Reef”. NGC 2014, the big gold shell of loops, was nicknamed the Brain Coral; its bubbles were blown by the winds of its cluster. NGC 2020, the blue ring above it, was blown and lit by a single Wolf-Rayet star, a massive star that’s shed its outer layers, about 15 times the Sun’s mass and about 200,000 times as bright. It’s blue here for the same reason it’s blue in Hubble’s picture: oxygen.

Cut by the right-hand edge, the young cluster NGC 2004, about 20 million years old, has only its southern half in the picture. Below it, N51D is a superbubble about 400 light-years across, filled with oxygen’s teal glow. Its stars should have put about three times as much energy into it as it now holds, and where the rest went is a puzzle.

N44

In the lower right, N44 is a superbubble round the cluster NGC 1929: a hole in the gas blown first by its stars’ winds, then widened by their supernovae, with new stars forming in its rim. Estimates of its size run from about 210 by 140 light-years, in Hubble’s picture of September 2026, to 325 by 250, in ESO’s of 2011, depending on which edge is measured. The stars inside it are about 5 million years older than those forming on its rim, and it’s expanding faster than they can explain. It’s about 5,400 light-years from the Tarantula.

On its left side, the vivid blue patch is N44C, the bluest nebula in the picture, and a puzzle. It glows in a line of helium that needs more energetic light than its star gives out. It may be lit by a hidden hot companion, or be the fossil of an X-ray source that has since switched off.

The middle

The middle of the picture is inside LMC 3. Below and left of the centre, N144 is a swirl of bubbles round the cluster LH 58, bluer than its neighbours, with three Wolf-Rayet stars in it; it’s on LMC 3’s western rim. To its right and a little lower, N138 is a smaller blue-violet nebula round another Wolf-Rayet star, Brey 29. And just below and right of the middle, N148 is a chain of bright knots. Above and right of them, the compact gold ring with a teal centre is round IRAS 05328−6827, a star of about 20 times the Sun’s mass still forming, round which ices were found in 2005: the first round a massive young star outside our Galaxy.

The bottom edge

At the bottom, the cyan arcs cut by the edge are the upper part of N119, an S-shaped nebula about 400 by 600 light-years. ESO described it in 1999 as the only nebula in the Cloud whose gas is laid out in a spiral, like a small barred galaxy. Just below the edge, out of the picture, are S Doradus, the star that gives its name to a whole class of unstable giant stars, and the centre of rotation of the Cloud’s hydrogen. At the bottom left, N120 is a loop of teal and gold, with a Wolf-Rayet star, Brey 22, and the debris of a supernova, NGC 1918.

Wrecks of supernovae

The supernova remnants here are easy to spot by their colour, since the shocks that light them make sulphur glow (see “The colours”). In the top left corner, the orange figure of eight is DEM L316: two remnants that look joined. X-rays show that they came from different kinds of explosion, one a white dwarf’s and the other a massive star’s collapse, so they’re probably not touching, just in line. The orange loop of DEM L241, beside the Dragon’s Head, is another. And near the bar’s centre, the faint blue shell is N132D, the remnant of a massive star that exploded about 2,500 years ago, as we see it; its blue is the oxygen it threw out. It still accelerates particles to such high energies that it gives out very-high-energy gamma rays.

Old clusters

Among the young clusters are a few old ones. NGC 2005, a tight ball of stars in the bar, is one of the Cloud’s ancient globular clusters, but its chemistry is unlike the others’: it’s probably the survivor of a smaller galaxy that the Cloud swallowed long ago. NGC 1916, near the bottom edge, is another old globular, whose age is hard to pin down because dust lies patchily across it. Right of the middle, the faint glow of Hodge 3 is about 2.5 to 3 billion years old, and above the Tarantula, near NGC 2100, NGC 2108 is about 0.6 to 1.25 billion: the estimates differ.

The colours

The colours are the Hubble palette: sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue. Most of the nebulae are gold, sulphur and hydrogen together, the ordinary glow of gas lit by young stars. Their cores are cyan, where hot O stars make oxygen glow as well. Pure blue marks the most energetic light, round Wolf-Rayet stars in NGC 2020, N138, and N144, and in N44C. Orange marks shocks, so supernova remnants and the rims of old shells stand out: the usual test for a remnant is that its sulphur line is at least 0.4 times as bright as its hydrogen, where in ordinary glowing gas it’s about 0.2. The bar’s glow is neither, being starlight. The stars are from separate red, green, and blue frames, so their colours are their own.

The picture

T10 is a Takahashi TOA-130, a 130 mm apochromatic refractor, with a focal reducer and an SBIG STX-16803 camera, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. Its frame is 2.77 degrees square, and this picture is a single frame. The nebula is from 4 hours 40 minutes through the Hα filter, 4 hours through SII, and 4 hours 30 minutes through OIII, 13 hours 10 minutes in all, and the stars’ colours from half an hour each through red, green, and blue. I think it was drizzled when it was stacked. Drizzling shrinks each frame’s pixels and drops them onto a finer grid, so that frames shifted by fractions of a pixel can recover detail that any one of them misses. The result is 4,249 by 4,193 pixels, each covering 2.26 arcseconds, a little finer than the camera’s own 2.44. I processed it in PixInsight and finished it in Photoshop.
