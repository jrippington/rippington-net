The Carina Nebula (NGC 3372), Nyx

Carina Nebula Core

A star that nearly destroyed itself, and the furnace around it

January 2023, 9 min read

On this page

  2. Eta Carinae
  3. The Great Eruption
  4. The Homunculus
  5. The Keyhole

The heart of one of the largest nebulae in the sky, about 7,500 light-years away in the southern constellation of Carina. Its brightest star, Eta Carinae, gives out about five million times as much energy as the Sun. In the 1840s it threw off a cloud of ten or more times the Sun's mass, the Homunculus, and all round it some sixty-five of the hottest kind of star are eating away the clouds they formed in.

The light in this picture left the nebula about 7,500 years ago, around 5,500 BC. The first farmers were spreading across central Europe then, along the Danube and the Rhine, and Britain, an island by then, was still home only to hunter-gatherers. So when Herschel watched Eta Carinae outshine Rigel in 1837, he was watching something that had happened about seven and a half thousand years earlier. Eta Carinae is expected to end as a supernova. If it already has, at any time in the last 7,500 years, the light hasn’t reached us yet.

This picture is a patch of sky 26 by 21 arcminutes at the nebula’s core, taken with iTelescope’s T72, a 20-inch reflector in Chile, in early January 2023. The stars and the Homunculus come from red, green, and blue frames, and the nebula from three narrowband filters, which pass only the light of sulphur, hydrogen, and oxygen. It’s one of a series of pictures of the Carina Nebula, gathered in its gallery, and my wide-field mosaic of the region shows the whole nebula among its neighbours.

What’s in the frame

The nebula is about 60 degrees south of the celestial equator, so from London it never rises. From the telescope’s site in Chile, at 30.5° south, it climbs to about 60 degrees above the southern horizon.

The frame is smaller than the full Moon, which is about 31 arcminutes across, but the nebula is far enough away that this small patch of sky is about 57 light-years wide and 46 high. Each pixel covers 0.36 arcseconds, and at 7,500 light-years that’s about 830 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun. Neptune’s orbit is 60 times that distance across, so the whole Solar System, out to Neptune, would fit across one pixel about fourteen times over.

Eta Carinae

The bright star with the spikes, left of centre, is Eta Carinae, and it’s two stars. The larger is about 100 times the Sun’s mass, though it was probably born with 150 or more and has since lost at least 30. It gives out about five million times as much energy as the Sun, which makes it one of the most luminous stars known in our galaxy. Its companion is somewhere between 30 and 80 times the Sun’s mass. They go round each other every 5.54 years, on an orbit so stretched that at their closest they’re about as far apart as Mars is from the Sun, and at their furthest about as far apart as Neptune.

Both stars blow their outer layers away in winds, and where the two winds meet, between the stars, the gas is heated to around 100 million degrees and shines in X-rays. Each time the companion swings round at its closest, the X-rays and the star’s spectrum change abruptly, and it was those changes, repeating every five and a half years, that first suggested there were two stars.

The Great Eruption

It hasn’t always looked like this. Edmond Halley, cataloguing the southern stars from St Helena in 1677, recorded it as a star of the fourth magnitude, a fairly ordinary naked-eye star. By 1751 Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille had it at the second, and through the 1830s it held steady among the brighter stars. Then, on the evening of 16 December 1837, John Herschel, observing from the Cape of Good Hope, saw it slightly outshine Rigel, the brightest star in Orion. By March 1843 it was brighter than Canopus, second only to Sirius in the whole night sky. Sirius is 8.6 light-years away; Eta Carinae is nearly 900 times further.

Over the following decades it faded, and by 1886 it had dropped below naked-eye visibility. The star itself hadn’t dimmed: the gas it had thrown out cooled, dust condensed in it, and the dust hid the star. It brightened again slowly from about 1940, then suddenly doubled in brightness in 1998 and 1999, and it’s now back to about the fourth magnitude.

An outburst on that scale would usually mean a supernova, the end of a star. Eta Carinae survived it, and it’s the best-known example of what astronomers now call a supernova impostor.

The eruption can still be watched, after a fashion. Some of its light set off towards dust clouds further from the star, bounced off them, and is only now arriving, about 170 years late. In 2012 a team led by Armin Rest, using the 4-metre Blanco telescope in Chile, reported spectra of these light echoes, the first modern look at an eruption last seen directly in the 1840s. They showed a surface of about 5,000 kelvin, cooler than the theories of the time predicted, a result that’s still argued over.

The Homunculus

Most of what the eruption threw out is still there, in a cloud called the Homunculus. In my picture it’s the small, pinkish shape in Eta Carinae’s glare: two lobes about 50 pixels end to end. That’s 18 arcseconds, a little more than half a light-year at this distance. Enrique Gaviola named it in 1950, when the photographic plates of the time showed only its brightest part, and that looked like a stout little man. Hubble’s pictures show what it really is: two hollow lobes blown out on either side of the star along its axis, with a ragged skirt round its waist, and the south-eastern lobe tilted towards us.

It holds at least ten times the Sun’s mass of gas and dust, and some estimates go to forty or more. It’s still expanding, at up to 670 kilometres a second, fast enough to cross from the Earth to the Moon in under ten minutes. Measured over thirteen years of Hubble’s pictures, that expansion runs back to about 1847, while the eruption was still going on. And comparing how fast the Homunculus grows across the sky with how fast its gas is moving, from the Doppler shifts in its spectrum, gives its distance: about 2,300 parsecs, or 7,500 light-years.

The dust in it has changed Eta Carinae’s part in the nebula. It absorbs nearly all of the star’s ultraviolet and visible light and gives it out again as infrared, which makes the Homunculus one of the brightest objects in the sky at mid-infrared wavelengths. So the nebula’s most famous star now contributes almost nothing to lighting it: when it wrapped itself in dust, the ultraviolet light reaching the surrounding nebula dropped by about a fifth.

The Keyhole

Just above Eta Carinae and to the right, the bright arc and the dark knots inside and below it are the Keyhole. John Herschel drew it from the Cape around 1840, while the Great Eruption was under way, and described it as a “lemniscate-oval vacuity”: a hole in the nebula shaped like a figure of eight. Its present name came from the popular astronomy writer Emma Converse, who in 1873 described it as “resembling a keyhole”.

It’s about seven light-years across. The dark knots are cold clouds of molecular gas and dust, seen in silhouette against the glowing gas behind them, and their edges are being worn away by the ultraviolet light of the hot stars all round. The Keyhole looks different now from Herschel’s drawing, and the likeliest explanation is that it really has changed, as Eta Carinae’s light and winds have changed. The bright arc that gives it its shape is still a puzzle: a 2008 review of the nebula called its physical origin unexplained.

To its right, on its own, the small dark cloud with a bright rim is the Defiant Finger, a globule of at least six times the Sun’s mass. Its rim is where the ultraviolet light of the hot stars is evaporating it, and it’s expected to be gone in 200,000 years to a million.

What lights it

The nebula’s light comes from the other stars around Eta Carinae, the cluster Trumpler 16, and from the cluster Trumpler 14, whose centre is just beyond the top of the frame. The whole nebula is lit by about 65 O-type stars, the hottest and most massive kind, and three very massive Wolf-Rayet stars, a rarer kind whose winds are blowing their outer layers away. One of those three is in the picture: WR 25, the bright star near the middle, at the western edge of Trumpler 16, which gives out more than a million times as much energy as the Sun. The Orion Nebula, by comparison, gets most of its light from one star, θ¹ Orionis C. For most of its life, Carina has given out about 150 times as much of the ultraviolet light that makes a nebula glow as Orion does. Trumpler 16 is about 3 million years old, and Trumpler 14 perhaps 1 to 2 million. At those ages the stars are still in the cloud they formed from, and they’re taking it apart.

The colours are a map of that radiation. In this palette, the Hubble palette, sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue. Knocking a second electron off an oxygen atom takes ultraviolet light with at least 35 electronvolts of energy, which only the hottest stars give out in quantity, so blue fills the core, where the light of the two clusters is fiercest. Sulphur that has lost one electron glows where the gas is only partly ionised, along the edges of the dense clouds, so red-brown rims the dark lanes and the Keyhole’s knots. Those rims are the ionisation fronts, where the starlight is eating into the clouds. The dark lanes across the lower half are more of the same cold dust and gas, lying in front of the glowing nebula.

The picture

T72 is a PlaneWave CDK20, a 0.51-metre reflector, with a monochrome CCD camera, at Deep Sky Chile in the Río Hurtado Valley. The stars, and the detail in the Homunculus, come from 12 minutes each through the red, green, and blue filters, and the nebula from 1 hour 56 minutes each through the Hα, OIII, and SII filters: about 6 hours 20 minutes in all. I processed it in PixInsight. Taking the stars from colour frames is a common way to work with narrowband, since in a narrowband palette stars come out in colours that aren’t their own.

For me, this picture shows the power of a large scope under a pristine dark sky. It’s wonderful: violent and serene at the same time.
