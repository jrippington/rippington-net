The Globular Cluster M3

An ancient ball of stars, and what lies far behind it

June 2024, 10 min read

M3, an ancient ball of stars about 410,000 times the Sun's mass, 33,200 light-years away, high above the disc of the Milky Way, with more RR Lyrae variables than any other globular cluster; and behind it, galaxies, clusters of galaxies, and quasars whose light set out more than 11 billion years ago. From my garden in Oxfordshire, in the short nights of June.

The light from M3 left it about 33,200 years ago. About then, a young man was buried in red ochre in a cave at Paviland, on the Gower in south Wales, then far inland above a grassy plain grazed by mammoth and woolly rhinoceros. William Buckland, Oxford’s professor of geology, found him in 1823 and took him for a Roman woman, and he’s still called the Red Lady of Paviland; his bones are in Oxford’s Museum of Natural History.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken in June 2024. It covers 1.1 degrees of sky by 0.7, about two full Moons by one and a half, in Canes Venatici. It’s in natural colour, from the colour camera with no filter at all. M3 has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is down, so east is to the right and west to the left. M3 is the ball of stars right of the middle. The thin sliver at the left is the galaxy NGC 5263, and the bright orange star at the top left is HD 119081, in the foreground. Many of the faint smudges and points are far beyond the cluster: galaxies, clusters of galaxies, and quasars. At M3’s distance the frame is about 620 light-years across and 420 high, and each pixel covers about 8,900 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 300 times Neptune’s distance.

From Oxfordshire, M3 crosses the meridian 67 degrees up in the south. In June 2024 that was between about 10 pm at the start of the month and 8 pm at the end, and the sky never got fully dark: from about 21 May to 20 July the Sun doesn’t sink 18 degrees below the horizon here, so the whole night is twilight. At about magnitude 6.2, M3 is on the edge of what the eye can see from a dark site.

M3

M3, NGC 5272, is a globular cluster: an ancient ball of stars, about 410,000 times the Sun’s mass, often said to hold about half a million stars. Gaia’s measurements, with other methods, put it about 33,200 light-years away. Its stars are about 11.75 billion years old, among the oldest in the Galaxy (you’ll read eight billion on one NASA page, an outdated figure), and poor in heavy elements, with about a thirtieth of the Sun’s share of iron. As it shows here it’s about 19 arcminutes across, about 180 light-years, and the stars resolve right into its middle: the orange ones are red giants, the blue-white ones stars burning helium in their cores.

Charles Messier found it on 3 May 1764, the first object in his catalogue that he discovered himself, and saw a round nebula with a bright centre and no stars. William Herschel was the first to see it as stars, around 1784.

Its variable stars

M3 holds about 240 RR Lyrae stars, old stars that pulse in brightness every half a day or so, more than any other globular cluster, though M62 is close behind. The first was found in 1889, and by 1895 Solon Bailey had found 87. They made M3 the model of one of the two families of globular clusters that Pieter Oosterhoff told apart in 1939 by their RR Lyrae stars’ periods.

It holds other oddities. In 1953 Allan Sandage found here the first blue stragglers, stars that look younger and hotter than the cluster’s age allows, now thought to have gained mass from a companion or by merging with it. vZ 1128, the bluest bright star in the cluster, below and right of its centre, is an old star that has shed its outer layers, its surface at about 36,600 degrees, on its way to becoming a white dwarf. And V95, the bright orange star just left of the cluster’s centre, is a red giant near the end of that stage, which swells and fades over about three and a half months.

Its path round the Galaxy

Like most globular clusters, M3 isn’t in the Milky Way’s disc but in the halo round it. It’s about 32,500 light-years above the disc, almost exactly towards the Galaxy’s north pole as we see it, on a stretched orbit that takes it between about 17,000 and 52,000 light-years from the Galaxy’s centre. It’s heading back down towards the disc now. Five millisecond pulsars, the spun-up remains of exploded stars, have been found in it by their radio pulses, though none shows here.

Far beyond the cluster

M3 sits high above the Milky Way’s dust, so the view past it runs clear across the universe, and the field has been searched for quasars for decades. Here’s what’s behind it, nearest first.

NGC 5263, the sliver at the left, is a spiral galaxy seen nearly edge on, about 240 million light-years away, more than 7,000 times further than M3; William Herschel noted it on 11 April 1785. PGC 93726, a faint irregular galaxy above it, is at almost exactly its distance, a neighbour about 1.5 million light-years from it.

Further out are clusters of galaxies. The little orange smudges at the top right corner are galaxies in a cluster whose light is about 2.8 billion years old, probably the one Fritz Zwicky catalogued in the 1960s as ZwCl 1341.0+2820 and classed as “extremely distant”; modern surveys put its mass at about 300 trillion times the Sun’s. The fuzzy elliptical at the bottom edge is the central galaxy of another, WHL J134002.0+284038, its light about 3 billion years old. The faintest galaxy in the picture with a measured redshift, SDSS J1339+2831, is a reddish smudge at the left whose light left it about 5.9 billion years ago, before the Sun formed.

Then the quasars, the bright cores of distant galaxies, lit by gas falling into huge black holes. They look like faint stars. B2 1340+29, at the bottom right, is a blazar, its jet pointed nearly at us, about 7.5 billion light-years away. QSO B1337+285, at the left, looks like any 17th-magnitude star, but it’s the brightest quasar in the frame, and its light set out about 11.2 billion years ago, when the universe was about 2.6 billion years old; astronomers have long used it to study the hydrogen its light passes on the way. Fainter still, SDSS J1341+2840, a small bluish dot near the bottom, is 11.3 billion light-years away.

The farthest thing that shows in the picture is SDSS J1341+2839, a faint bluish speck right at its limit, near the bottom: a quasar whose light left it about 11.8 billion years ago, when the universe was about 2 billion years old, a seventh of its present age. And the farthest thing catalogued in the frame, SDSS J1340+2813, is just too faint for my picture: a quasar at a redshift of 5.36, whose light set out 12.7 billion years ago, when the universe was about 1.1 billion years old. The James Webb Space Telescope has looked at it, and in 2024 astronomers reported a black hole of about 2.5 billion times the Sun’s mass in a host galaxy too small for it.

In front

The brightest star in the picture, HD 119081, at the top left, is an orange giant about 550 light-years away, about 60 times nearer than M3. The blue-white star with spikes above the cluster, HD 119391, is nearer still, about 450 light-years away.

The colours

The camera’s own colours, with no filter: the cluster’s red giants orange-yellow, its stars burning helium blue-white, the distant elliptical galaxies yellowish, and the quasars bluish.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. The picture is 2 hours 23 minutes with no filter, in June 2024. It’s 4,408 by 2,990 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
