The Heart Nebula (IC 1805), Nyx

Melotte 15 and the Fish Head

The heart of the Heart, and its neighbour

November 2025, 12 min read

The heart of the Heart Nebula, about 6,800 light-years away in Cassiopeia: the young cluster Melotte 15, whose hottest stars have blown a cavity in the gas round them and are eating into its walls, and to the west the Fish Head and W3, where the same cloud is forming massive stars now. Two panels, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light in this picture left the Heart about 6,800 years ago, around 4,800 BC. Britain was already an island then, cut off from the continent by the rising sea, and home only to hunter-gatherers: farming wouldn’t arrive for another seven or eight centuries.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire: two frames side by side, taken over eight nights in November 2025. Together they cover 2.1 degrees of sky by 1, about four full Moons by two, in Cassiopeia. It’s from two filters that each pass two narrow bands of light, one hydrogen’s red line and oxygen’s green-blue, the other sulphur’s red and hydrogen’s blue, and the stars are from exposures with no filter. The Heart Nebula has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is up but tilted about 35 degrees to the right, so east is to the left and tilted up. The left half is the middle of the Heart Nebula: the blue cavity, with the cluster Melotte 15 in it, walled by gold clouds and dark pillars. The round cloud on the right is the Fish Head, with the W3 star-forming region round its edge. At the Heart’s distance, about 6,800 light-years, the frame is about 250 light-years across and 115 high, and each pixel covers about 1,800 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

It’s in Cassiopeia, about 5 degrees from ε Cassiopeiae, the eastern end of the W, and about as far north of the Double Cluster. From Oxfordshire it never sets: it passes 10 degrees north of the zenith, at 80 degrees up, around midnight at the start of November and 10 pm by the end, and it was above 34 degrees all through those nights’ darkness.

The Heart Nebula

The Heart Nebula, IC 1805, is a cloud of hydrogen lit by Melotte 15’s hottest stars, about 300 light-years across in all; this frame holds its middle. Gaia’s measurements of the cluster’s stars put it about 6,800 light-years away. You’ll often read 7,500, which matches an older distance from the stars’ colours and brightness; Gaia brings it in by about a tenth. It’s in the Perseus Arm, the next spiral arm out from ours, with its neighbours the Soul Nebula and the Fish Head: the three together are the brightest complex of glowing gas in this quarter of the Milky Way.

The cluster’s stars have blown a cavity in the gas, about 130 light-years across: the blue region here, where oxygen glows in their hardest light. And the gas they’ve pushed out has to go somewhere. In 1996 a radio survey found a funnel-shaped hole in the hydrogen above the Heart, rising out of the Milky Way’s disc with the cluster at its apex, which its finders called a chimney, venting hot gas towards the Galaxy’s halo. Whether it’s a chimney yet, or a bubble that’s only starting to break open, is still argued. In this picture “up” points away from the disc, so it rises out of the top of the frame.

Melotte 15

Melotte 15 is less a tight cluster than a loose group of young, massive stars: nine of the hottest kind, O stars, and about 40 of types O and early B, together about 2,700 times the Sun’s mass, a few million years old. Its brightest star, HD 15558, is a pair of massive stars, perhaps three, circling each other every 446 days. One orbit, from 2006, gave the larger at least 150 times the Sun’s mass, which would put it among the heaviest stars known, but later work revised the orbit and doubts it; models give it about 90. Just below the centre and a little left, the bright star with spikes is HD 15570, one of the most luminous stars in the Galaxy, whose spectrum is part-way to a Wolf-Rayet star’s: a massive star starting to blow away its outer layers. Just above the centre, HD 15629 is another of its hottest stars, about 61 times the Sun’s mass by the models, still in the prime of its life.

Philibert Jacques Melotte listed the cluster in 1915, as number 15 of the 245 he found on the Franklin-Adams photographic charts of the whole sky. The nebula came into the catalogues from Edward Barnard’s photographs, as IC 1805. You may read that William Herschel discovered the Heart in 1787; what he found was the brightest knot of the Fish Head.

Pillars and globules

The cavity’s walls are being eaten away, and what’s left points back at the stars doing it. Just left of the cluster, two pillars, the longer about 15 light-years, stand with their bright-rimmed heads towards it. Hanging from the top of the cavity, BRC 7 is a great V-shaped curtain of dust with its tip pointing down at the cluster, and stars are forming in that tip, the youngest nearest the bright rim: a sign that the cluster’s light, squeezing the cloud, set them off. On the left-hand wall, BRC 8 is a trunk of dust about 12 light-years long, also pointing back at the cluster, with young stars and dense cores at its head. Nearer the cluster, CG 7S is a small globule with a bright head and a tail streaming away from the light.

Look closely at the left-hand wall and there are tiny dark specks against the blue. Eighteen of them are catalogued here as globulettes, among the smallest clouds known: thousands of times the distance from the Earth to the Sun across, and weighing from a few to a couple of hundred times Jupiter’s mass, too little to make an ordinary star. None seems to hold one. They may become brown dwarfs, or planets drifting free of any star.

Where the Heart meets W3

Right of the cluster, the cavity’s western wall is where the Heart’s bubble runs into a giant cloud of molecular gas, the W3 cloud, about 50,000 times the Sun’s mass. Along that edge, at BRC 5 and AFGL 333, stars are forming where the two meet: astronomers have found more than 800 young stars in AFGL 333 alone. Whether the Heart’s expansion triggered them, or they’d have formed anyway, the studies don’t agree.

The bright star above the middle of the picture, HD 15557, has nothing to do with any of this. It’s an ordinary star a little hotter than the Sun, about 340 light-years away, twenty times nearer.

The Fish Head and W3

The round cloud on the right is the Fish Head, IC 1795, named for its shape, about 27 light-years across here and about 110 light-years from Melotte 15. It’s lit by its own cluster, a few million years old, whose brightest star is an O star near its middle. Its brightest knot, at its lower right, is NGC 896, and that’s the part of this whole region William Herschel saw, on 3 November 1787, a century before photographs showed the rest.

Round the Fish Head’s edge, hidden in the dust of the W3 cloud, is one of the most active places where massive stars are forming in our part of the Galaxy. W3 Main, at its right-hand edge, holds every kind of young hydrogen cloud a newborn massive star makes, from the smallest and densest to the full-grown, and about 900 young stars seen in X-rays; at its heart a cluster like Orion’s Trapezium may be forming now. Almost none of it shows here, only a few faint knots in a dark lane. Below the Fish Head and to its left, W3(OH) is invisible in ordinary light, but famous to radio astronomers: it was among the first masers found in space, natural microwave lasers, and in 2006 measuring their movement over the year fixed the distance to the Perseus Arm, about 6,400 light-years here. Above the Fish Head, the small bright cloud crossed by dark lanes is W3 North, lit by a single O star with no cluster round it, which may have been thrown out of one nearby.

These may be three generations of stars, each setting off the next. One study of 2005 proposed that an older bubble, older than the Heart’s cluster, ran into the W3 cloud and set off the Fish Head’s cluster, whose bubble in turn set off the newest stars round its edge. It doesn’t credit the Heart’s own cluster with the Fish Head.

The colours

The colours are the Hubble palette: sulphur red, hydrogen’s red line green, and oxygen blue, with hydrogen’s blue line blended in with the oxygen. So the cavity is blue where the cluster’s hardest light makes oxygen glow, and the walls are gold where the gas is shielded and sulphur and hydrogen glow together. The stars are from exposures with no filter, so their colours are their own.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. In front of it, two Antlia dual-band filters each pass two bands 5 nm wide: one hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III, the other sulphur II and hydrogen-beta. This is two panels, joined: 18 hours 31 minutes through the first filter and 24 hours 16 minutes through the second, and 1 hour 14 minutes with no filter for the stars, in all, over eight nights in November 2025. Joined, it’s 8,625 by 3,960 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight and finished it in Photoshop.
