The telescopes, Optica

250 mm Newtonian

An Orion Optics CT10 in a converted bike shed

April 2024, 4 min read

On this page

  1. The telescope
  2. The camera and its filters
  3. The mount and the guiding
  4. In numbers

My 250 mm Newtonian is an Orion Optics UK CT10, on a Sky-Watcher EQ8-R mount, in a converted bike shed with a roll-off roof in the garden here in Oxfordshire. With a 0.75× reducer it works at 882 mm and f/3.5, and its Altair colour camera takes in about 1.5° by 1°, three full Moons by two.

The telescope

The CT10 is made by Orion Optics UK, the British maker (not the American Orion, which has since closed), and I chose it for its reputation for extremely high build and optical quality. It’s a Newtonian reflector: a 250 mm concave primary mirror at the bottom of the tube, and a small flat secondary mirror near the top that turns the light out through the side of the tube to the focuser. Its tube is carbon fibre, which barely changes length as the night cools, so the focus drifts less than it would in a metal tube.

Its primary mirror has a Strehl ratio of 0.992. The Strehl ratio compares the peak brightness of a star’s image with what a perfect optic would give: 1 is perfect, and 0.8 is the usual mark for an optic whose stars are limited only by diffraction, so 0.992 is within 1 per cent of perfect.

On its own the telescope has a focal length of 1,176 mm, which makes it f/4.7. A fast Newtonian like this shows coma towards the edges of the frame, where stars are drawn out into small comet shapes. The Starizona Nexus between the focuser and the camera corrects that, and reduces the focal length by 0.75× to 882 mm, f/3.5, which widens the field and shortens the exposures a faint target needs.

The camera and its filters

The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a one-shot colour camera built round Sony’s IMX571 sensor and cooled, usually to −10 °C: 6248 by 4176 pixels, each 3.76 µm, behind a fine pattern of red, green, and blue filters. At 882 mm each pixel covers 0.88 arcseconds of sky, and the whole frame about 1.5° by 1°.

In front of it, an Altair filter wheel holds three filters, each chosen to make the most of a colour sensor. Two are Antlia dual-band filters, each passing two bands 5 nm wide: one hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III, the other sulphur II and hydrogen-beta. Hydrogen-alpha and sulphur II are deep red and fall on the camera’s red pixels, while oxygen III and hydrogen-beta are blue-green and fall on its green and blue ones, so a single set of exposures records two kinds of glowing gas at once. The third, an Altair TRI-RGB, passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, and the gaps between them cut out much of the light of street lamps.

There’s also a ZWO rotator, which turns the camera to frame a target at the angle I want, and brings it back to the same framing on another night.

For the Moon I use the telescope without the Nexus, at its full 1,176 mm, with a small ZWO ASI678MC planetary camera: 3840 by 2160 pixels, each 2 µm, so each covers 0.35 arcseconds, and the frame about 22 arcminutes by 13, nearly three quarters of the Moon’s width. It records video, and the sharpest frames are stacked. In front of it goes an infrared filter, to keep the atmosphere a bit more still: the air’s turbulence blurs red and infrared light less than blue.

The mount and the guiding

The mount is a Sky-Watcher EQ8-R, a large German equatorial mount. It’s guided through a 102 mm SVBONY SV503 refractor with a focal reducer, riding on top of the tube, with a ZWO ASI120MM guide camera. I guide with a separate scope rather than an off-axis guider, which would take its guide stars from the main telescope’s own light, because with the rotator in place there isn’t room for one; the SV503 will also be used for solar imaging later.
