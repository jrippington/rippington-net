Aligning and stacking

Many frames into one picture, and the faint signal out of the noise

A single frame of a faint nebula is mostly noise. Take many, line them up star for star, and average them, and the noise shrinks while the nebula stays, so the faint parts rise out of the grain. This is why stacking works and how much it gives, how software finds and matches the stars to line the frames up, how it evens out a changing sky and leaves out satellites, and how frames through several filters, from several nights, or from several patches of sky become one picture.

This is the next stop along Optica’s path after calibration, and it builds on How astro cameras work, which explains where the noise in a frame comes from. It starts with why stacking works and how much it gives, then follows the steps the software takes, then the cases that make it more involved: several filters, several nights, colour cameras, and mosaics. As before, everything is explained in words first, and the last section sets the same ideas out as mathematics. Two more articles at this stop go further: Drizzle, which builds a finer picture from frames that moved a little, and Stacking beyond the average, on newer methods that do more than average.

Why stacking works

Every frame records two things at once: the light from the target, which is the same in every frame, and noise, which isn’t. Photons arrive at random, so a pixel that collects 200 electrons from a nebula on average collects 190 one time and 215 the next. The sky’s light scatters in the same way, and the camera adds a little read noise every time it reads. In a single frame of a faint target the scatter can be as large as the target’s light, and the target is lost in the grain.

Averaging separates them. Take the same pixel in many frames, add up its values, and divide by the number of frames. The target’s light comes out the same as in any one frame, since it was the same in each. The noise doesn’t, because it was up in some frames and down in others, and in the sum the ups and downs partly cancel. Only partly: the noise in an average of N frames is the noise of one frame divided by the square root of N. Four frames halve it, sixteen cut it to a quarter, and sixty-four to an eighth, while the target stays as bright as it was.

Why the square root, and not N itself? Think of tossing a coin a hundred times. You’d expect fifty heads, but you’d rarely get exactly fifty: typically you’d be about five out, either way. Toss it ten thousand times and you’d typically be about fifty out. The error in the count grows, but only as the square root of the number of tosses, so as a share of the total it shrinks: 5 in 100 is 5 per cent, 50 in 10,000 only half of 1 per cent. Noise in a stack behaves the same way. The signal grows in proportion to the number of frames, the noise only as its square root, so their ratio, the signal-to-noise ratio, improves as the square root.

How astro cameras work worked through an example: a five-minute frame of a faint nebula under a suburban sky, with a signal-to-noise ratio of about 5, enough to see the nebula but grainy. Thirty-six such frames, three hours in all, raise it to about 29. PixInsight’s documentation calls the square-root law an upper limit that’s never quite reached, since real frames differ in quality and some values are always left out, but it comes close.

Stacking does two more things. It makes the steps finer. A camera records whole numbers, so one frame can’t record a difference smaller than one step of its converter, but an average of sixteen frames can take any value in sixteenths of a step. Because noise jitters every reading from step to step, even a signal fainter than one step nudges the average: Jack Hogan, simulating it, recovered a signal a twentieth of a step deep from 10,000 frames, and Roger Clark, who has written widely on the science of astrophotography, shows real stacks bringing out detail 10 to 20 times fainter than the sky’s own glow. That’s why a stack is kept as 32-bit floating-point numbers rather than the camera’s 16-bit whole numbers, which would round the fine steps away. And it gives the software many witnesses for every pixel, so something that appears in only one frame, such as a satellite’s trail, can be outvoted and left out. That’s rejection, which comes later.

Many short frames or a few long ones

Since the noise falls with the total exposure, why not take one very long frame instead of many short ones? Partly because a long frame risks more: a gust of wind, a guiding error, a cloud, or an aircraft spoils one frame, and the longer the frame, the more is lost. Bright stars and the cores of galaxies saturate sooner, so a long frame has less dynamic range; Clark shows that ten one-minute frames hold a wider range of brightness than two five-minute ones, and keep more of the stars’ colour. Under a bright sky, a long frame fills up with the sky’s own light. And satellites now cross a growing share of frames: in the Zwicky Transient Facility’s survey pictures taken in twilight, the share with a Starlink trail rose from under half of 1 per cent in late 2019 to 18 per cent in August 2021, and there were 11,156 Starlink satellites in orbit at the start of October 2026. A trail in one short frame is easy to reject; a trail through a single long one is there for good.

The case for longer frames is read noise, which the camera adds once each time it reads a frame. How astro cameras work gives Robin Glover’s rule, worked out for the SharpCap capture software: each frame should be long enough for the sky to put about ten times the square of the read noise in electrons into every pixel, at which point the read noise adds only about 5 per cent to the total noise, and longer frames gain almost nothing. With modern low-noise cameras that’s often a minute or two, or seconds under a bright sky; with the old CCDs it was many minutes. Past that point the total exposure is what matters, however it’s divided, and shorter frames are safer.

How much is enough

The square-root law has an awkward consequence: every doubling of the signal-to-noise ratio costs four times as much exposure as the last. Four hours give twice the signal-to-noise ratio of one; sixteen hours, four times. Each extra frame helps a little less than the one before, which is why imagers think in doublings of time rather than in a few more frames.

There’s no standard answer to how much is enough, because it depends on how faint the target is, how dark the sky, and how much grain you’ll put up with. Advice has changed with the cameras, too. PixInsight’s documentation, written when long CCD exposures were the norm, says the gain is significant up to about 30 frames and becomes impractical beyond 50; Sky at Night’s guide in 2019 called about 20 typically ideal. Short frames from today’s CMOS cameras are counted in hundreds, and PixInsight now has a tool, FastIntegration, built for thousands. One guide suggests an hour or two for a bright target such as the Orion Nebula, and ten hours or more for faint galaxies and dark nebulae.

One more number matters: rejection needs enough frames to tell an outlier from the rest. Most of its methods want at least eight or ten, and the best ones work better with fifteen or more.

The steps

Stacking software follows much the same steps everywhere, though each program names and arranges them its own way. Calibration comes first, as Darks, flats, and bias describes, and with a colour camera the frames are then debayered. The frames are measured and graded. They’re registered: their stars are found and matched, and every frame is moved on to one reference frame. They’re normalised, so that changes in the sky between them are evened out, and given weights. And they’re integrated: every pixel is averaged through the stack, leaving out values that don’t belong.

The free programs Siril (version 1.4.4 in June 2026) and DeepSkyStacker (6.2.2, for Windows) do all of this, and so do the paid ones, PixInsight, whose WeightedBatchPreprocessing script, WBPP, runs the whole chain, and Astro Pixel Processor, whose newest version was still a beta of 2.0 in October 2026. ASTAP, a plate solver that also stacks, and Sequator, for wide fields and nightscapes, are simpler.

Grading the frames

Not every frame deserves a place. Software measures each one, and the measurements show which went wrong. PixInsight’s SubframeSelector and Siril’s plots measure, among other things:

FWHMHow wide the stars are at half their peak brightness: the sharpness.
Eccentricity or roundnessHow far the stars are from round. Elongated stars mean a guiding error or wind; PixInsight’s documentation says an eccentricity below about 0.42 isn’t noticeable to most people.
Number of starsFewer stars than usual means cloud or haze.
BackgroundThe sky’s brightness: the Moon, dawn, or light pollution as the target sinks.
Noise and signal-to-noiseHow clean the frame is.

Frames can then be set aside by hand, by a threshold, or by keeping the best share: Siril can keep a percentage of the best frames by any of its measures, and DeepSkyStacker gives every frame a score and can stack only a chosen percentage of the best. It’s worth looking at the frames too, flicking through them one after another (blinking, in the jargon), which catches trails, cloud, and odd frames that a single number misses.

How much to throw away is a matter of taste. Every frame left out is light not used, so many imagers discard only the obvious failures, such as cloud or trailed stars, and let weighting, which comes later, give the rest the say they deserve.

Registration: putting every frame in the same place

No two frames line up exactly. The telescope is moved a few pixels between frames on purpose (dithering), and drifts a little anyway. After a meridian flip, when a German equatorial mount swings the telescope to the other side of its pier partway through the night, every later frame is upside down, turned 180°. Frames from another night are framed a little differently and turned slightly; frames from another telescope have a different scale; frames from an alt-azimuth mount turn steadily as the night goes on, at about 10.7° an hour for a target 30° up on the meridian, seen from 52° north. And wide-field optics distort the edges of the field, so that the same star falls in slightly different places as a dithered frame moves. Registration measures all of that, frame by frame, and moves every frame on to one reference. It’s done in three steps: find the stars, match them, and move the pixels.

Finding the stars

Stars are ideal markers: each is essentially a point, and needs only a position and a brightness to describe it. Software first measures the sky’s background and its noise, then looks for small peaks that rise well above the noise: Siril’s default threshold is five times the noise above the background. Each candidate must be the brightest point in its neighbourhood, and must be more than a single bright pixel, which would be a hot pixel or a cosmic ray: Siril insists that several neighbouring pixels are bright too. Then a model of a star’s profile, a smooth bell shape (a Gaussian, or a Moffat profile with broader wings), is fitted to each one, which gives its centre to a fraction of a pixel. Stars that fit badly, that are too elongated, or that are saturated in the middle are dealt with or left out; Siril fits a saturated star using only its unsaturated wings.

How precisely a centre can be found depends on how bright the star is. A star’s centre is uncertain by roughly its width divided by its signal-to-noise ratio, so a bright star is placed to a few hundredths of a pixel and a faint one to a few tenths. Since registration fits all the stars at once, hundreds or thousands of them, the frame as a whole is placed much more precisely than any single star.

Matching the stars

Now the software has two lists of star positions, one for the frame and one for the reference, with no labels to say which star is which. The frame may be shifted, turned, or even scaled; some stars in one list may be missing from the other. PixInsight’s documentation calls this the correspondence problem, and the classic answer is to match shapes rather than stars.

Take any three stars: they make a triangle, and the triangle’s shape, the ratios of its sides, stays the same however the frame is shifted, turned, or scaled. So the software makes triangles from the brightest stars in each list, and looks for pairs of triangles with the same shape. A single match could be chance, but if many matched triangles agree that star 12 in the frame is star 7 in the reference, it almost certainly is: each matched triangle casts a vote for its three pairs of stars, and the pairs with the most votes are kept.

This is the method Valdes and colleagues published in 1995 for the FOCAS package, and it’s what Siril uses, through code by Michael Richmond: triangles from the 20 brightest stars of each frame, matched by their shape, and voted on. PixInsight switched in 2013 to bigger shapes, pentagons by default, which are much harder to match by chance, though unlike triangles they can’t match a frame that’s been mirrored. The astrometry.net service, which works out where on the sky any picture is, uses shapes of four stars. Some of the votes will still be wrong, so a final step, RANSAC (for random sample consensus), repeatedly fits a transform to a few pairs chosen at random, counts how many of the rest agree with it, and keeps the transform with the most support, ignoring the pairs that disagree.

The transform

How a frame relates to the reference is described by a transform, and there’s a family of them, each adding something to the last. A shift moves the frame up, down, or sideways. A rotation turns it as well, which a meridian flip needs. A similarity transform adds a change of scale, for frames from different telescopes or cameras. An affine transform allows different scales across and down and a slant, or shear. And a projective transform, or homography, adds the slight keystoning of perspective. Each takes more matched stars to fit: one pair for a shift, two for a similarity, three for an affine transform, four for a homography. The transform is fitted to all the matched stars at once by least squares, which finds the version that leaves the smallest total of squared mismatches. Siril and PixInsight both use a homography by default.

Optical distortion bends the field in ways no single transform can capture, and for wide fields, mosaics, and frames from different telescopes, software can model it. PixInsight’s StarAlignment fits surface splines, which bend smoothly to follow the stars like a thin flexible sheet; Siril 1.4 uses the distortion measured when a frame is plate-solved, matched against a star catalogue to find exactly where it points. Siril can register frames entirely from their plate solutions, too, which suits mosaics and frames with little in common.

Moving the pixels

Once the transform is known, every frame is redrawn on the reference’s grid of pixels. A frame moved by a fraction of a pixel no longer lines up with the grid, so each new pixel’s value has to be estimated from the old pixels around it: that’s interpolation, and the choice of method matters more than it might seem. Nearest neighbour just copies the closest old pixel, which puts things up to half a pixel out of place. Bilinear interpolation averages the four nearest pixels, which keeps things in place but softens them. Bicubic and Lanczos interpolation weigh more neighbours with carefully shaped weights, some of them negative, which keeps detail sharp; Lanczos weights come from the mathematically ideal way to rebuild a smooth image from samples, trimmed to a few pixels either side.

Those negative weights have a cost: next to a very sharp, bright star they can dig a dark ring round it, which is called ringing. Software guards against it by clamping, which falls back to a gentler method where a ring would form. PixInsight uses Lanczos-3 by default, clamped at a threshold of 0.3. Siril uses Lanczos-4, clamped, and says that gives stars often 8 per cent narrower than its old default did. PixInsight’s measurements show that ringing matters only for undersampled stars: beyond about three pixels across, it’s negligible whatever the method. They also show that how well an image is sampled matters more than which method is used.

Interpolation has a subtler effect: it makes the noise look smaller than it is. Bilinear interpolation at a half-pixel shift averages four pixels, which halves the noise from one pixel to the next, but only by blurring them together; nothing has been gained. PixInsight’s documentation puts it plainly: a registered frame that keeps 90 per cent of its noise has kept 90 per cent of its fine detail. That’s one reason Drizzle avoids interpolation altogether.

The reference frame

The reference itself is never resampled: everything else is moved on to it. So it’s worth choosing well, and software does it automatically. Siril chooses the sharpest frame once frames have been measured, and its two-pass mode also prefers a frame with plenty of stars that sits near the middle of the others’ framing, so the others move as little as possible. PixInsight’s WBPP chooses the frame with the most stars; DeepSkyStacker, the frame with the best score.

Normalisation: evening out the sky

The sky doesn’t keep still through a night. As the Moon rises, or the target sinks towards the glow of a town, the background brightens; thin cloud or haze dims everything coming through it. So the frames differ in two ways: by an offset, the sky’s brightness added to every pixel, and by a scale, the share of the target’s light that got through. Before frames can be compared pixel by pixel, those differences have to go, or rejection would compare a faint nebula in one frame with plain sky in another and throw out good values.

Normalisation measures each frame’s background level and its spread, and scales and shifts every frame to match the reference: Siril’s default, and PixInsight’s for combining light frames, is additive with scaling, which does both. The measurements use robust statistics, chosen because stars and nebulae would throw ordinary ones off: the median rather than the mean for the background level, and for the spread an estimator that ignores outliers, such as the one called IKSS that Siril and PixInsight both use. PixInsight normalises twice, in fact: once to decide which values to reject, and once, separately, for the average itself.

Ordinary normalisation treats a whole frame alike. But light pollution and the Moon light the sky unevenly, and the gradient across a frame changes as the target moves through the night, so one frame can be brighter on the left and the next on the right. Local normalisation matches frames region by region. PixInsight’s LocalNormalization, introduced in 2017 and rewritten in 2022, does this; so does John Murphy’s NormalizeScaleGradient script, which measures the scale from the stars’ brightness and models the difference in gradient as a smooth surface; and so does Astro Pixel Processor’s local normalisation correction, which its developer says helps little with one night’s data but clearly with several.

Weighting: letting the best frames count for more

Normalised, the frames match in brightness but not in quality: the frame taken through haze, or by moonlight, is noisier than the one taken under a dark sky. Averaged with equal weights, its noise counts as much as the clean frame’s, so the stack is noisier than it need be. Weighting gives every frame a say in proportion to its quality.

The statistical answer is to weight each frame by one over the square of its noise, its variance, which gives the average with the least possible noise. PixInsight’s classic weighting does essentially that, from a careful estimate of each frame’s noise. How much it gains depends on how much the frames differ: if half the frames are twice as noisy as the rest, weighting improves the stack’s signal-to-noise ratio by about 25 per cent, worth about 56 per cent more frames; if they differ by only 20 per cent, the gain is under 2 per cent.

PixInsight’s PSF Signal Weight, introduced in 2022 and now the default in WBPP, combines the brightness of the stars, their size, the noise, and the background, so it rewards sharp frames as well as clean ones. Siril can weight by the number of stars, by a star width it calls weighted FWHM, or by noise, though by default it doesn’t weight at all; Astro Pixel Processor offers a quality weight combining noise, star density, and star shape. Not everyone agrees that sharpness belongs in a weight. John Murphy argues that only the signal-to-noise ratio does, and that weighting by FWHM gives a noisier stack; others prefer a sharper one. It’s a choice between the least noise and a balance of noise and sharpness, and Stacking beyond the average goes into it further.

Combining, and leaving things out

With the frames registered, normalised, and weighted, every pixel of the result is worked out from that pixel’s values through the stack: one value from each frame, a column of numbers.

The obvious way to combine them is the average, which gives the least noise. Its weakness is that a single wild value, a satellite’s trail or a cosmic ray, pulls it a long way. The median, the middle value, ignores wild values, but it’s noisier: about 25 per cent noisier than the average for the same frames, so it needs about 57 per cent more frames to match it, and in big stacks it can make faint gradations look stepped. So almost every program averages, and leaves out the wild values first. That’s rejection, and choosing how to do it is one of stacking’s main settings.

Rejection catches anything that appears in one frame, or a few, and not the rest: satellites and aircraft, cosmic rays, and hot pixels that dithering has moved about. The methods differ in how they decide what’s too far out:

Minimum and maximumAlways drops the highest and lowest values. It can’t miss, but it throws away good data every time.
Percentile clippingRejects values more than a set percentage from the median. For very small stacks, up to about six frames.
Sigma clippingMeasures the scatter (the standard deviation), rejects values more than a set number of times it from the median, and repeats until nothing more goes. From about eight or ten frames.
Winsorized sigma clippingSigma clipping that measures the scatter more robustly, by first pulling extreme values in rather than letting them inflate it. Siril’s default; PixInsight’s documentation recommends it from about 15 frames.
Linear fit clippingFits a straight line to the sorted values, which copes with a sky that changed steadily through the night. For larger stacks, from 15 frames or more.
Generalised extreme Studentized deviate (ESD)A statistical test that removes the most extreme value, checks whether it really was an outlier, and repeats, with thresholds that grow with the number of frames. PixInsight’s developer called it the best available in 2022; for large stacks, 25 frames or more, and especially 50 or more.

PixInsight’s WBPP can choose a method for itself by the number of frames, and its choice has changed between versions. Sources differ on these thresholds, sometimes within one program’s own documentation, so they’re guides rather than rules.

The thresholds themselves are set in multiples of the scatter, often between 2 and 4, sometimes different for values too low and too high. Rejection has a cost: set too tightly, it throws away good values along with the bad, and the stack is noisier. With a threshold of 3 either side, only about 1 good value in 370 is lost; with 2, about 1 in 22. Satellite trails need special care, since a bright trail’s faint edges may not look like outliers, and PixInsight’s large-scale rejection widens the rejection round a trail for that reason. Most programs can also save a rejection map, showing how much was rejected at every pixel, which is the easiest way to check that the trails have gone and the stars haven’t. In a survey of 203 astrophotographers by DarkSky International in 2025, stacking with rejection was rated the most effective defence against satellites, with 63 per cent finding it extremely effective.

What comes out

The result is one picture, kept as 32-bit floating-point numbers. It’s an average, not a sum, so its brightness is that of a single frame, with the noise of many frames divided down. It’s also still linear: every pixel’s value is still in proportion to the light that fell there, which is what the next stages need, for removing gradients and calibrating colour, before the picture is stretched. Unstretched, it looks almost black, with a few bright stars; the faint nebula is there, but squeezed into the bottom of the range.

More than one filter

With a mono camera, each filter’s frames are a set of their own. They’re calibrated with flats taken through that filter, since each filter carries its own dust and slightly different vignetting, and stacked separately: one stack of red, one of green, one of blue, and perhaps one of luminance, or of hydrogen-alpha, OIII, and SII. Exposures can differ from filter to filter: a narrowband filter lets through much less of the sky, so its frames can be much longer before the sky’s noise swamps the read noise. ASTAP advises against mixing exposure lengths within one filter, since rejection works less well, but different lengths for different filters are fine.

The stacks then have to line up with each other exactly, or the colours would fringe at every star. The simplest way is to register every filter’s frames to the same reference frame, which PixInsight’s WBPP has done since 2021; or the finished stacks can be registered to one another. Combining them into a colour picture comes later, at the colour stop on Optica’s path.

More than one night

A picture often takes several nights, and each night’s frames must be calibrated with that night’s own flats: the camera has been taken off and put back, or turned, or the dust has moved. Siril’s guidance is to calibrate each night separately with its own master frames, then register all the calibrated frames to one reference and stack them together as one set. WBPP does the same with keywords, labels taken from folder names or the files’ headers, which say which frames belong to which night: a keyword used for calibration keeps the nights’ calibration apart; left out of integration, it lets the nights be stacked together.

Normalisation and weighting then do much of the work of reconciling the nights, and local normalisation earns its keep here, since each night’s gradients differ. Frames before and after a meridian flip are handled by registration, since a flip simply turns the picture by 180°. One trap is worth knowing: frames mustn’t be turned round to match before calibration, or they’ll no longer line up with their own flats.

Mono and one-shot colour

A one-shot colour camera’s frames are calibrated on the raw mosaic of red, green, and blue pixels, as Darks, flats, and bias explains, and then debayered, which turns each into a full colour picture by estimating the missing colours at every pixel (How astro cameras work describes how; Siril’s default method, RCD, suits stars). The colour frames are then registered and stacked like mono ones, with the stars found on the green, where the pixels are densest. One set of frames gives one colour stack. The alternative to debayering is Bayer drizzle, which builds the colour from measured values alone, and has an article of its own, Drizzle.

With a dual-band filter, which passes hydrogen-alpha and OIII together, the two can be separated before stacking. Siril’s extraction takes the hydrogen-alpha from the red pixels, a quarter of them, giving a picture half the width and height, and the OIII from the green and blue, three pixels in four; each is then registered and stacked on its own.

Mono needs a stack per filter, as above. For broadband colour the two collect much the same light: three hours with a colour camera puts a quarter of the pixels to work on red, half on green, and a quarter on blue, which adds up to the same as an hour through each of three filters on a mono camera. In narrowband, mono gathers roughly three to four times as much hydrogen-alpha in the same time, since every pixel collects it rather than mainly the red quarter.

Mosaics

Some targets are bigger than the camera’s field: the Andromeda Galaxy spans about 3° by 1°, the Veil Nebula nearly 3°, and the North America Nebula 2°. Others call for a long focal length to show their detail over a wide field. A mosaic covers them with several overlapping fields, called panels or tiles, each stacked on its own and joined into one picture. The cost is time: each panel needs its own full share of exposure, so four panels take four times as long, often across several nights.

The panels must overlap, both so they can be registered to each other and so their brightness can be matched where they meet. How much is another point where sources differ: KStars’ planner defaults to 5 per cent, PixInsight’s MosaicPlanner to 15, N.I.N.A.’s framing assistant to 20, and the conventional wisdom on Astro Pixel Processor’s forum is about 10, with more if stars are scarce or the optics distort the edges. Every panel should be taken with the camera turned to the same angle, which N.I.N.A. and similar programs help with, measuring the angle by plate solving.

Joining the panels has three parts. First, each stacked panel is plate-solved to find exactly where it lies on the sky, and all of them are redrawn on one common projection, a flat map of that part of the sky. For a mosaic a few degrees across that’s the gnomonic projection, the one a telescope itself makes, and wider mosaics need others, since a gnomonic map stretches badly far from its centre. Distortion has to be corrected too, or stars come out doubled or smeared along the joins. Second, the panels’ brightness is matched. Each was taken under its own sky, with its own background and gradient, so a scale and an offset are fitted from the overlaps, where both panels saw the same sky, and gradients are removed from each panel or evened out across the joins. Third, the joins are blended. Rather than cutting from one panel to the next, each panel’s weight is faded towards its edge across the overlap (feathering), so the join has no sharp step. Astro Pixel Processor’s multi-band blending goes further, blending broad features over a wide zone and fine ones over a narrow one, so stars aren’t doubled; its developer notes that wide blending costs a little signal-to-noise.

What goes wrong is usually visible: steps in brightness at the joins, colour that changes from panel to panel, doubled stars where the distortion wasn’t corrected, or noise that differs from panel to panel because they had different amounts of exposure. Siril 1.4 brought mosaic support, with plate-solved registration, matching on the overlaps, and feathering; PixInsight has MosaicByCoordinates for the registration, with other tools for the joins; and Astro Pixel Processor is known for building large mosaics, even solving many panels at once. With a mono camera, each filter’s mosaic is usually assembled on its own and the colour combined afterwards.

Matching triangles

Valdes’s method describes a triangle with sides a≥b≥ca \ge b \ge ca≥b≥c by two ratios, which don’t change when the triangle is shifted, turned, or scaled:
