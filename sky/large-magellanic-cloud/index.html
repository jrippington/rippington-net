The Large Magellanic Cloud

The heart of a neighbouring galaxy

The heart of the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy about 162,000 light-years away that orbits our own: its off-centre bar of old stars, the Tarantula, the great shells blown by generations of massive stars, ancient globular clusters, the wrecks of supernovae, and the first complex organic molecules found beyond our Galaxy; and behind it all, quasars that astronomers use to measure how it moves. Four panels from a fast telescope in Chile, in the Hubble palette.

The light in this picture left the Cloud about 162,000 years ago, in the ice age before last, when our own species was young, in Africa. Because the Cloud is tilted, the light from the bottom right corner had been on its way for seven to eleven thousand years when the light from the top left set out.

This picture is from iTelescope’s T71, a fast 180 mm telescope at Deep Sky Chile, which I used remotely in January 2023. It’s four frames joined into one, covering 4.7 degrees of sky by 3, about nine full Moons by six, in Dorado. It’s in the Hubble palette, from three filters that each pass the light of one element: hydrogen, oxygen, and sulphur. The Large Magellanic Cloud has its own gallery, and so does the Tarantula; two closer pictures of that side of the Cloud are Tarantula Wide and The Tarantula and Its Neighbours.

What’s in the frame

North is up, tilted a little to the left, so east is to the left, west to the right, and south down. The pale band of light across the middle, from the lower left to the upper right, is the bar, the glow of countless old stars too faint to see one by one. The great blue cloud on the left is the Tarantula, with the arcs of a supergiant shell curving round it, and alone in the dark top left corner is the round bubble of N70. Across the top are N144 and N138; at the upper right, the S-shaped N119, N113, and, at the top edge, the cluster NGC 1850 in its ring of gas. The right-hand third is a faint lacework of filaments, LMC 8, with an orange shell at the right edge. Along the bottom are a big blue bubble, N206, and the faint arc of N200, and at the lower left, N180. At the Cloud’s distance, about 162,000 light-years, the frame is about 13,300 light-years across and 8,600 high, and each pixel covers about 1.2 light-years.

From Deep Sky Chile the Cloud never sets, and in January it crossed the meridian about 50 degrees up, around midnight. From Britain it never rises.

The Large Magellanic Cloud

The Large Magellanic Cloud is a small galaxy orbiting our own. Twenty pairs of stars that eclipse each other gave its distance in 2019, to about 1 per cent: about 162,000 light-years. With its dark matter it weighs about 140 to 190 billion times the Sun, judged by how it pulls on streams of stars in our Galaxy’s halo; its stars alone are a couple of per cent of that.

It’s moving fast, about 321 kilometres a second relative to our Galaxy, and it’s probably on its first close pass, though a study of 2024 argues for a second. About 150 million years ago it collided with the Small Magellanic Cloud, which probably knocked its bar askew. The two Clouds trail a stream of gas that, with a leading arm ahead of them, stretches over 200 degrees of sky: they have lost most of the gas they began with.

Its disc is tilted to us, the north-east side nearer, so the light from the top left corner of this picture left it 156,000 to 158,000 years ago, and the light from the bottom right 165,000 to 167,000, depending on how the tilt is measured.

The bar

The bar is old and middle-aged stars, mostly 1 to 10 billion years old, with no gas of its own. It’s strange in other ways too. It’s off the centre of the Cloud’s disc by 1,300 to 2,600 light-years, tilted to it, and turns slowly if at all, and its long axis runs about 14,000 light-years. This mosaic is centred on it, and holds at least about 85 per cent of it; by some measures of its length, all of it.

Astronomers have measured the Cloud’s centre in half a dozen ways, from its starlight, its stars’ motions, and the rotation of its hydrogen, and they don’t agree: all of those centres are in this picture, the hydrogen’s near N119 at the upper right. The Cloud is the prototype of a whole class of galaxies, the Magellanic spirals, with one spiral arm and a bar off their centre; simulations of a collision between two small galaxies leave just such a lopsided disc.

The Tarantula and the south-east

The Tarantula, 30 Doradus, on the left, is the biggest and brightest region of star formation in our Local Group of galaxies, and the pictures of it linked above tell its story. Right of it and a little down, SN 1987A, the first supernova visible to the naked eye since 1604, is a small knot, mostly the light of two stars beside it that merge with it at this scale. Below the Tarantula runs the molecular ridge, a column of glowing knots where the Cloud’s densest gas is forming stars.

The great arcs of gold and blue filaments curving round the left side seem to be LMC 2, the brightest of the Cloud’s supergiant shells, about 3,000 light-years across, blown over about 9 million years by generations of massive stars. At the lower left, in N180, astronomers found a jet from a massive young star, and in 2024 the first disc of gas seen turning round a massive young star in another galaxy, feeding it; the jet and star are too small to pick out here.

The upper right

N119, the S-shaped nebula, is the only one in the Cloud whose gas lies in a spiral, about 400 by 600 light-years. The brightest star in its glow is S Doradus, the prototype of the luminous blue variables, unstable giant stars that brighten and fade by several magnitudes over years: S Doradus varies between magnitudes 8.6 and 11.5, never bright enough to see without a telescope.

Right of it, in N113, the ALMA telescope found the first complex organic molecules known outside our own Galaxy, in 2018: dimethyl ether and methyl formate, in two hot, dense cores where stars are forming.

At the top edge, NGC 1850 is a rich double cluster in a ring of gas, the superbubble N103B; the main cluster is something like 50 to 100 million years old, its smaller partner only about 4 million. In 2022 a black hole was claimed in it, but other astronomers showed that the star said to be circling it is probably a stripped star of about one Sun, and a normal companion fits: probably no black hole. Just above it, the remnant of a supernova, commonly also called N103B, is the debris of a white dwarf that exploded: as we see it, it’s about 700 to 900 years old, and still ploughing into dense gas that its parent system shed before it blew.

Old clusters

Five of the Cloud’s globular clusters in the frame are, by Hubble’s measurements, as old as our own Galaxy’s classic globular clusters, and all five are in or near the bar. NGC 2005 is probably a relic of a small galaxy the Cloud swallowed long ago: its stars’ chemistry is unlike any other cluster’s in it. And beside NGC 1835, the brightest, a small young cluster seems to be being pulled into it; if confirmed, that would be the first sign of a massive globular capturing a small cluster.

Shells, real and false

The Cloud is full of shells blown by massive stars’ winds and supernovae. Besides LMC 2, LMC 8, the lacework on the right, is several connected shells about 3,000 light-years across in all, lit only on its northern and eastern walls; and LMC 9, along the bottom, turns out to be a “false” shell, a chance line-up of separate bubbles and filaments with no shell in its hydrogen. Among them, N204 is the most purely blue of the big nebulae here, glowing in oxygen’s light, and N206, near the bottom edge, is a region of star formation about the size of Orion’s belt and sword, with a middle-aged supernova remnant on one side. N186, the round shell at the right edge, is orange, strong in sulphur’s light, a sign of shocks, and a supernova remnant is catalogued on its upper edge.

Far beyond

Behind the Cloud are galaxies and quasars. Its dust is thin enough to see through, and a survey of 2013 found 243 quasars behind this frame alone; about six to eight of them show here. They matter: the Hubble telescope measured the Cloud’s speed by watching its stars against background quasars, and the gas round the Clouds was weighed from what it absorbs from quasars’ light.

The farthest thing that shows is a quasar near the top edge, a clean faint point whose light set out about 9.9 billion years ago. The farthest catalogued in the frame, a quasar at redshift 3.78, in the upper part of the frame, set out 12.1 billion years ago, when the universe was about 1.6 billion years old, but it’s too faint for this picture. NGC 1809, a pale streak at the right edge, is a spiral galaxy seen through the Cloud, about 60 million light-years away, and ESO 56-104, a lilac oval seen through the bar, is about 270 million.

The colours

In the Hubble palette, sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue. Most of the Cloud’s nebulae are cyan and blue here, hydrogen and oxygen together; their edges are gold, where sulphur glows where the gas is shielded, and orange where it’s strongest, at shocks. The bar’s pale glow is the light of old stars, passing through the narrow filters.

The picture

T71 is a Takahashi Epsilon-180ED, a 180 mm Newtonian made for photography, at f/2.8, with a ZWO ASI2600MM Pro, a cooled monochrome camera whose pixels each cover 1.54 arcseconds, at Deep Sky Chile in the Río Hurtado Valley. Its frame is about 2.7 degrees by 1.8, so this is four of them, two by two, overlapping. The picture is 1 hour 40 minutes each through the hydrogen-alpha, oxygen III, and sulphur II filters, across the four panels: 5 hours in all. Joined, it’s 11,003 by 7,101 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
