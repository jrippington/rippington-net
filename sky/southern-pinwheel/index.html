The Southern Pinwheel Galaxy

A barred spiral, almost face on

M83, the Southern Pinwheel, a barred spiral seen almost face on, about 16 million light-years away in Hydra: its pink star-forming clouds, a nucleus that's really a giant star cluster, six supernovae in a century, and small galaxies far behind it, the farthest whose light set out a billion years ago. From a 17-inch telescope in Australia, with its old CCD camera.

The light from M83 left it about 16 million years ago, in the Miocene, during a warm spell when the Earth’s climate was milder than it is now. The light from the two galaxies at the left edge left about 650 million years ago, in the time of the Snowball Earth, when ice may have reached the equator.

This picture is from iTelescope’s T17, a 17-inch reflector at Siding Spring Observatory in Australia, which I used remotely in March 2023. That was before its camera was upgraded: then it had an FLI ProLine PL4710, a CCD camera with a small square sensor of about a million pixels, and the picture is a crop of it. It covers 16 arcminutes of sky by 12, about half the full Moon across, in Hydra. It’s in natural colour, from four filters, luminance, red, green, and blue, an hour through each. The galaxy has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is up and east is to the left. M83 fills the frame, its bright nucleus just left of the middle and above it, a short yellow bar running through it from upper left to lower right, and its arms winding out from the bar’s ends, laced with dark dust and studded with pink clouds where stars are forming, among the pinkest on the arm up the right-hand side. At the left edge are two small galaxies far behind it, ESO 444-85 and PRC C-47, and at the upper right a tiny fuzzy oval, the farthest thing in the picture. The brightest star, HD 118333, near the bottom, is in our own Galaxy. At M83’s distance the frame is about 72,000 light-years across and 57,000 high, and each pixel covers about 70 light-years.

The Southern Pinwheel

M83, NGC 5236, is a barred spiral, tilted only about 24 degrees from face on, so we look almost straight down on its disc. Measurements of the brightest red giants among its stars put it about 16 million light-years away; its Cepheids give about 14.7 million, and you’ll often read 15. Its bright disc is about 60,000 light-years across, though its hydrogen gas reaches much further. It makes stars at about four times the Sun’s mass a year, and dozens of Wolf-Rayet stars are catalogued in its arms: massive young stars stripped to their hot cores, close to exploding. It belongs to a small group of galaxies which, with Centaurus A’s group, makes up one loose complex.

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille found it from the Cape of Good Hope on 23 February 1752, the first galaxy found beyond our own Local Group. Charles Messier, in Paris, catalogued it on 17 February 1781, so low in his sky that the least light to see his micrometer by made it disappear. It’s the most southerly galaxy in his catalogue.

The nucleus and the bar

The bright nucleus is a giant star cluster, about 11 million times the Sun’s mass, from a burst of star formation about 100 million years ago. Round it is a ring of hundreds of young clusters, most only 5 to 7 million years old: the galaxy’s centre is still forming stars fast. For twenty years astronomers have argued over a second, hidden mass a few arcseconds away, behind the dust; it would be lost in the bright core here.

The bar runs from the upper left to the lower right through the nucleus, about 18,000 light-years long, and the arms grow from its ends. In 1834 John Herschel, at the Cape, noted that the nebula was drawn out in just that direction.

Six supernovae

Six supernovae have been seen in M83 since 1923, and for years it held the record for one galaxy, until NGC 6946 passed it. SN 1923A, in the eastern arm, was the first. SN 1957D, in the northern arm, is one of the few supernovae in another galaxy still detectable decades on: the Chandra X-ray telescope found X-rays from it in 2010 and 2011 that probably come from a young pulsar. SN 1968L went off in the crowded centre, beside the nucleus, found by Jack Bennett, an amateur in Pretoria sweeping for comets. SN 1983N, at the lower right, was the brightest, peaking at magnitude 11.7: a star that had lost its hydrogen before it exploded, a type Ib. None has appeared since, and none shows now.

Far beyond

Behind M83 are galaxies much further away. The two at the left edge, the lumpy streak ESO 444-85 and the smaller PRC C-47 just above and right of it, are about 40 times further away than M83: their light set out about 650 million years ago, and they’re at the same redshift as the great Shapley Supercluster, whose nearest clusters are about 2 degrees away on the sky. PRC C-47 is listed as a possible polar-ring galaxy, with a ring of stars and gas at right angles to its body, though that isn’t confirmed; and in April 2026, three years after this picture, a new star, perhaps a supernova, appeared in it.

The farthest thing that shows is a tiny fuzzy oval at the upper right, seen through M83’s faint outer disc: a galaxy whose light set out about a billion years ago. Three other galaxies are catalogued further still, about 3.5 billion light-years away, but they’re too faint for this picture: they were found by accident, because their oxygen’s light, shifted by the expansion of the universe, fell into the filter astronomers used to pick out M83’s star-forming clouds, so they passed for clouds in M83. No quasar is known in the frame.

In front

The brightest star, HD 118333, near the bottom, is a star a little hotter than the Sun, about 760 light-years away. The double star at the lower left isn’t a pair at all: the fainter one is about 4,200 light-years away, five times further than the brighter, and just happens to lie behind it.

The colours

The colours are natural, from separate exposures through red, green, and blue filters, with the detail from a fourth, luminance, which passes all visible light. The centre and the bar are yellow with older stars, the arms bluer with young ones, and the pink knots are hydrogen glowing round newborn stars.

The picture

T17 is a PlaneWave CDK17, a 0.43-metre reflector, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. In March 2023 its camera was an FLI ProLine PL4710, with an e2v CCD47-10 sensor of 1,024 by 1,024 pixels, each 13 microns across, giving 0.92 arcseconds per pixel at the telescope’s 2,912 mm; it was replaced by a ZWO ASI6200MM Pro the following year. The picture is an hour each through luminance, red, green, and blue, four hours in all. It’s 1,016 by 806 pixels, the sensor’s square cropped. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
