The Moon, Nyx

Gassendi

A crater on the shore of the Sea of Moisture

June 2024, 8 min read

Gassendi, a crater 111 km across on the shore of the Sea of Moisture, its floor lifted and cracked from below, which Apollo 17's planners nearly chose to land in. Round it, the lava plain of Mare Humorum, its drowned and broken craters, the rilles and scarps its weight has opened, and night still holding the lower left, where the Sun hadn't yet risen. From my garden in Oxfordshire, through an infrared filter.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, without its corrector, at its full 1,176 mm, with a small ZWO ASI678MC planetary camera and an infrared filter, on the night of 18 June 2024. It covers about 900 km of the Moon by 700, in the south-west of the face we see. Gassendi is also at the edge of my picture of Aristarchus, taken the same night, and the Moon has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

Lunar north is up, tilted to the left, so east, towards the Sea of Crises, is to the right, south is down and to the right, and west to the left. Gassendi is the big crater just left of the middle, with a smaller crater, Gassendi A, breaking its northern rim just above it. Below it to the right is the smooth, dark plain of Mare Humorum, the Sea of Moisture, ringed by drowned and broken craters: Doppelmayer and Vitello on its southern shore, Hippalus and Campanus on its eastern. Down its western side runs the bright line of Rupes Liebig, and west of that is Mersenius. At the top is the edge of the Ocean of Storms, with the flooded bay of Letronne at the upper left, and at the lower left it’s still night. Each pixel covers about 670 metres.

The Sun was rising there. It was 16 degrees above Gassendi, low in the east, on the right, so shadows fall to the left, and craters’ right-hand inner walls are dark. The line of sunrise runs from the left edge down to the bottom; just inside it only rims and peaks catch the light, and beyond it the craters are black. From Oxfordshire the Moon was very low, crossing the meridian at under 17 degrees, in the twilight of a midsummer night. Summer full Moons are always low, and in 2024 they were lower than usual: the Moon was near a major standstill, which comes round every 18.6 years. It was 90 per cent lit, three days before full.

Gassendi

Gassendi is 111 km across, and shallow for its size, about a kilometre and a half to two kilometres deep. Its floor has been lifted and cracked: it’s crossed by a network of fractures, Rimae Gassendi, some of them thousands of metres wide; a few of the largest may show here as faint lines. It’s one of the Moon’s floor-fractured craters, and the most widely held explanation is that magma rose beneath it and spread out under the floor, lifting it and splitting it. Its floor stands above the mare, with central peaks in the middle, and its southern rim, facing Humorum, is low and broken where the lava has lapped against it.

Gassendi A, 32 km across, punches through its northern wall, so it’s much younger, and its rays mark it as one of the Moon’s young craters.

Gassendi nearly had visitors. For the last Apollo landing, Apollo 17, NASA’s planners narrowed the choice to three sites, and ranked Gassendi second, for its central peaks. In February 1972 they chose Taurus-Littrow instead: at Gassendi, a landing even slightly off target might have left the astronauts unable to reach the peaks.

The Sea of Moisture

Mare Humorum is lava filling an impact basin about 420 km across, formed something like 3.9 billion years ago. The lava is over 3 km thick at its centre, and most of it erupted 3.3 to 3.5 billion years ago. Its weight did more: there’s a mascon under it, a concentration of mass found in 1968 from the way it tugged on the Lunar Orbiter spacecraft, and the crust bent under the load, which is the usual explanation for the rilles and scarps that follow its shores. Rimae Hippalus curve round its eastern side, one of them cutting straight through the flooded crater Hippalus. On its western side, Rupes Liebig, a fault scarp about 145 km long, faces the morning Sun and shows as a bright line, with Rimae Mersenius running beside it.

The craters round its shore are half drowned. Doppelmayer has lost the side facing the mare, leaving a horseshoe of wall round its central peak. Vitello has an inner ring round its central peak, another floor lifted from below. Campanus, at the right, is crisp; its twin, Mercator, is just beyond the frame. Mersenius, on the western side, has a domed floor, and with the Sun only about 7 degrees above it, its eastern wall throws a long shadow.

To the west and north

Further west, Billy has a floor as dark as a mare, filled with the same lava, and beside Hansteen is Mons Hansteen, nicknamed the Arrowhead, one of the few places on the Moon built of silica-rich lava, more like a volcanic dome on Earth than the usual basalt. At the upper left, Letronne, 118 km across, has been drowned by the Ocean of Storms, and only its southern wall and a few hills remain. And right at the top edge, north of the small crater Flamsteed, is where Surveyor 1 landed in June 1966, the first American spacecraft to land softly on the Moon. Ranger 7 crashed at the top edge too, in Mare Cognitum, “the sea that has become known”, which was named for it in 1964.

Names

Gassendi is named for Pierre Gassendi (1592–1655), a French astronomer, mathematician, and philosopher, who in November 1631 watched from Paris the transit of Mercury that Kepler had predicted, projecting the Sun’s image onto paper: the first transit of a planet ever seen. He also revived the ancient idea that everything is made of atoms. Riccioli gave his name to the crater in 1651, and named the Sea of Moisture too. Mersenius, nearby, is named for Marin Mersenne, the French mathematician and friar who was Gassendi’s close friend.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, used without its corrector at its own focal length of 1,176 mm, f/4.7. The camera is a ZWO ASI678MC, a small planetary camera that records video, and in front of it was an infrared filter, to keep the atmosphere a bit more still. I captured it in SharpCap, stacked the sharpest frames in AutoStakkert, sharpened them with wavelets, and finished it in Photoshop. It’s in shades of grey. It’s 1,332 by 1,082 pixels.
