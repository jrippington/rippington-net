The Eastern Veil

The bright edge of an old supernova's blast

The Eastern Veil, about 2,400 light-years away in Cygnus: the bright eastern edge of the Cygnus Loop, the shell of a star that exploded about 20,000 years ago, where its blast wave is still running into clouds of gas, oxygen's blue-green glowing just ahead of hydrogen's red, in strands that are rippled sheets seen edge on. Two short July nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from the Eastern Veil left it about 2,400 years ago, around 340 BC, in Britain’s Iron Age. A few years later, around 325 BC, Pytheas of Massalia visited Britain and wrote the earliest account of it that survives, in fragments. In Oxfordshire, Segsbury Camp on the Ridgeway was in use, and the White Horse at Uffington was already centuries old.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over two nights in July 2024. It covers 1.4 degrees of sky by 0.9, nearly three full Moons by two, in Cygnus. It’s in natural colour, from a filter that passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands. The Veil Nebula has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the right, so east is up, west down, and south to the left. The Eastern Veil is the great arc of red and white filaments, its outer edge facing up, to the east; the rest of the supernova remnant it belongs to lies below the frame, its centre far beyond the bottom edge. On the right, NGC 6992 is the brightest part, a whitish ridge sweeping down to the lower right; at the top of the arc is NGC 6995; and on the left, IC 1340 is a tangle of filaments ending in a bright point. Along the arc’s outer edge runs a thin line of blue-green, oxygen, just ahead of hydrogen’s red. Beyond NGC 6992’s edge, a faint red thread is the filament the Hubble telescope has filmed moving. At the Veil’s distance the frame is about 57 light-years across and 38 high, and each pixel covers about 640 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

From Oxfordshire it crosses the meridian about 70 degrees up, in July between about 1.20 and 3.20 in the morning. The finished picture was saved on 19 July, so both nights came before the night of 21 July, when the Sun first got 18 degrees below the horizon here again after midsummer: the sky never got fully dark.

The Cygnus Loop

The Veil is part of the Cygnus Loop, the shell of gas swept up by the blast wave of a star that exploded about 20,000 years ago. On the sky it’s about 2.9 degrees by 3.7, and at its distance about 120 light-years by 150. The star was probably about 15 times the Sun’s mass, by the elements in its debris, which glows in X-rays inside the shell at about 7 million degrees; you’ll also read 20. The light of the explosion itself reached the Earth roughly 20,000 years ago.

Gaia’s measurements of stars just in front of the remnant and just behind it, which show or don’t show its fast-moving gas in their spectra, put it about 2,400 light-years away. A 2024 study questions two of those stars and allows up to about 2,700; older figures ran from about 1,400 to 4,600, which is why you’ll read ages from 5,000 years up.

The remnant’s shape is set by what it has run into. The bright eastern nebulae, the Eastern Veil, are where the blast wave has hit denser clouds of gas; whether those clouds are the wall of a cavity the star blew round itself before it exploded, or clouds that were simply there, is still argued.

Why it glows

There are two kinds of shock here. Where the blast wave runs fast through thin gas, it heats it so quickly that only hydrogen glows, in thin, faint filaments. Where it has slowed to about 100 to 160 kilometres a second in a denser cloud, the heated gas cools within a few hundred years and glows in many colours as it does. Oxygen glows first, near the front, at about 80,000 degrees; hydrogen glows later, further behind, at about 7,000. So oxygen’s light runs just ahead of hydrogen’s: in the Hubble telescope’s images, by 2 or 3 arcseconds. That’s barely a few pixels here, but the same order shows along the whole arc, a thin blue-green edge with red behind it.

The fine strands are the shock seen edge on. The shock front is a thin, rippled sheet, and where a ripple lies along our line of sight we look through a long path of glowing gas and see a sharp line; where it faces us, it looks diffuse. William Blair, one of the astronomers who studied it with Hubble, put it as “twisted ribbons of light”. In NGC 6992 the strands run parallel along the arc; in NGC 6995 and IC 1340 they cross and branch, wrinkled sheets seen at many angles.

The three parts

NGC 6992, the Network Nebula, is the brightest part, its ridge about 22 light-years long, where the blast wave has slowed in a dense cloud. NGC 6995, at the top of the arc, is a tangle of pink-white filaments, its eastern face hung with blue-green. And IC 1340, the southern end, has long, nearly straight filaments sweeping down to a bright point. Together, NGC 6992 and NGC 6995 are Caldwell 33.

Near the arc’s left end is the XA region, the brightest spot in X-rays on the remnant’s eastern edge, where the blast wave has hit a protrusion of denser gas; just above it here, a little to the right, is a dark loop with a bright rim.

Hubble’s moving filament

Beyond NGC 6992’s outer edge, to the right, is a faint, thin red line: one of the fast, thin shocks that glow only in hydrogen’s light. The Hubble telescope photographed it in 1997, 2001, and 2020, for a time-lapse film of the Cygnus Loop, and found it moving outwards at about 240 kilometres a second, with no measurable slowing in 22 years. Even so, it takes about 13 years to move one pixel of my picture.

History

William Herschel found the Eastern Veil on 5 September 1784, by the date in his own catalogue, and described a “branching nebulosity” whose following part “divides into several streams uniting again towards the south”. Two nights later he found the western Veil. His son John catalogued NGC 6995 separately on 7 September 1825, and Truman Safford found IC 1340 on 13 September 1866, with the 18.5-inch refractor of the Dearborn Observatory, noting it was “possibly connected” with NGC 6995; it is, as part of the same shock front. In 1937 Edwin Hubble measured the bright filaments moving outwards, about 0.03 arcseconds a year.

In front and behind

Some of the stars here helped measure the Veil’s distance. Star Y, a bluish-white star in NGC 6992’s red body, about 2,310 light-years away, seemed to show the remnant’s gas in its spectrum, so it must lie just behind; the 2024 study doubts that. KPD 2055+3111, a faint, very hot little star under a filament at the top, about 2,670 light-years away, shows the remnant’s hot oxygen in its ultraviolet spectrum, so it’s behind it. J205601, a cool red giant with a small bow-shaped glow round it, was hoped to be meeting the remnant’s shock, but Gaia puts it about 7,000 light-years away, far behind, and its glow’s origin is still a mystery.

KELT-16, just above the top of NGC 6992’s ridge, about 1,420 light-years away, has a giant planet that orbits it in less than a day, 23.3 hours, one of the shortest years of any hot Jupiter known; it was found in 2017 by the dips it makes in the star’s light. The brightest star, HD 199716, golden with spikes near the top edge, is about 930 light-years away, a giant by its brightness. The nearest are a pair of red dwarfs at the left edge, about 109 light-years away, moving together.

Far beyond

Nine degrees from the plane of the Milky Way, the dust here is fairly thin, but no survey that measures redshifts has covered this patch, so nothing in the frame has a measured distance beyond our Galaxy. Galaxies do show: 2MFGC 15860, a thin streak above the nebula, is a disc galaxy seen edge on, and PGC 55741 a small oval at the left edge; by their colours their light set out about 850 and 530 million years ago. The farthest thing that shows is probably a faint point at the left edge, which Gaia flags as a quasar: if its estimated redshift is right, its light set out about 11.4 billion years ago.

The farthest thing with a measured distance is a star: an RR Lyrae star, a faint dot in NGC 6992’s red glow, below its ridge, an old star that swells and shrinks every 14 hours. All such stars have about the same true brightness, so its faintness gives its distance: about 95,000 light-years, out in the halo below the Galaxy’s disc, about 40 times as far as the Veil.

The colours

The colours are close to natural: the filter passes three bands, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record. Hydrogen’s red light, with nitrogen’s and sulphur’s beside it, falls only on the red pixels; oxygen’s blue-green light falls where the green and blue pixels overlap, so it shows as teal. The stars keep their own colours.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. The picture is 3 hours 16 minutes through an Altair TRI-RGB filter, over two nights in July 2024. It’s 5,710 by 3,732 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
