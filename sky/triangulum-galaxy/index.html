The Triangulum Galaxy

The third great galaxy of our group

The Triangulum Galaxy, M33, about 2.7 million light-years away, the third great galaxy of our Local Group: a loose spiral with no central bulge and no giant black hole, its arms studded with pink clouds of hydrogen where stars are forming, NGC 604 the greatest of them; and, seen through and beyond it, quasars whose light set out nearly 12 billion years ago. Seven nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from M33 left it about 2.7 million years ago, in the late Pliocene, as the great ice sheets of the northern ice ages were beginning to grow. Not long before, in what’s now the Afar region of Ethiopia, lived the owner of the oldest jawbone yet given to our own genus, found at Ledi-Geraru and dated to about 2.8 million years ago.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over seven nights in September 2024. It covers 1.2 degrees of sky by 0.8, about two and a half full Moons by one and a half, in Triangulum. It’s in natural colour, from a filter that passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, with hydrogen’s own red light added from frames through a second filter, so that the clouds where stars are forming stand out pink. The Triangulum Galaxy has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the right, turned a little up, so east is up and west down. The Triangulum Galaxy fills the frame, its long axis running from the lower left to the upper right, its bright little nucleus in the middle. The largest pink knot, at the upper right, is NGC 604; the others strung through the arms are smaller clouds of glowing hydrogen like it. At the galaxy’s distance the frame is about 58,000 light-years across and 38,000 high, and each pixel covers about 11.7 light-years.

It’s in the little constellation of Triangulum, and from Oxfordshire it crosses the meridian 69 degrees up in the south. In September 2024 that was in the small hours, between about 4 am at the start of the month and 2 am at the end, and the Moon was new on the 3rd. M33 is a famous test of a dark sky: at about magnitude 5.7 it’s one of the most distant things the eye can see, but its light is spread over nearly four times the area of the full Moon, so only a really dark site shows it without help.

The Triangulum Galaxy

M33 is the third-largest galaxy of our Local Group, after Andromeda and the Milky Way. Hubble’s measurements of its Cepheid variables, published in 2023, put it about 2.7 million light-years away, a little further than Andromeda; you’ll also read 3 million, and other methods give anything from 2.4 to 3.1. Its bright disc is about 56,000 light-years across, about half the Milky Way’s, with something like 3 to 5 billion times the Sun’s mass in stars, and about 430 billion in all, most of it dark matter.

It’s a pure disc. It has no central bulge, and no giant black hole: Hubble’s look at its nucleus in 2001 found that anything there must be less than about 1,500 times the Sun’s mass, against Andromeda’s 140 million. The bright point at its centre is a dense cluster of stars. And it’s busy: it makes roughly half the Sun’s mass in new stars a year, and per area about ten times as intensely as Andromeda does.

It’s about 720,000 light-years from Andromeda, and whether the two have met is argued. In 2009 astronomers found M33’s outskirts warped into an S, and modelled it as the mark of a pass close to Andromeda about 2.6 billion years ago; in 2017, measurements of its motion across the sky suggested instead that it’s falling towards Andromeda for the first time. A thin stream of hydrogen clouds runs between them.

History

Giovanni Battista Hodierna probably saw it before 1654, as a nebulosity near the Triangle. Charles Messier catalogued it on the night of 25 to 26 August 1764, as an even, whitish light with no stars. William Herschel catalogued it again on 11 September 1784, and gave its greatest cloud a number of its own, which became NGC 604. In 1849 Lord Rosse’s great telescope showed it as a spiral, “full of knots”, one of the first spiral nebulae.

Then it helped show what spiral nebulae are. In 1922 John Duncan found three variable stars in it, the first found in another galaxy; one of them, Var 2, shows here. In 1926 Edwin Hubble published 35 Cepheids in it, with a distance far beyond the Milky Way, though too close, for the same reason as his distance to Andromeda, and with his Andromeda result it helped settle that the spiral nebulae are galaxies of their own.

NGC 604

NGC 604 is a cloud of glowing hydrogen so great that in our Local Group only the Tarantula, in the Large Magellanic Cloud, outshines it. It’s lit by more than 200 of the hottest, most massive kinds of star, some over 100 times the Sun’s mass, about 3.5 to 4 million years old. Here it’s about 1.6 arcminutes across, about 1,300 light-years; you’ll also read 1,500, depending on where its edge is drawn. The James Webb Space Telescope’s pictures of it were released in March 2024.

The pink clouds

The other pink knots are smaller clouds of the same kind, many with catalogue numbers of their own. NGC 595, just right of the nucleus and below it, is the galaxy’s second brightest. NGC 592 and NGC 588 are further down the same side, NGC 588 a neat round shell out in the dark outer disc. IC 131 is right of NGC 592, and further right still, IC 133 is a chain of knots in which, in 1977, astronomers found the first water maser outside our Galaxy, a natural microwave laser; measured again over the years with another in M33, it gave in 2005 the galaxy’s first distance by pure geometry. Beyond it, IC 132 is an isolated pink shell, lit by a cluster less than 3 million years old.

Stars worth a look

A few single stars of M33’s own show. Var C, below and left of the nucleus, is a luminous blue variable, a huge unstable star that has had long bright spells, the latest brightening in 2010 and 2011; Var 2, above the nucleus, is another. Romano’s star, near the top edge, is one that seems to be moving on from that stage towards becoming a Wolf-Rayet star, the first confidently classed so. And at a small knot left of the middle is M33 X-7, a black hole and a hot star that eclipse each other every 3.45 days, the first such pair found; in 2022 their masses were revised to about 11 and 38 times the Sun’s (in 2007, about 16 and 70). The star can’t be picked out here.

Far beyond M33

M33’s disc is thin enough to see through. About 31 of the 50 confirmed quasars in this frame, the bright cores of distant galaxies lit by gas falling into huge black holes, show as faint points, some of them through the galaxy itself. LGGS J0133+3027, left of the middle, shines through M33’s blue disc from 10.3 billion light-years away. MC83 25, at the lower left, was catalogued as a Wolf-Rayet star in M33 until 2011, when its spectrum showed a quasar whose light set out about 11.5 billion years ago; it’s the brightest of the distant ones here.

Some of M33’s supposed star clusters turned out to be galaxies far behind it. The small yellowish fuzz right beside IC 132 is one, about 1.6 billion light-years away; another, in the galaxy’s upper right, is about 1.8 billion. The finest of the background galaxies is the edge-on Z 502-114 at the top right, about 490 million light-years away. The farthest ordinary galaxy that shows, WISEA J0135+3036, near the top edge, is a faint point whose light left it about 6.5 billion years ago.

The farthest thing in the frame, and the farthest that shows, is SDSS J0135+3012, a faint point near the top left corner: a quasar at a redshift of 3.06, whose light set out about 11.7 billion years ago, when the universe was about 2.1 billion years old, more than 7 billion years before the Sun formed.

In front

The bright blue-white star with spikes left of the galaxy, HD 9483, is in our own Galaxy, about 790 light-years away, and the orange one at the top, HD 9687, about 1,420.

The colours

The colours are close to natural: the filter passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record. The arms are blue with young stars, the dust lanes brown. The pink of the knots is hydrogen’s light, from 4 hours through the dual-band filter. That filter passes ordinary starlight too, so the starlight is taken away (continuum subtraction: a broadband image, scaled so that the stars match, is subtracted), leaving the hydrogen’s light alone, which I added to the colour picture. Only the glowing gas gets the extra red.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. The picture is 12 hours through an Altair TRI-RGB filter, and 4 hours through an Antlia dual-band filter that passes hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III, over seven nights in September 2024. It’s 4,940 by 3,240 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
