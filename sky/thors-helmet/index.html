Thor's Helmet

A bubble with wings, blown by a dying star

Thor's Helmet, NGC 2359, a bubble about 18 light-years across blown by the wind of a dying star, about 13,400 light-years away in Canis Major: the helmet glowing blue with oxygen, a wing of glowing gas rising on each side, and dark clouds of molecular gas the bubble is pushing into. From a 20-inch telescope in Chile, in the Hubble palette.

The light from Thor’s Helmet left it about 13,400 years ago, around 11,400 BC. Britain was warming after the last ice age, and people were using the caves at Creswell Crags, on the border of Nottinghamshire and Derbyshire, where engravings of animals and birds, at least 12,800 years old, are Britain’s oldest known rock art.

This picture is from iTelescope’s T72, a 20-inch reflector at Deep Sky Chile, which I used remotely in March 2023. It covers 21 arcminutes of sky by 26, a little smaller than the full Moon, in Canis Major. It’s in the Hubble palette, from three filters that each pass the light of one element: hydrogen, oxygen, and sulphur. Thor’s Helmet has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left, so east is down, west up, and south to the right. Thor’s Helmet is the blue bubble in the middle, a lumpy dome of crossing filaments, and the white star inside its upper left is WR 7, the dying star that blew it. With west up, as here, it looks most like its name: one wing rises to the upper left and the other to the upper right, edged with orange, and below the helmet a long tail runs down to the bottom edge. Dark knots of dust line the bubble’s right-hand side. The bright golden star with spikes at the lower middle is HD 57057. At the nebula’s distance, about 13,400 light-years, the frame is about 81 light-years across and 102 high, and each pixel covers about 1,500 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

The nebula lies right on the plane of the Milky Way, which crosses the frame itself; we’re looking along it, outwards, away from the centre of the Galaxy.

Thor’s Helmet

Thor’s Helmet, NGC 2359, is a bubble blown by the wind of WR 7, like the Crescent Nebula, in stages. While the star was young, its wind and radiation cleared a great cavity round it: radio maps show a shell of hydrogen nearly 200 light-years long, far bigger than this frame, about 2 million years old. Later, as a supergiant, perhaps a luminous blue variable, it shed mass in slow, dense, uneven outbursts. Now, as a Wolf-Rayet star, its fast wind is sweeping that material, and the gas round it, into the bubble that shows here.

Unlike the Crescent, most of it isn’t the star’s own. Radio measurements find hundreds of times the Sun’s mass of glowing gas in the whole nebula, far more than the star could have shed: it’s mostly interstellar gas, swept up and lit by the star, and only some filaments near the star are enriched with material from inside it.

The bubble itself, the helmet, is about 4.5 arcminutes across, about 18 light-years at Gaia’s distance. You’ll often read 30 light-years, which measures a larger outline at a distance of 15,000. With its wings, the whole nebula here is about 63 light-years across. It’s expanding at about 15 to 30 kilometres a second, so slowly that its edge takes two to five centuries to move one pixel of my picture, and its age is poorly known: something like 50,000 to 250,000 years.

Gaia’s measurement of WR 7 puts it about 13,400 light-years away, between about 12,100 and 15,200. You’ll often read 15,000, and older studies used anything from about 12,000 to 16,000, which changes their sizes and masses.

WR 7

WR 7, HD 56925, is a Wolf-Rayet star, a massive star stripped to its hot interior. How hot depends on the model: the two latest studies put it at about 112,000 and 145,000 degrees at the base of its wind, and its visible surface, out in the wind, is about 75,000 degrees. Its wind blows at about 1,300 to 1,600 kilometres a second. It shines about 130,000 to 230,000 times as brightly as the Sun, which is faint for a Wolf-Rayet star, and the newest study, of 2026, puts its mass now at only about 6 times the Sun’s: it may be a star of middling mass that lost its outer layers, not one of the giants. NASA’s TESS satellite sees it vary every 2.64 days, probably pulsations and structures in its wind; no companion has been found.

It’s off-centre, about half an arcminute up and to the left of the bubble’s middle, to the north-west. Gaia shows it moving at about 50 kilometres a second relative to the gas round it, towards the north-north-west, which would explain it: it’s moving towards that side.

The wings and the clouds

The wings aren’t part of the bubble: they’re the glowing interstellar gas round it, lit by the same star. One sweeps up to the upper left, the north-west, and the other to the upper right, the south-west, with a second branch curving off to the right. Below the helmet, the north-east side has broken out into a lobe, and the tail runs on down to the bottom edge, the east, ending in orange.

The nebula is lopsided because of what’s round it. To the east and south, below and right of the bubble here, are dense clouds of molecular gas that hold it back, while on the other sides it expands freely; and the star’s earlier outbursts were probably uneven too.

The dark knots on the bubble’s right-hand side, the south, edged with orange, are part of a cloud of cold molecular gas the bubble is pushing into, and a dark patch below the bubble, to the east, is probably part of another. Radio telescopes find the southern cloud’s gas warmer and more stirred up than the gas round it, up to about 60 to 80 degrees above absolute zero, against about 10: a shock from the bubble has hit it. Among its molecules is ammonia, released from the dust grains by the shock, and in 2001 that was the first molecule other than carbon monoxide and hydrogen found round a Wolf-Rayet star. Beyond the dark ridge a smaller bubble bulges out, its edge a thin bright arc, and further out an orange arc curves round the lower right.

Hot gas and fast particles

Inside, the bubble is full of hot gas. The XMM-Newton X-ray telescope found it at about 2 million degrees, with a little at about 57 million, filling the helmet and the lobe below it: cooler than the star’s wind alone would make it, because gas from the nebula has mixed in. And in 2026 radio astronomers found that some of its radio waves come from particles accelerated nearly to the speed of light in the wind’s shocks: it’s only the second bubble round a Wolf-Rayet star known to do that.

History

William Herschel found it on 31 January 1785, from Datchet, near Windsor, with his reflector of 18.7 inches, and described “a broad elongated nebulosity, in the form of a parallelogram”. His son John, at the Cape, likened it to the profile of a bust. A knot on its upper left rim, NGC 2361, was catalogued separately by Wilhelm Tempel in 1877, and IC 468, a fainter part to the west, by Guillaume Bigourdan in 1887; catalogues disagree on where IC 468 is. Who first called it Thor’s Helmet isn’t recorded; some call it the Duck Nebula. In 2012 the European Southern Observatory photographed it with the Very Large Telescope for its 50th anniversary, the target chosen by the public.

Far beyond

Looking along the plane of the Milky Way, through this much dust, anything beyond the Galaxy is dimmed by several hundred times or more. The farthest thing known in the frame is invisible: a radio source at the right, which the Fermi telescope also sees in gamma rays, probably a blazar, an active galaxy whose jet points almost at us. No one has measured how far away it is.

The farthest thing that shows is probably a faint fuzzy smudge at the lower right, level with HD 57057, broader than the stars round it: a galaxy, by its look, whose colours suggest its light set out about 630 million years ago, about when the last of the “Snowball Earth” glaciations ended. Neither its distance nor whether it’s a galaxy has been confirmed. The farthest star with a well-measured distance is a luminous red giant at the upper left, about 21,400 light-years away, more than one and a half times as far as the nebula.

The stars

The brightest star, HD 57057, is a cool, very large star about 10,200 light-years away, classed a giant by one catalogue and a supergiant by another; it shines about 30,000 times as brightly as the Sun. HD 57083, just below it, is a yellow giant about 1,700 light-years away. The nearest star in the picture is a red dwarf on the orange arc, only 305 light-years away. TYC 5407-391-1, at the upper right, an orange star about 530 light-years away, is moving at about 104 kilometres a second relative to the Sun, about four times as fast as most stars near us. And at the left edge, TOI-2869 dims by about 1 per cent every 1.72 days: NASA lists it as a candidate planet, though Gaia’s measurements hint that it isn’t a single star.

The colours

In the Hubble palette, sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue; there’s almost no green here, so blue means oxygen, and orange and gold hydrogen and sulphur. Oxygen glows in this line only once it has lost two electrons, which takes the hard ultraviolet of a star as hot as WR 7, so the bubble, the wings, and the tail are blue. The orange is where the gas meets denser clouds: along the dark ridge, the wings’ outer edges, and the arc at the lower right.

The picture

T72 is a PlaneWave CDK20, a 0.51-metre reflector, at f/6.8, with an FLI ML16200 CCD camera, at Deep Sky Chile in the Río Hurtado Valley. The picture is 6 hours 20 minutes through the hydrogen-alpha filter, 6 hours through oxygen III, and 4 hours 30 minutes through sulphur II: 16 hours 50 minutes in all. It’s 3,466 by 4,380 pixels, each covering 0.36 arcseconds. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
