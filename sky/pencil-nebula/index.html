The Pencil Nebula

A shock seen edge on

March 2023, 9 min read

On this page

  3. The colours of a shock

  5. Far beyond

The Pencil Nebula, NGC 2736, the brightest edge of the Vela supernova remnant's shock, about 940 light-years away: a sheet of gas seen edge on, its sharp blue front where a fragment of the exploded star is ploughing into a cloud, gold filaments cooling behind it, and, hidden behind the Milky Way's dust, galaxies a billion light-years away. From a 17-inch telescope in Australia.

The light from the Pencil left it about 940 years ago, around 1083: in England, the time of the Domesday survey of 1086, when William the Conqueror’s White Tower was rising in London. The supernova’s own light had reached the Earth about 11,000 years ago.

This picture is from iTelescope’s T32, a 17-inch reflector at Siding Spring Observatory in Australia, which I used remotely in March 2023, with the CCD camera it had then. It covers 36 arcminutes of sky by 35, a little more than the full Moon, in Vela. It’s from three narrowband filters, which pass only the light of hydrogen, oxygen, and sulphur, nearly 14 hours in all, in the Hubble palette. The Pencil is part of the Vela supernova remnant, which has its own gallery, and my picture of the whole remnant shows it on its left-hand edge.

What’s in the frame

North is to the right, so east is up, west down, and south to the left. The Pencil is the bright, straight filament slanting across the upper half, its sharp blue edge on top, facing east, the way the shock is moving; its brightest stretch is near the upper right, by the bright white star HD 77433. Behind the edge, below it, is a web of gold filaments, and below that a fan of fainter blue ones trails down and to the left across the frame. At 940 light-years the frame is about 10 light-years across, and each pixel covers about 190 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

The Pencil

The Pencil, NGC 2736, is the brightest piece of the Vela supernova remnant’s shock, the blast of a star that exploded about 11,000 years ago. It lies along the outer edge of an X-ray fragment called shrapnel D, one of the clumps of the exploded star’s own matter, rich in oxygen, neon, and magnesium, that have flown out beyond the main blast wave. In front of it the clump drives a bow shock, like a bullet’s, into an ordinary interstellar cloud, and that’s what glows here.

Its sharp edge runs right across the frame, about 8.6 light-years long, and its bright part about 4. It looks so thin and sharp because it’s a rippled sheet of shocked gas and we see it edge on: looking along the sheet, the line of sight runs a long way through the thin glowing layer, so it’s bright; anywhere the sheet faces us, we look straight through it, and it’s faint.

The shock is moving at about 100 to 180 kilometres a second, the faster in the hottest gas, which shows only in ultraviolet. You’ll read that it’s 800 light-years away, an older figure; the distance used here, 940, is from the Vela pulsar’s parallax, as on the page for the whole remnant.

The colours of a shock

In the Hubble palette, sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue, so gold means hydrogen and sulphur together, and blue means oxygen. Across the Pencil the colours run in bands. Right at the edge is a sharp blue line: oxygen glows in the hot gas just behind the shock, and only a fast shock heats it enough. Then, a little under a minute of arc behind it, hydrogen and sulphur catch up, and the band turns white and gold, as the gas cools. The colours are a thermometer, hot at the front, cooling behind; studies of the Pencil’s spectrum find the same bands, oxygen’s edge leading, hydrogen brightest about a minute behind.

Sulphur glowing strongly against hydrogen is itself the mark of a shock: in a cloud lit by stars it’s much weaker. Further back, the fan of blue filaments trailing down the frame is the part the European Southern Observatory likened to a witch’s broom.

History

John Herschel found it at the Cape of Good Hope on 1 March 1835, with his reflector of 18¼ inches, almost the same size as T32’s mirror. He saw it only once, and called it “an extraordinary long narrow ray of excessively feeble light”: it’s still sometimes called Herschel’s Ray. Its popular name comes from its shape. The Hubble telescope photographed a small part of its brightest stretch in 2002.

Far beyond

The Pencil lies almost exactly on the plane of the Milky Way, behind so much dust that galaxies beyond it are dimmed by about seven magnitudes, a factor of several hundred. Nothing extragalactic shows. But radio telescopes, which see through dust, have found some. A pair of galaxies near the bottom right, 2MASX J08580941−4548126 and its neighbour, about 130 million light-years away, were picked up by the radio glow of their hydrogen. And South Africa’s MeerKAT array found two more by the same means, invisible here: one left of the middle, probably the farthest thing known in the frame, whose light set out about 1.13 billion years ago, and one near the Pencil’s lower end, about 800 million.

The farthest thing that shows is a star: a faint orange dot in the lower middle, about 14,400 light-years away by Gaia’s measurement, reddened by the dust in front of it. A pulsating red giant, OGLE GD-LPV-558, at the lower left, is further still, about 37,500 light-years, out in the Milky Way’s outer disc, but it’s too faint here.

In front

The brightest star, HD 77433, near the top, is about 490 light-years away, in front of the Pencil; its light left it in the reign of Henry VIII. The orange star on the bright streak is behind it, about 3,400 light-years away.

The picture

T32 is a PlaneWave CDK17, a 0.43-metre reflector, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. In March 2023 its camera had a KAF-16803 sensor, 4,096 pixels square, each 9 microns across, giving 0.64 arcseconds per pixel: iTelescope’s records show an FLI ProLine 16803 until December 2022 and a Moravian G4-16000, with the same sensor, from March 2023. The picture is 4 hours 55 minutes through the hydrogen-alpha filter, 4 hours 40 minutes through oxygen III, and 4 hours 5 minutes through sulphur II: 13 hours 40 minutes in all. It’s 3,368 by 3,301 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
