The Carina Mosaic

From the Running Chicken to Eta Carinae

Ten degrees of the southern Milky Way, from the Running Chicken Nebula to the Carina Nebula, in six panels. Looking this way, we're looking along one of the Galaxy's spiral arms, so the sky is crowded with clouds of glowing gas and the young clusters that light them, at distances from a few hundred light-years to more than twenty thousand.

The light in this picture spans a long stretch of history. u Carinae’s set out in the 1920s. The Southern Pleiades’ left around the 1530s, in Tudor England. The Wishing Well’s left in the fifth century AD, and the Carina Nebula’s and the Running Chicken’s about 7,500 years ago. NGC 3603’s set out about 23,000 years ago, near the height of the last ice age, and Gum 35’s a little earlier.

This picture is six frames from iTelescope’s T8, a wide-field refractor at Siding Spring in Australia, joined into one, taken in early February 2023. It covers about 10 degrees of sky by 7, twenty full Moons across and thirteen high, from the Running Chicken Nebula at lower left to the Carina Nebula at upper right. It’s made from three narrowband filters, which pass only the light of hydrogen, sulphur, and oxygen, and the stars’ colours were made from the same three. Three of the things in it have pages of their own, with closer pictures: the core of the Carina Nebula, the Statue of Liberty Nebula, and the Running Chicken Nebula.

What’s in the frame

North is up and east is to the left, as in the other pictures. The plane of the Milky Way runs diagonally across the picture, entering low on the left-hand edge and leaving at the top right-hand corner, so the frame runs along about 11 degrees of it. Most of what you can see is close to that line.

All of it is far south, between about 57 and 64 degrees south of the celestial equator. From Britain none of it rises: the Carina Nebula only clears the horizon from south of about 30 degrees north, the latitude of the Canary Islands. From anywhere south of about 30 degrees south, which takes in Siding Spring, Sydney, Cape Town and Santiago, the Carina Nebula never sets. The middle of the picture is due south at midnight in early March, so it’s best in the evenings from about March to May.

Looking along the Carina arm

Why is this part of the sky so crowded? Because of the direction we’re looking in. The Sun is in the Milky Way’s disc, between two of its main spiral arms, and looking towards Carina we look almost along one of them, the Carina arm (the southern part of the Carina–Sagittarius arm). Its tangent, where our line of sight just grazes it, is a little beyond the picture’s right-hand edge. So every line of sight in this picture runs a long way through the arm, and then crosses it again on its far side, and the arms are where the Galaxy’s gas and young stars are.

Most of what glows here is on the near side of the arm, 7,500 to 9,000 light-years away. A few things are on the far side, more than 20,000 light-years away, and several of the brightest clusters are in front of the arm altogether, much nearer. Gaia, the European Space Agency’s star-mapping satellite, has measured most of these distances:

The Carina Nebula and its neighbours

The brightest thing in the picture, at upper right, is the Carina Nebula, one of the largest and brightest nebulae in the sky. Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille found it from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751 or 1752. It’s lit by about 65 of the hottest kind of star, the O type, in a string of young clusters, and NASA’s Chandra X-ray telescope has found about 14,000 X-ray sources in it, most of them young stars. Gaia puts its clusters, and the Gem Cluster and the Gabriela Mistral Nebula beside it, at the same distance to within about 2 per cent, so they’re one physical complex, about 7,500 light-years away. My picture of its core shows Eta Carinae, the Homunculus and the Keyhole close up; here they’re in the brightest part, round Eta Carinae.

Above it and to the right is the Gabriela Mistral Nebula, NGC 3324, a bubble of glowing gas blown by three O stars at its centre. The dark edge of its rim is said to look like a face in profile, and the nebula is named for the Chilean poet Gabriela Mistral, who won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1945. James Dunlop found it from Parramatta, near Sydney, in May 1826. Part of the same rim is the “Cosmic Cliffs” in one of the first pictures from the James Webb Space Telescope, released in July 2022.

Just above that, the bright cluster near the top edge is the Gem Cluster, NGC 3293, visible to the naked eye. Henry Chamberlain Russell, the Government Astronomer of New South Wales, named it in 1879: he described its bright stars curving like an M, a miniature Southern Cross in the middle, and a bright red star at its foot, which is presumably the red supergiant V361 Carinae. It’s somewhere between about 8 and 20 million years old (the studies disagree), and it has eleven pulsating stars of the β Cephei kind, more than any other known cluster.

Westerlund 2, in the corner

In the top right-hand corner is Westerlund 2, in the nebula RCW 49: one of the most massive young clusters in the Milky Way, about 36,000 times the Sun’s mass and no more than about two million years old. Bengt Westerlund recognised it as a cluster around 1960, and Hubble’s picture for its 25th anniversary, in 2015, was of it. It contains WR 20a, a pair of Wolf-Rayet stars of about 82 times the Sun’s mass each, eclipsing each other every 3.7 days. Its distance has been argued over for decades, with estimates from about 9,000 to 26,000 light-years; Gaia and Hubble now agree on about 14,000. In my picture it’s a small blue-green patch near the corner.

The Wishing Well

Above the middle of the picture, the swarm of blue-white stars in a darker patch of sky is the Wishing Well, NGC 3532, one of the finest open clusters in the sky, and easy to see with the naked eye. The European Southern Observatory explains its name: its stars look like silver coins dropped into a well. It’s about 1,550 light-years away and about 300 million years old, with more than 660 confirmed members and probably over a thousand, as rich as the Pleiades, and its seven red giants are the orange stars among them. Lacaille found it from the Cape, and John Herschel is often quoted as calling it “the most brilliant object of the kind I have ever seen”. It was also the first thing Hubble’s Wide Field and Planetary Camera photographed, on 20 May 1990.

The bright yellow star to its lower left is x Carinae, a yellow hypergiant, one of the largest and most luminous kinds of star there is. It’s about 8,500 light-years away, more than five times further than the cluster, and nothing to do with it.

NGC 3572 and the Southern Tadpoles

Below and to the left of the Wishing Well, the blue nebula with dark pillars in it is Gum 37, round the young cluster NGC 3572, about 8,000 light-years away. The cluster is 3 to 5 million years old, but stars have been forming there for longer: the European Southern Observatory found stars more than ten million years old in it still gathering gas. The two dark pillars are known as the Southern Tadpoles, a name from the astrophotographer Josep Drudis. The bright star just to the cluster’s left is y Carinae, a supergiant in the same group. The cluster Trumpler 18, just below, looks like part of it, but it’s in front, about 4,700 light-years away, and much older.

The Statue of Liberty, NGC 3603 and Gum 35

Below NGC 3572, left of centre, the bright curled loop is the Statue of Liberty Nebula, about 9,000 light-years away, on the near side of the arm; my picture of it shows it close up. The small, compact knot just to its left is NGC 3603, one of the most massive young clusters in the Milky Way, and in the sky its neighbour. But NGC 3603 is about 23,000 light-years away, on the far side of the arm. It looks small because it’s two and a half times further away, and faint because its light comes through three or four magnitudes of dust. At its heart is a pair of stars of about 93 and 70 times the Sun’s mass, eclipsing each other every 3.8 days, as massive as any pair weighed directly in our Galaxy. John Herschel found NGC 3603 and most of the Statue of Liberty’s pieces from the Cape on 14 March 1834, and took NGC 3603 for a possible globular cluster; that night he found seven or eight new things in this part of the picture.

Just right of the centre of the picture, among dark lanes, the faint patch of blue-green glow is probably Gum 35, a nebula at about NGC 3603’s distance on the far side of the arm. At about 24,000 light-years, it’s probably the most distant nebula that shows here.

The Pearl Cluster and the Running Chicken

On the left-hand edge, the bright, compact cluster is the Pearl Cluster, NGC 3766, about 6,500 light-years away and about 20 million years old. It has more stars of the Be type, fast-spinning stars throwing off discs of gas, than most clusters, and they come and go: a study in 2008 found that most of them had gained or lost their discs between observations. In 2013 the European Southern Observatory found 36 stars in it varying in brightness where no variable stars had been expected. The name is recent: it seems to have come from the amateur astronomer Ray Palmer, in 2006.

Below it, the large blue and gold bubble is the Running Chicken Nebula, about 7,600 light-years away, with Thackeray’s globules, small dark clouds, in front of its young cluster; my picture of it shows them close up. The bright star above and to its right, λ Centauri, is much nearer, about 400 light-years away. To the nebula’s right, the two smaller round nebulae are RCW 60, at about the same distance.

The Southern Pleiades

On the bottom edge, below the Carina Nebula, the brightest star in the picture is θ Carinae, at the heart of the Southern Pleiades, IC 2602. Lacaille, cataloguing it, compared the stars round θ to the Pleiades, and the comparison has stuck. The cluster is only about 490 light-years away, so it’s spread wide, and only its northern half is in the frame. It’s between about 30 and 46 million years old, but θ Carinae looks younger than that: it’s a blue straggler, a star that has probably gained mass from a close companion and been made over as a hotter, bluer one. A young planet has been found orbiting one of the cluster’s other stars.

What doesn’t show

Some of the most interesting things in this field are invisible in the picture. The very middle of it is a pulsar, PSR J1101−6101, leaving the supernova remnant it was born in at more than 1,000 kilometres a second, with a jet of X-rays about 36 light-years long: X-ray astronomers call it the Lighthouse Nebula. Centaurus X-3, the first X-ray pulsar found, is a faint star here, about 20,000 light-years away. And there are eight known supernova remnants in the frame, but only one shows clearly: G292.0+1.8, a remnant about 3,000 years old, left of the middle near the top. It’s rich in oxygen and has almost no hydrogen, and the small patch of pure blue (oxygen) light at its position is very probably its brightest debris.

The picture

T8 is a Takahashi FSQ-106ED, a 106 mm refractor built for photography, with a monochrome CCD camera, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. Each of its frames is about 4 degrees square, so the mosaic is three frames across and two down, overlapping. The picture is from 8 hours 50 minutes through the Hα filter, 8 hours 1 minute through SII, and 7 hours 51 minutes through OIII, across the six panels: nearly 25 hours in all. Joined, it’s 10,642 by 7,161 pixels, each covering 3.5 arcseconds. I processed it in PixInsight.

The colours are the Hubble palette: sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue. Over most of the picture hydrogen and sulphur dominate, which comes out gold and olive. Blue marks oxygen ionised twice over, which takes the ultraviolet light of the hottest stars, so it shows where they are: the heart of the Carina Nebula, the Gabriela Mistral Nebula, Gum 37, and the inside of the Running Chicken’s bubble. Narrowband filters don’t give stars their own colours, so here the stars’ colours were made from the same three filters: they’re an approximation.
