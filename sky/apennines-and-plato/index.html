The Apennines, Archimedes and Plato

Sunset on the shore of the Sea of Showers

June 2024, 10 min read

The great ring of mountains round the Sea of Showers, at sunset: the Apennines, the rim of a basin blasted out about 3.9 billion years ago, where Apollo 15 landed; Archimedes, Autolycus and Aristillus out on the lava; the Alps and their straight valley; Plato; and the Caucasus at the edge of night. From my garden in Oxfordshire, through an infrared filter.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, without its corrector, at its full 1,176 mm, with a small ZWO ASI678MC planetary camera and an infrared filter, on the night of 27 June 2024. It covers about 1,700 km of the Moon by 960, along the eastern shore of the Sea of Showers, where the Apennines, the Caucasus and the Alps ring the great Imbrium basin, with Archimedes and Plato out on its lava. The Moon has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

The picture is on its side: lunar north is to the right, tilted a little down, so east, towards the Sea of Crises, is down, south is to the left and west is up. The long, bright range running down from the top, left of the middle, is the Apennines, from the crater Eratosthenes at its top end to its northern end, where Apollo 15 landed. To their right, out on the smooth lava of Mare Imbrium, the Sea of Showers, is the big flat-floored crater Archimedes, with Timocharis above it and Autolycus and Aristillus below. Further right are the lone peaks of Mons Piton and Mons Pico, the shallow crater Cassini lower down, the blocky Alps at the lower right, cut by the straight Alpine Valley, and the dark oval of Plato near the right edge. Along the bottom the peaks of the Caucasus stand at the edge of night, and the bottom left corner is already dark. At the far left, behind the Apennines, are the smaller seas of Mare Vaporum and Sinus Medii, near the middle of the face we see. Each pixel covers about 620 metres.

The Sun was setting here, low in the lunar west, which is at the top, so shadows fall downwards: the Apennines’ steep inner faces are lit, and inside each crater the shadow lies under its upper wall. It was 22 degrees above Eratosthenes, 14 above Archimedes and Plato, under 8 at the Apollo 15 site, and only 2 over the Caucasus, where just the summits still catch it. From Oxfordshire the Moon rose at twenty to one in the morning, British Summer Time, and had climbed to about 30 degrees by dawn, in the twilight of a midsummer night. It was 60 per cent lit, six days after full.

The Imbrium basin

About 3.9 billion years ago something large hit the Moon here and blasted out the Imbrium basin, roughly 1,200 km across. The mountains in this picture mark its edge: the Apennines, the Caucasus and the Alps, with the Carpathians beyond the top edge. Over the next billion years and more, lava welled up and flooded it, making Mare Imbrium, the Sea of Showers, and burying all but the tops of an inner ring of mountains. Those are the lone peaks out on the lava: Mons Piton, about 2.3 km high, Mons Pico, about 2.4 km, and the short chain of Montes Spitzbergen to the right of Archimedes. With the Sun this low, each throws a long shadow down the picture.

The Apennines and Apollo 15

The Apennines run for about 600 km, and they’re among the highest mountains on the Moon: the highest, Mons Huygens, rises about 5.3 km above the lava at its foot, by one recent measurement. They’re the raised edge of the basin, so their steep side faces into it, lit here by the setting Sun, with their shadows reaching down across the plain.

At their northern end, in a bay of lava at the foot of Mons Hadley, Apollo 15 landed on 30 July 1971. David Scott and James Irwin made three moonwalks, 19 hours in all, and drove the first of the Lunar Roving Vehicles about 28 km, up the slopes of Hadley Delta and to the edge of Rima Hadley, the winding channel of an old lava flow. They brought back 77 kg of rock, among it the Genesis Rock, a piece of anorthosite from the Moon’s early crust about 4 billion years old. At the end of the last moonwalk Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather together, and with no air to slow the feather, they hit the ground together. Here the landing site is a smooth, darker patch at the foot of the mountains, with Mons Hadley’s sunlit face beside it and the northern part of the rille a thin dark line; the spacecraft is far too small to see.

Archimedes and its neighbours

Archimedes, 81 km across, is the largest crater on the Sea of Showers, and its floor is as smooth as the plain round it because the same lava flooded it. That makes it a clock of sorts: it’s younger than the basin it sits in, and older than the lava that filled it. Autolycus, 39 km across, and Aristillus, 54 km, are much younger, with faint rays, and Aristillus’s rays cross Autolycus’s, so it’s the younger of the two. Some of the rock Apollo 15 brought back may have been thrown out by them, and its ages point to about 2.1 billion years for Autolycus and 1.3 for Aristillus, though counting the craters on Autolycus’s own ejecta gives a much older age, and the question is still open. Here Aristillus’s central peaks stand up out of the shadow filling its floor.

On the plain between Archimedes and Autolycus, in September 1959, the Soviet Luna 2 became the first spacecraft to reach the Moon, by crashing into it; nobody has found its crater for certain. Cassini, lower down, is a shallow ring flooded with lava, with two sharp younger craters inside it. It’s named for Giovanni Domenico Cassini and his son Jacques, astronomers in Paris.

Eratosthenes and Timocharis

At the top end of the Apennines is Eratosthenes, 59 km across and about 3.4 km deep, with terraced walls and central peaks. The faint streaks across the top left of the picture are rays from Copernicus, just beyond the top edge, and they lie across Eratosthenes, while Eratosthenes’s own debris lies on the lava. In the 1960s that made the two craters the markers for the Moon’s two youngest ages, and the second youngest, from about 3.2 to 1.1 billion years ago, is still called the Eratosthenian Period. Timocharis, 34 km across, out on the lava above Archimedes, was long mapped as one of the youngest craters, until later lava flows were found lapping against it.

The Alps and Plato

The Alps, at the lower right, are a blocky range between the Sea of Showers and the Sea of Cold, cut straight through by the Alpine Valley, a trench about 155 km long and up to 10 km wide, floored with lava. It’s probably a strip of ground dropped between two faults and later flooded; a thin channel winds along its floor, too fine to see here.

Plato, 101 km across, is beyond the Alps, its floor flooded with lava so dark that Hevelius called it the Greater Black Lake. With the Sun setting, the peaks on its western rim throw jagged shadows across that floor. It’s also in my picture of Sinus Iridum, taken nine nights earlier, with the Sun rising on it.

Names

Most of the craters here carry the names Riccioli gave them on his map of 1651, after Greeks: Archimedes, Plato, Eratosthenes, who measured the size of the Earth, and the astronomers Aristillus and Autolycus. The mountain ranges are older. Johannes Hevelius, in 1647, named the Moon’s features after places on Earth, and of nearly 300 of his names only about ten survive, among them the Apennines and the Alps. Mons Hadley is named for John Hadley, the British instrument maker who invented the octant, and Mons Huygens for Christiaan Huygens, the Dutch astronomer.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, used without its corrector at its own focal length of 1,176 mm, f/4.7. The camera is a ZWO ASI678MC, a small planetary camera that records video, and in front of it was an infrared filter, to keep the atmosphere a bit more still. I captured it in SharpCap, stacked the sharpest frames in AutoStakkert, sharpened them with wavelets, and finished it in Photoshop. It’s in shades of grey. It’s 2,703 by 1,544 pixels, and the closer view is a crop of it.
