Running Chicken Nebula

Small dark clouds in the glare of a young cluster

January 2023, 9 min read

A cloud of glowing hydrogen about 7,600 light-years away in Centaurus, lit by a young cluster of hot, massive stars. In front of the glow float Thackeray's globules, small dark clouds of gas and dust that the starlight is evaporating, and pushing away from the cluster as it does. The name is informal, and nobody agrees which part of it is the chicken.

The light from the nebula left it about 7,600 years ago, around 5,600 BC, about the same time as the light in my picture of the Carina Nebula’s core. λ Centauri’s light, in the same frame, is only about 400 years old: it left the star around 1620, the year the Mayflower sailed from Plymouth.

This picture is a patch of sky 55 arcminutes square, nearly two full Moons across, taken with iTelescope’s T31, a 20-inch reflector at Siding Spring in Australia, in January 2023. It’s made from three narrowband filters, which pass only the light of hydrogen, oxygen, and sulphur, with 3 hours 15 minutes through each. The nebula has its own gallery, and it’s also in the lower left of my wide-field mosaic of the region, which runs from here to the Carina Nebula.

What’s in the frame

The nebula is in Centaurus, about 63 degrees south of the celestial equator, so from Britain it never rises. From Siding Spring, at 31 degrees south, it never sets: it circles the south celestial pole, and at its highest it’s about 58 degrees above the horizon.

At about 7,600 light-years, the frame is about 120 light-years across. Each pixel covers 1.1 arcseconds, about 2,500 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun at that range. The bright star with spikes at top right, λ Centauri, has nothing to do with the nebula. It’s about 400 light-years away, roughly nineteen times nearer, and just happens to lie in the same direction.

Which part is the chicken

Nobody seems to know who first saw a chicken in it. The earliest use of the name I’ve found is a NASA picture from its WISE infrared telescope, released in 2010. In 2011 the European Southern Observatory released a picture of the nebula and invited people to mark the chicken on it, noting that there was “some disagreement” about which part looked like one. In 2023, releasing another, it put the bird in the brightest region of the nebula, “where some people see the chicken’s head and others its rear end”. I can’t see it either way, but the name has stuck.

IC 2944, IC 2948, and the rest

The catalogue numbers aren’t much clearer. Royal Frost found the nebula in 1904, on a photograph taken on 5 May with the 24-inch Bruce telescope at Harvard’s southern station in Arequipa, Peru. He listed two objects: a large nebula around λ Centauri, which became IC 2944 in the Index Catalogue, and a large nebulous patch to the south-east, which became IC 2948. Then, in 1931, Per Collinder catalogued the group of bright stars at the nebula’s heart, called it IC 2944, and the name moved to the cluster. Today “IC 2944” can mean the nebula near λ Centauri, the cluster, or the whole complex, and the European Southern Observatory calls the brightest part, in the middle of my picture, IC 2948. The whole region of glowing gas, about a square degree of sky, is also RCW 62 and Gum 42, and it’s number 100 in Patrick Moore’s Caldwell catalogue.

The stars that light it

The cluster at the centre, Collinder 249, is the one most papers call IC 2944. It’s about 3 to 5 million years old, about the age of Trumpler 16 in the Carina Nebula or a little older, and still young enough to have its most massive stars. A dozen O-type stars, the hottest and most massive kind, light the nebula, and about 70 per cent of them are in close pairs. The brightest of the cluster is HD 101205, just below the largest of the dark globules: at least four stars, two of them eclipsing each other every 2.1 days, and its ultraviolet light is what’s eroding the globules. Above it and to the right, HD 101131 is another pair of O stars, which eclipse each other every 9.65 days, and further up, HD 101190 leads a smaller group in the north, which may have formed separately.

The cluster’s distance was argued over for decades. Estimates ranged from about 5,000 light-years to 7,500, and some studies thought the stars were in several groups at different distances along the line of sight. Gaia, the European Space Agency’s star-mapping satellite, has settled it: three separate analyses of its measurements put the cluster between 7,400 and 7,900 light-years away, about 7,600. That’s the same distance as the Carina Nebula, 7 degrees away in the sky, so the two are separate star-forming regions in the same spiral arm, about 1,000 light-years apart. The 6,500 light-years often quoted for the Running Chicken matches an estimate made in 1965.

Thackeray’s globules

Right of centre, and slightly above, the small dark clouds in silhouette against the blue are Thackeray’s globules. David Thackeray, at the Radcliffe Observatory in Pretoria, described them in 1950. They’re dense, cold clouds of molecular gas and dust, between us and the cluster, and their edges are being eaten away by its ultraviolet light.

Hubble photographed them in 1999. In its picture the largest turned out to be two clouds overlapping, and they were all moving among each other faster than the speed of sound in the gas. A study using ALMA, the radio telescope array in Chile’s Atacama Desert, made the most detailed map yet; it was posted as a preprint in 2025. It counts 46 globules, together about 35 to 44 times the Sun’s mass, most of it in the largest. In my picture that one is about 50 pixels long, about two light-years.

The ALMA study explains how they move by the rocket effect. Gas boils off each globule’s lit face and streams away towards the stars, and the reaction pushes the globule the other way, away from them. Most of the globules have less than 100,000 years left before they evaporate; the largest, more than 3 million. A press release from the European Southern Observatory in 2011 said infrared observations had revealed stars forming in many of them, but the research hasn’t borne that out: neither the 1997 study of the globules nor the ALMA one found any stars inside them. ALMA did find dense cores in five of them that may be starting to collapse, so some may yet form stars, if they can before they’re gone. The smaller globules in a row below and to the right are outside the fields Hubble and ALMA studied.

Stars forming now

The nebula is still making stars, at its edges, where the cluster’s light presses into the surrounding cloud. The gold rim at the right-hand edge of the picture is SFO 68, a bright-rimmed cloud. Inside it is a core of about 66 times the Sun’s mass, with masers (natural microwave lasers, which mark where young stars are forming) and the signs of a young star of type B1 or B2 hidden in the dust, perhaps a few hundred thousand years old. The study that found them thought it possible, even likely, that the starlight pressing on the cloud set off that collapse. The European Southern Observatory’s 2023 release seems to call this rim “IC 2944”, which would fit Frost’s original nebula near λ Centauri.

Left of centre, below the brown patch, the bright knot with a sharp right-hand edge is IRAS 11368−6312, a compact region of ionised gas with a methanol maser and a small embedded cluster of about 24 stars. A younger generation of massive stars is forming there, in the wall of the cloud on the nebula’s south-east side.

The picture

T31 is a PlaneWave CDK20, a 0.51-metre reflector, with a focal reducer and a monochrome CCD camera, at Siding Spring Observatory in New South Wales. The picture is from 3 hours 15 minutes each through the Hα, OIII, and SII filters: nine hours and three quarters in all.

The colours are the Hubble palette: sulphur is red, hydrogen green, and oxygen blue. Knocking a second electron off an oxygen atom takes ultraviolet light that only the hottest stars give out in quantity, so blue fills the inside of the bubble, round the cluster. Towards the edges the gas is shielded and less ionised, and gold takes over where hydrogen and sulphur dominate, deepening to orange in the outer rim.

There are no colour frames: the stars come from the same three narrowband filters, so their colours aren’t their own. I processed it in PixInsight.
