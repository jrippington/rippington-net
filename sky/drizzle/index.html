Drizzle

A finer picture from frames that moved

When a camera's pixels are large compared with the stars, the finest detail falls between them and is lost from every frame. But frames that moved by a fraction of a pixel between exposures each saw the sky from a slightly different place, and drizzle, invented for the Hubble Deep Field, puts them back together on a finer grid. This is how it works, what it needs, when it helps, and how colour cameras use it to do without debayering.

This follows on from Aligning and stacking, and leans on two parts of How astro cameras work: how a camera’s pixels sample the sky, and how a colour camera’s mosaic of filters records colour. Drizzle replaces one step of ordinary stacking, the way each frame is moved on to the reference before the frames are averaged, so everything else in the stacking article still applies. As before, it’s explained in words first, and the last section sets the same ideas out as mathematics.

Pixels too big for the stars

How much sky each pixel sees is the pixel scale, which depends on the size of the pixel and the focal length of the telescope or lens, and is measured in arcseconds a pixel. The air blurs every star into a small disc, and the width of that disc at half its peak brightness, its FWHM, is the seeing: amateurs more often get 2 to 4 arcseconds than anything better. The usual advice, explained in How astro cameras work, is to spread a star’s FWHM over about two or three pixels. Sources disagree on the exact number: the Nyquist rule, from the theory of sampling, and the Hubble team’s DrizzlePac handbook say at least two, Andrew Fruchter and Richard Hook, who invented drizzle, about two and a half, and other amateur guides three or more.

A camera whose pixels see more sky than that is undersampled. Its stars fall into one or two pixels and come out square, and any detail finer than a pixel, a close pair of stars, the edge of a dust lane, simply isn’t recorded: everything that fell on a pixel is added up into one number, and where within the pixel it fell is lost. Short focal lengths make this common. iTelescope T70, a 135 mm camera lens in front of a modern sensor, sees about 5.8 arcseconds a pixel, and the portable rig with a 35 mm lens about 26. My 150 mm Newtonian, at 1.6 arcseconds a pixel, is undersampled in all but poor seeing too, while the 250 mm, at 0.88, isn’t.

No processing of a single frame can bring that detail back, but many frames together can recover some of it.

Many frames, each a little different

Between exposures the telescope is usually moved a few pixels on purpose, which is called dithering, so that hot pixels and other faults fall on a different part of the target each time and stacking can reject them. A dither is rarely a whole number of pixels, and even without one, a mount drifts a little. So each frame’s grid of pixels lands on the sky in a slightly different place, and each frame samples the sky at different points within the pixels. Only the fraction of a pixel matters for this: a shift of 5.3 pixels samples the sky the same way as a shift of 0.3.

If the frames could be put together on a finer grid, some of the detail that each one lost would come back. The question is how.

Why not just interpolate?

Ordinary stacking moves each frame on to the reference frame’s grid by interpolation: each new pixel’s value is estimated from the old pixels around it, as Aligning and stacking describes. The obvious way to use the finer sampling is to do the same on to a grid twice as fine, and average.

It helps less than it should, because it adds two more blurs. A pixel’s value is already the sky blurred by the pixel itself, since everything that fell anywhere on it was added up. Interpolating spreads each value out again over at least the width of a pixel, and the finer output grid adds a little more blur of its own. The result is smooth but soft: the extra sampling is there, but the blurring hides much of what it would show.

How drizzle works

Drizzle avoids the extra blurring by treating each pixel as a small square of light rather than a value to be spread out. Registration still decides where each frame’s pixels land on the sky, allowing for any shift, rotation, or distortion. Then each input pixel is shrunk about its centre to a smaller square, the drop, and laid on the finer output grid. Its value is shared among the output pixels the drop overlaps, each getting a share in proportion to the area of overlap, and each output pixel ends up as the weighted average of every drop, from every frame, that landed on it. The image of drops of light raining on to the grid gave the method its name; its formal name is variable-pixel linear reconstruction.

Two settings control it. The scale is how much finer the output grid is: at a scale of 2, each output pixel is half the width of an input pixel, so the picture has twice the width and height. The drop’s size, as a fraction of a pixel’s width, is the pixfrac (PixInsight calls it drop shrink, Siril pixel fraction).

The pixfrac is where drizzle sits between two older methods. With a pixfrac of 1 the drops are whole pixels, and drizzle becomes plain shift and add: each pixel added in at its new position, with all the blur of its full width. As the pixfrac shrinks towards zero, each pixel’s value lands only on the output pixel under its centre, as a point sample, which is called interlacing. Interlacing adds no blur at all, but it only works if the frames’ positions happen to fill every output pixel, which real dithers never quite do. Drizzle’s drops are big enough to fill the gaps and small enough to add little blur, and Fruchter and Hook show that the gain from a smaller drop can be significant, since the blurs of the sky, the optics, the pixel, and the drop combine roughly in quadrature.

Drizzle is still an average. Every output pixel is a weighted average of measured values, so it can’t invent detail, and it keeps the light’s total where it was: a star’s brightness measured on a drizzled picture is the same as on the frames. Fruchter and Hook call it reconstruction rather than restoration. It recovers detail the pixels lost, but it can’t sharpen what the air and the optics blurred before the light reached the camera; methods that try to do that are the subject of Stacking beyond the average.

Choosing the scale and the pixfrac

For undersampled data from a mono camera, a scale of 2 is the usual choice, and it’s Siril’s suggestion. DeepSkyStacker offers only 2 and 3. Going further rarely pays, and every step costs: a scale of 2 has four times as many pixels, so four times the memory and disk space, and a scale of 3 has nine times as many.

The pixfrac is a trade between sharpness and evenness. Smaller drops add less blur, but each output pixel then averages fewer of them, so it’s noisier, and if the drops are too small for the number of frames, some output pixels receive nothing at all and are left empty. Too small a pixfrac shows as grain, as patchy, uneven stars, and as moiré patterns or holes in the background.

Recommendations vary. The Hubble team’s handbook puts the best pixfrac at about 0.7 to 0.9 for most dithered data; PixInsight’s default is 0.9, at a scale of 2; Siril’s rule of thumb is a pixfrac of about one over the scale, 0.5 at a scale of 2, a little more with some drop shapes. All of them say the same thing underneath: the more frames there are, and the more evenly they’re dithered, the smaller the drops can be. Most software also writes a weight map, which shows how much data reached each output pixel. It should look nearly even; the Hubble team’s test is that its scatter should be less than about a fifth of its typical value. Holes or a strong pattern in it mean the drops are too small for the frames.

The drop doesn’t have to be square. Siril and the Hubble team’s software also offer round Gaussian drops, which give rounder stars but don’t keep the light’s total exactly; point drops, which are interlacing; and Lanczos drops, which are meant only for a scale and pixfrac of 1. The square drop, the original, is the default nearly everywhere.

What drizzle needs

Drizzle only helps when three things are true, and it’s worth checking all three before using it.

The data must be undersampled. If the stars already spread over two or three pixels, the frames have already recorded everything the sky offered, and drizzle can’t add detail that isn’t there. Siril’s documentation says plainly that it can’t produce detail beyond the telescope’s resolving power. The author of Astro Pixel Processor puts it more practically: wide fields taken at focal lengths under about 200 mm almost always benefit, and 0.5 arcseconds a pixel in 2-arcsecond seeing makes no sense.

The frames must be dithered by amounts that aren’t whole pixels, in both directions, and scattered enough to sample the sub-pixel positions fairly evenly. Random dithers, or a mount’s natural drift of a few pixels, are usually enough.

And there must be plenty of frames. Here the sources disagree widely. Hubble manages with two or three frames placed exactly on a planned pattern, and about eight to twelve placed at random. For amateur data, PixInsight’s developers suggest drizzling routinely from about 15 well-dithered frames, others on its forum from 25 or 30, and more for a higher scale or smaller drops. Registration has to be accurate too, to a small fraction of a pixel, but modern software manages that easily with good stars.

What it costs

Drizzle’s output looks noisier, pixel for pixel, than an ordinary stack of the same frames, because each output pixel is smaller and averages fewer measurements. Whether that’s a real loss depends on how you measure it. Fruchter and Hook say drizzle sacrifices no signal-to-noise: over any patch of sky bigger than a pixel, the noise is the same as before. Practitioners, Siril’s documentation among them, say the extra resolution is paid for in noise and has to be paid back with more exposure. Both are right in their own terms. Per output pixel, the noise is higher; per patch of sky, it isn’t. Wei-Hao Wang, whose tests of Bayer drizzle come up below, found that the drizzled pictures looked noisier but showed stars just as faint as the interpolated ones.

There’s a subtlety in measuring it, too. When a drop overlaps several output pixels, they share part of the same measurement, so their noise is correlated: it rises and falls together. Measuring the scatter from one pixel to the next then underestimates the true noise on larger scales. In the Hubble Deep Field, the pixel-to-pixel scatter of the drizzled pictures was 1.9 times too small. The mathematics below puts a number on it.

The rest of the cost is time and space. Drizzling is slower than interpolation, and in PixInsight its step is often the slowest of the whole stacking run.

When to use it

If your stars come out blocky, your pixel scale is coarser than about half the seeing, and you have a few dozen dithered frames, drizzle at a scale of 2 is worth trying. If your data are well sampled, a scale above 1 gains nothing, and the files are four times larger.

Drizzling at a scale of 1, which recovers no resolution, is argued about. PixInsight’s developers recommend it routinely once there are enough frames, for mono as well as colour data, because it avoids the interpolation of ordinary registration, with its slight blurring and the patterns it leaves in the noise. Experienced users on the same forum report no benefit for mono data, and only a small one for colour. Siril recommends it for colour cameras, for a reason that comes up below.

Where it came from

Drizzle was made for the Hubble Deep Field. In December 1995, Hubble spent about 150 orbits over ten days looking at one small patch of sky, through four filters. Its main camera then, WFPC2, had pixels 0.1 arcseconds across in its three wide-field cameras, as wide as the telescope’s own star images in the near-infrared and much wider in blue light, so it was badly undersampled. The team planned nine dither positions, chosen so that between them they sampled nine different points within a pixel.

Andrew Fruchter, at the Space Telescope Science Institute, and Richard Hook, at the European coordinating facility for Hubble, wrote drizzle to put those frames together. For the Deep Field they used output pixels 0.4 of a wide-field pixel across and drops half a pixel wide. Their paper, published in 2002, shows a galaxy whose two nuclei, 0.2 arcseconds apart, look like one in a single frame and come apart when twelve dithered frames are drizzled. The Hubble Ultra Deep Field went further: about 800 exposures, enough to use points rather than drops, gave stars close to the sharpest the telescope’s optics allow.

Drizzle still combines Hubble’s pictures today, in software the Space Telescope Science Institute maintains. In Siril 1.4, released in December 2025, it’s the institute’s own code, adapted, in place of Siril’s earlier simplified version.

Colour cameras: Bayer drizzle

A one-shot colour camera has a mosaic of red, green, and blue filters over its pixels, so each pixel measures only one colour: red and blue at one pixel in four, green at two. Debayering estimates the two missing colours at every pixel from its neighbours, as How astro cameras work explains, so two thirds of the colour in an ordinary stack is estimated rather than measured.

Bayer drizzle, also called CFA drizzle (for colour filter array), skips debayering altogether. Each pixel of each raw frame is dropped, exactly as above, but only into the picture of its own colour: a red pixel’s drop lands in the red picture, a green pixel’s in the green. In one frame, the red picture has data at only a quarter of its pixels. But with each frame dithered, the mosaic lands somewhere different each time, so after enough frames every output pixel has red, green, and blue values of its own, all measured.

Because red and blue are so sparse, a Bayer drizzle at a scale of 1 already has the thin coverage of an ordinary drizzle at a scale of 2, so Siril recommends a scale of 1 for colour cameras, and colour data need more frames than mono to fill in. Wang, an astronomer who tested it carefully in 2011, put the minimum at about 10 frames, with more than 20 good. Too few show as colour patterns in the stack.

What it gains has changed as debayering has improved. Wang measured thousands of stars in 24 frames from a DSLR and found them about a fifth narrower with Bayer drizzle than with the simplest debayering, bilinear interpolation. Siril’s documentation compares it with the much better methods now in use, such as the RCD method Siril uses by default, and finds the gain in sharpness often marginal, a few per cent. Its real advantage, Siril says, is cleaner, less grainy noise, which is easier to reduce later and gives more consistent colour calibration.

Bayer drizzle began in DeepSkyStacker, whose author, Luc Coiffier, took the idea from Dave Coffin, who wrote dcraw, the program behind many raw-file converters. In 2011 Wang knew of no other software that offered it. PixInsight added it later, as CFA drizzle (the name covers other filter patterns too, such as Fujifilm’s X-Trans), and Astro Pixel Processor and Siril offer it now.

If there are only a few frames, superpixel debayering is the alternative: it makes one colour pixel from each square of four, measured rather than estimated, at half the width and height. And when the hydrogen-alpha light is taken from a colour camera’s red pixels alone, as it can be with a dual-band filter, the result is half the width and height, and drizzle can bring it back to full size. Siril’s documentation shows that case: drizzled at a scale of 2 with a pixfrac of 0.5, the stars came out about 9 per cent narrower than the same data enlarged by interpolation.

Drizzle in practice

Most stacking software can drizzle, each with its own way of fitting it in.

In Siril 1.4, drizzle happens during registration: each frame is drizzled on to the output grid as it’s registered, with the scale, pixel fraction, and drop shape chosen there, and the drizzled frames are then stacked in the usual way, with normal rejection. For a colour camera, the frames must be kept as raw mosaics, not debayered at calibration. Individual drizzled frames look odd, full of gaps; that’s expected, and the stack fills them.

PixInsight does it in three steps. StarAlignment registers the frames and records where every pixel goes. ImageIntegration stacks them in the usual way, working out the normalisation, the weights, and which values to reject, and writes all of that into drizzle files beside the frames. DrizzleIntegration then drizzles the original frames, the raw mosaics for CFA drizzle, using what the other two worked out. Its batch script sets this up when drizzle is ticked, and uses CFA drizzle automatically for colour frames.

DeepSkyStacker offers drizzle at a scale of 2 or 3, and Bayer drizzle as a separate option for raw colour frames; it advises against combining the two. Astro Pixel Processor offers both as ways of integrating.
