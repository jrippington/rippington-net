The Crescent Nebula

A shell blown by a dying star

The Crescent Nebula, a shell about 29 light-years long blown by the winds of a dying star, about 5,400 light-years away in Cygnus: hydrogen crumpled into clumps, a thin skin of oxygen where hot gas is breaking out, a dark bullet by the star, and round it the glowing hydrogen of the Milky Way. Four short July nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from the Crescent left it about 5,400 years ago, around 3,400 BC. About then, on the Ridgeway in Oxfordshire, the great chambered long barrow at Wayland’s Smithy was being built over an older burial place.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over four nights in July 2024. It covers 1.4 degrees of sky by 1, about three full Moons by two, in Cygnus. It’s from a filter that passes two narrow bands of light, hydrogen’s red and oxygen’s blue-green, shown red and blue; the stars are from exposures with no filter. The Crescent Nebula has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left, so east is down, west up, and south to the right. The Crescent Nebula is the shell in the middle, red with hydrogen, its brightest edge, the crescent, at its lower left, and a thin blue skin of oxygen round its upper left. The star near its middle is WR 136, the dying star that blew it. The orange star at the lower left is RS Cygni, and the red veils that fill the frame, brightest down the left-hand side, are the glowing hydrogen of the Milky Way in Cygnus. At the nebula’s distance, about 5,400 light-years, the frame is about 133 light-years across and 92 high, and each pixel covers about 1,500 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 49 times Neptune’s distance.

From Oxfordshire it crosses the meridian 77 degrees up, near the zenith, at about 2.30 am at the start of July and just after midnight at the end. But July has no full darkness here: the Sun doesn’t get 18 degrees below the horizon until the night of the 21st, and then only for about half an hour. Even nautical darkness, the Sun 12 degrees down, lasted only about three and a half hours a night at the start of the month and a little over five by the end; a filter that passes only narrow bands of light shuts out most of that twilight’s glow. The full Moon of 21 July was low, never more than about 14 degrees up: the Moon was near its major standstill, which comes round every 18.6 years, and the midsummer full Moons then stay very low.

The Crescent Nebula

The Crescent Nebula, NGC 6888, is a bubble blown by the winds of a single star, WR 136, in three stages. While the star was young and burning hydrogen, its wind blew a large, hot, thin bubble in the gas round it. Then it swelled into a red supergiant and shed its outer layers in a dense, slow wind, which partly filled that bubble. Now, stripped to its hot interior, it’s a Wolf-Rayet star, and its fast wind has caught the slow one, sweeping it into this shell and crumpling it into clumps and filaments. Infrared measurements put the nebula’s mass at about 25 times the Sun’s, nearly all of it once part of the star.

Where the fast wind hits the shell there are two shocks: one runs outwards, and it’s what glows here, and one runs back inwards, heating the wind inside the shell to X-ray temperatures. The Chandra and XMM-Newton telescopes found gas inside at about 1.4 million degrees, and some at 7 to 8 million, brightest in caps at the two ends of the shell’s long axis, at the lower left and upper right here.

The shell is about 18 by 12 arcminutes, about 29 by 19 light-years, and it’s expanding at about 80 kilometres a second; even so, its edge moves outward by about one pixel of my picture every 87 years. It’s a few tens of thousands of years old, about 30,000 by most estimates, and it won’t last: shells like this disperse within tens of thousands of years.

Gaia’s measurement of WR 136 puts it about 5,400 light-years away. You’ll often read 5,000, and older studies used figures from about 4,100 to 6,300 light-years, which makes their sizes and ages different from these.

WR 136

WR 136 is a Wolf-Rayet star, a massive star near the end of its life that has lost its outer layers. Its surface is at about 70,000 degrees, and it shines about 600,000 times as brightly as the Sun, though at this distance it’s only magnitude 7.5. It was born about 4.5 million years ago with perhaps 25 to 40 times the Sun’s mass, and its wind now blows at about 1,700 kilometres a second. It’s expected to end as a supernova, perhaps within about 100,000 years.

The oxygen skin

Round the hydrogen shell is a thin skin that glows in oxygen’s light, where the shell’s shock runs into the thin gas outside it. In my picture it’s blue beyond the red: widest at the upper left, which is the north-west, thinner along the lower right, and in filaments along the upper right. One interpretation is that the clumpy hydrogen shell is the red supergiant’s wind, rich in nitrogen and helium from inside the star, and the rounder outer shell of oxygen is what’s left of the first bubble, broken by the collision. At the upper left, an arc of oxygen with no hydrogen to match reaches beyond the rest: hot gas seems to be breaking out there, its edge perhaps moving at over 600 kilometres a second.

The rim and the bullet

The bright rim at the lower left, the north-east side, is the crescent itself, the shell’s densest and brightest hydrogen and one of its X-ray caps. The Hubble telescope resolved it into clumps each about 6,700 times as wide as the Earth’s distance from the Sun, and its pictures suggest the shock broke through the densest part of the shell only about 1,500 years ago.

Just below and right of WR 136 is a small dark knot, the bullet, about 20 arcseconds across. It’s dark at every wavelength it’s been looked at, and it’s probably in front of the shell: perhaps a dense clump being driven out by the star’s wind, or material from an earlier stage. Inside the right half of the shell a fainter, whitish arc curves like the edge of a smaller bubble; it’s strong in oxygen’s light, and what it is isn’t clear.

William Herschel found the nebula on 15 September 1792, as a double star with a faint “milky ray” attached. It’s named for the bright arc; under high magnification, some call it the Euro Sign Nebula.

The Milky Way round it

The frame is 2 or 3 degrees from the plane of the Milky Way, looking almost along our own spiral arm, and it’s full of glowing hydrogen. The brightest is the sweep of filaments down the whole left side, the north, with dark lanes between them; LBN 208, one of the clouds catalogued there, is centred in the top left corner. Fainter red veils fill the rest. At the lower right, a small dark globule, a dense knot of dust, blots out the glow, a faint red rim on its lower side, and a dark lane crosses the glow above it. The Soap Bubble Nebula, a faint planetary nebula found by the amateur Dave Jurasevich in pictures of this neighbourhood in 2007 and 2008, is just beyond the bottom edge.

The stars

The brightest star in the picture, RS Cygni, at the lower left, is a carbon star, a cool giant whose carbon-rich atmosphere soaks up blue light, which is why it’s so orange. It’s variable, between magnitudes 6.5 and 9.5 over about 417 days, and about 2,400 light-years away. HD 192422, the white star beside it, is a hot blue supergiant; they look like a pair, but they’re not.

Some of the stars are remarkable only for what they are. V1043 Cygni, at the right, is a Cepheid, a pulsating supergiant of the kind used to measure distances across the universe, brightening and fading every 8.85 days; Gaia puts it about 14,600 light-years away. AS 386, near the top, is a hot star circling something every 131 days that has at least seven times the Sun’s mass and gives off no light of its own, most likely a black hole. And at XTE J2012+381, right of the nebula, an X-ray telescope saw a flare in 1998, probably gas falling onto a black hole from a companion star; the system itself is too faint for this picture.

In front

The nearest star in the picture is HD 192020, at the nebula’s top edge: a star much like the Sun, about 80 light-years away, with a fainter partner beside it. The bright star on the nebula’s left-hand edge, HD 192182, is a yellow giant about 900 light-years away, in front of it, and a little brighter than WR 136.

Far beyond

Nothing in the frame is a galaxy you can see: looking this close to the Milky Way’s plane, through this much dust, a galaxy behind would be dimmed about a hundredfold and lost among the stars and the nebula. The farthest thing that’s known in the frame is invisible: a fast radio burst, a flash of radio waves a few thousandths of a second long, caught by China’s FAST radio telescope on 5 July 2021, near the left edge. No one has found the galaxy it came from, but how much its signal was smeared by the gas it crossed gives an estimate: its light set out something like 8.5 to 9.5 billion years ago, before the Sun formed. Also invisible, PSR B2011+38, a pulsar, the spinning core left by an earlier supernova, is probably on the far side of the Galaxy’s disc, about 40,000 light-years away by a model of the gas its pulses cross.

The farthest thing that shows is a star. [NH52] 20, near the top, is a hot blue star about 18,000 light-years away by Gaia’s measurement, more than three times as far as the nebula; to show at all through so much dust it must be very luminous. LS II +38 19, below the nebula and right of it, is another, about 15,000.

The colours

Only two narrow bands of light were taken through the nebula filter: hydrogen’s deep red, which falls on the camera’s red pixels, and oxygen’s blue-green, which falls on its green and blue ones, shown blue. So red means hydrogen, blue oxygen, and the pinks and whites both. The stars are from exposures with no filter, so their colours are their own.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. In front of it, an Antlia dual-band filter passes two bands 5 nm wide, hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III. The picture is 12 hours 48 minutes through it, and 30 minutes with no filter for the stars, over four nights in July 2024. It’s 5,749 by 3,960 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
