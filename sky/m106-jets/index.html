M106 and Its Jets

A spiral with a restless core

M106, a spiral about 24.7 million light-years away in Canes Venatici, whose distance is measured by pure geometry and anchors the cosmic distance scale; with hydrogen's light added to show its anomalous arms, gas shocked by jets from the black hole at its heart; and round it, its companions, galaxies far behind, and quasars whose light set out 12 billion years ago. Ten nights, from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from M106 left it about 24.7 million years ago, in the late Oligocene. Not long before, about 25.2 million years ago, the earliest known apes and Old World monkeys were living in what’s now the Rukwa Rift in Tanzania.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken over ten nights in March and April 2025. It covers 1.3 degrees of sky by 0.9, about two and a half full Moons by two, in Canes Venatici. It’s in natural colour, from a filter that passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, with hydrogen’s own red light added from frames through a second filter, to show M106’s famous jets. M106 has its own gallery, and my wider picture of it, from the year before, shows more of its surroundings.

What’s in the frame

North is up and east is to the left. M106 is the large spiral at the left, tilted steeply to us, its arms laced with pink clouds of hydrogen. Look below its bright core for a sharp red streak that bends away to the left: one of its anomalous arms. The edge-on galaxy among the bright stars right of the middle is NGC 4217, and above and right of M106 is its companion, the little irregular NGC 4248. At M106’s distance the frame is about 560,000 light-years across and 400,000 high, and each pixel covers about 105 light-years.

From Oxfordshire M106 never sets, and it crosses the meridian only 4.5 degrees from the zenith, so through March and April it stays high all night; by the end of April, though, there are only about four hours of full darkness a night. At about magnitude 8.4 it’s a binocular object from a dark site.

M106

M106, NGC 4258, is a spiral galaxy about 134,000 light-years across, and one of the most useful galaxies in the sky. Gas clouds orbiting the black hole at its centre shine as water masers, natural microwave lasers, and by tracking their motions in a disc less than a light-year across astronomers measure its distance by pure geometry: about 24.7 million light-years, to within a couple of per cent. That makes it one of the three anchors of the cosmic distance scale, with Gaia’s measurements of nearby Cepheids and the eclipsing stars of the Large Magellanic Cloud, and so part of the measurement that gives the universe’s expansion rate a value that disagrees with the one predicted from the early universe, the “Hubble tension”. The same masers weigh the black hole: about 40 million times the Sun’s mass.

Its centre is an active one, a Seyfert nucleus, and that’s what makes the jets.

The jets

In 1961 Courtès and Cruvellier, photographing M106 in hydrogen’s light, found two extra arms that cross the ordinary spiral arms instead of following them: smooth, diffuse, with none of the knots of young stars that stud the real arms. In 1972 van der Kruit, Oort, and Mathewson found them shining strongly in radio waves too, and proposed that they were powered by something thrown out of the nucleus.

The picture that’s emerged since is that jets from the black hole, leaving the nucleus at an angle to the disc, drive shocks into the disc’s gas along their path, and it’s that shocked gas that glows. Because the disc is tilted to us, the line of shocked gas appears on the sky away from the jets themselves; a model of 2001 predicted its angle to within about 2 degrees. In 2014 the Spitzer telescope found millions of times the Sun’s mass of warm molecular hydrogen in the inner arms, heated by the shocks, and much of the disc’s gas seems to have been blown out into the galaxy’s halo; at this rate, a NASA release said, the rest would be gone in about 300 million years unless it’s replenished.

In my picture the south-eastern arm is the most striking thing that shows only in hydrogen’s light: a sharp red filament that leaves the red-laced inner disc just below the nucleus and, about 1.6 arcminutes out, bends away to the left, east, the bend the studies describe, between about 8,000 and 18,000 light-years from the centre. Its partner on the north-western side should run up and to the right; there’s a fainter red band there, but it can’t be told apart with confidence from the disc’s own glow.

History

Pierre Méchain found it in July 1781, and described it in a letter of 1783; William Herschel catalogued it on 9 March 1788. Charles Messier’s own catalogue stopped at 103, and it wasn’t until 1947 that Helen Sawyer Hogg, reading Méchain’s letter, added it, with M105 and M107, as one of the objects Messier and Méchain had known: so it’s M106.

Its neighbours

M106 has companions in its own group. NGC 4248, the small irregular galaxy above and right of it, is at about the same distance and disturbed by it; KDG 101, a faint dwarf below it, and LV J1218+4655, a thin edge-on dwarf lower down, are two more.

Others only look like neighbours. NGC 4217, the edge-on spiral right of the middle, is probably two to three times further away, though catalogues that group galaxies by their speeds put it with M106. And the pair NGC 4232 and NGC 4231, at the top, and NGC 4226, right of the middle, are about 340 million light-years away, a group of their own far behind; NGC 4232 and NGC 4231 are probably interacting.

Far beyond M106

Further still, clusters of galaxies. Above and right of M106, small orange galaxies scattered round WHL J121739.7+472923 belong to a cluster whose light is about 3.25 billion years old, one of three at that distance across the frame. The farthest cluster that shows, WHL J122026.2+473050, is a faint orange smudge at the left edge, its light about 6.3 billion years old. And SDSS J1213+4707, a compact galaxy near the right edge, is the farthest single galaxy that shows clearly, its light about 7.3 billion years old.

The quasars, the bright cores of distant galaxies lit by gas falling into huge black holes, go further. Several show clearly as faint points, among them Q 1215+4754 near the top and SDSS J1217+4704, whose light set out about 12.1 billion years ago. One, SDSS J1218+4714, is behind M106 itself: a faint point below and right of its core, whose light has crossed M106’s outer disc on its way to us. The farthest, SDSS J1216+4655, a clear point below and right of the middle, is both the farthest thing catalogued in the frame and the farthest that shows: a quasar at a redshift of 3.78, whose light set out about 12.1 billion years ago, when the universe was about 1.65 billion years old.

In front

The brightest star in the picture, HD 106556, right of the middle, is an orange star of our own Galaxy, about 2,740 light-years away, some 9,000 times nearer than M106.

The colours

The colours are close to natural: the filter passes red, green, and blue in three separate bands, which the camera’s own red, green, and blue pixels record. M106’s core is yellow with old stars, its outer arms bluer with young ones. The red is hydrogen’s light, from 26 hours through the dual-band filter. That filter passes ordinary starlight too, so the starlight is taken away (continuum subtraction: a broadband image, scaled so that the stars match, is subtracted), leaving the hydrogen’s light alone, which I added to the colour picture. So the clouds where stars are forming show pink along the arms, and the shocked gas of the jets shows red.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. The picture is 20 hours through an Altair TRI-RGB filter and 26 hours through an Antlia dual-band filter that passes hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III, over ten nights in March and April 2025. It’s 5,325 by 3,795 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
