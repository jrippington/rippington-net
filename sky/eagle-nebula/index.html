The Eagle Nebula

The Pillars of Creation, from a garden in England

The Eagle Nebula, M16, about 5,600 light-years away in Serpens: a cloud of glowing hydrogen lit by a cluster of young stars, whose light has carved the dark cloud round them into the Pillars of Creation and the Spire. A quick picture, an hour and a half on a short July night, of a nebula that never rises far above the horizon from my garden in Oxfordshire.

The light from the Eagle Nebula left it about 5,600 years ago, around 3,540 BC. About then, on the Ridgeway in Oxfordshire, the first burial place at Wayland’s Smithy had just seen its last burials, under a mound the great stone long barrow would later cover, and near Abingdon a causewayed enclosure was built.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, taken in July 2025. It covers 38 arcminutes of sky by 32, a little more than the full Moon, in Serpens. It’s from a filter that passes two narrow bands of light, hydrogen’s red and oxygen’s blue-green. It’s a quick picture, an hour and a half: the Eagle is very low from the UK, and I’ll be going back for much longer. The Eagle Nebula has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

North is up, turned a little to the left, so east is to the left, west to the right, and south down. The glowing heart of the Eagle Nebula fills the middle, and its stars, NGC 6611, are scattered just right of the middle. Below them are the Pillars of Creation, three dark fingers pointing up and a little to the right, at the stars; left of them is the Spire, another column lying on its side. A dark cloud runs across the top, with a dark wedge reaching down from it towards the stars, and near the top edge is a round dark knot in a ring of glowing gas. The bright star with spikes at the upper left, HD 168136, is much nearer. At the nebula’s distance, about 5,600 light-years, the frame is about 62 light-years across and 52 high, and each pixel covers about 1,500 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

From Oxfordshire the Eagle never gets higher than about 24 degrees, and its light passes through nearly two and a half times as much air as it would overhead. In early July it was due south at about a quarter to one in the morning, but the Sun never got more than about 15 degrees below the horizon: from 21 May to 21 July there was no full darkness here at all, and only about three and a half hours a night of the deep twilight when the Sun is 12 degrees down.

The Eagle Nebula

The Eagle Nebula, M16, is a cloud of hydrogen lit by the hot young stars of NGC 6611, whose ultraviolet light has carved what’s left of the dark cloud they formed from into pillars. Most of its massive stars are only about 1.3 million years old, though there’s an older, more scattered group of about 7.5 million. Nineteen of the cluster’s stars are O stars, the hottest and most massive kind, and eight of them have been flung out of it as runaways. The most massive, HD 168076, is about 75 to 80 times the Sun’s mass, and gives about half the light that makes the nebula glow.

Gaia’s measurements of the cluster’s stars put it about 5,600 light-years away. You’ll often read 7,000 light-years, or 6,500, from the older measurements, which put it 17 to 26 per cent farther. The whole nebula is about 60 light-years by 47, and this frame shows its heart.

The Pillars of Creation

The Pillars of Creation are three columns of gas and dust, the tallest, on the left, about 5 light-years from its tip to where it joins the others. The dense knots at their tips shade the gas behind them from the cluster’s ultraviolet light, so while the thinner gas round them has boiled away, they’re left as fingers pointing back at the stars that are eroding them. Together they hold about 200 times the Sun’s mass, and at the rate they’re losing it they’ll last another 1 to 3 million years. Stars are still forming inside them: the James Webb telescope found protostars only a few hundred thousand years old. The bright star just left of the tallest pillar’s tip, HD 168137, is one of the cluster’s O stars, beside it, not inside it.

The Hubble telescope photographed them on 1 April 1995, and the picture’s release that November made them famous. Hubble photographed them again in 2014, and the James Webb telescope in 2022. Their name comes from a phrase in a sermon Charles Spurgeon preached in 1857.

In 2007 the Spitzer telescope’s team suggested that a supernova’s shock might already have toppled them, about 6,000 years ago, so that we’re seeing them only because their light hasn’t caught up. But the same team’s own paper of 2011 favoured the stars’ winds instead, calling the supernova “more speculative”; no supernova remnant is catalogued here, and the pillars’ measured erosion gives them a million years or more. Either way, we see them as they were about 5,600 years ago.

The Spire, and a jet

The Spire, to the left of the pillars, the north-east, is another column, about 8 light-years long, its dense head facing the stars, here lying on its side with its head at the right. The Hubble telescope’s portrait of it in 2005 likened it to a winged fairy-tale creature on a pedestal, and water masers at its tip show stars forming inside.

At the foot of the pillars is HH 216, a small bright white arc: the shock where a jet from a newborn star, two or three times the Sun’s mass, hidden in the dark column below, slams into the gas round it at about 150 kilometres a second.

The dark cloud

The top third of the frame is the dark molecular cloud the nebula is eating into, laced with fine dark filaments. From it a dark wedge reaches down towards the stars, and on its bright right-hand rim is SFO 30, a cloud the cluster’s light is pressing into, where stars are forming in a dense core. Near the top edge, a round dark knot of dust is silhouetted against a ring of glowing gas; whether it belongs to the Eagle’s cloud isn’t known. And right of the pillars, a small dark globule, with a bright rim, floats in the glowing gas, a fragment of cloud being evaporated by the cluster.

History

Philippe Loys de Chéseaux found the cluster in 1745 or 1746, and Charles Messier found it again on 3 June 1764, and saw “a cluster of small stars, mixed with a faint light”. The Herschels saw only the cluster, so the New General Catalogue lists only that, as NGC 6611; the nebula was added to the Index Catalogue, as IC 4703, after Isaac Roberts photographed it in 1897. Its popular name is the Eagle Nebula; Robert Burnham Jr. called it the Star Queen, seeing the tallest pillar as a queen in silhouette.

Far beyond

The Eagle is almost on the plane of the Milky Way, behind several magnitudes of dust, and nothing beyond our Galaxy shows or is catalogued in the frame. The farthest star with a precisely measured distance is BKP 31989, a small orange point at the lower right, about 6,700 light-years away, only about 1,100 light-years behind the Eagle: beyond it, the dust closes the view. The nearest thing in the picture is a white dwarf at the top edge, the burnt-out core of a star like the Sun, about 106 light-years away; its light left it in about 1919.

HD 168136, the bright star at the upper left, is an orange giant about 1,260 light-years away, less than a quarter of the way to the Eagle.

The colours

Only two narrow bands of light were taken, through the dual-band filter: hydrogen’s deep red, which falls on the camera’s red pixels, and oxygen’s blue-green, which falls on its green and blue ones. The nebula is red-brown where hydrogen glows alone, and pinkish-white in the bright heart round the stars, where oxygen glows strongly too.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, with a Starizona Nexus corrector that takes its focal length to 882 mm, f/3.5. The camera is an Altair Hypercam 26C TEC, a cooled colour camera whose pixels each cover 0.88 arcseconds here. In front of it, an Antlia dual-band filter passes two bands 5 nm wide, hydrogen-alpha and oxygen III. The picture is 1 hour 30 minutes through it, in July 2025. It’s 2,618 by 2,178 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
