The Orion Nebula

The nearest great nursery of stars, close up

The Orion Nebula, about 1,270 light-years away, the nearest place where stars of every size are forming: a glowing blister on the face of a dark cloud, lit by the Trapezium, with its small neighbour M43, the blue Running Man, the dust of the cloud the stars are born from, and through the thin dust beyond it a quasar whose light set out 12 billion years ago. Short and long exposures blended, from a fast telescope in Chile.

The light from the Orion Nebula left it about 1,270 years ago, around 756. The next year, in 757, Offa seized the throne of Mercia; he ruled for nearly forty years. The light from 45 Orionis, in front, left it around 1650, and the quasar’s 12 billion years ago, more than seven billion years before the Sun and the Earth formed.

This picture is from iTelescope’s T71, a fast 180 mm telescope at Deep Sky Chile, which I used remotely in November 2022. It covers 1.7 degrees of sky by 2.6, about three full Moons by five, in Orion’s sword. It’s in natural colour, from red, green, and blue filters, with hydrogen’s light added from a hydrogen-alpha filter, and each filter’s frames were taken at two lengths, 60 seconds and 600, and blended, to hold both the nebula’s dazzling core and the faint dust round it. The Orion Nebula has its own gallery, and it’s also in Orion Wide, a much wider view in hydrogen’s light.

What’s in the frame

North is to the left, so east is down, west up, and south to the right, each turned a little. The Orion Nebula is the pink and white glow below the middle, and in its white core are the Trapezium stars that light it. Just below and left of the core, cut off by a dark lane of dust, the Fish’s Mouth, is the small round M43. Further left, the blue and pink patch is the Running Man, and on the right, the bright star with blue haze round it is ι Orionis, the tip of the sword. Brown dust of the cloud the stars are forming from fills the lower half. The top third, to the west, is clear of the cloud, and through it shine galaxies and a quasar far beyond. At the nebula’s distance the frame is about 38 light-years across and 57 high, and each pixel covers about 600 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

The Orion Nebula

The Orion Nebula, M42, is the nearest place where stars of every size, the most massive included, are forming. Its glowing gas is only a thin layer, shaped like a shallow bowl, on the near face of a much larger dark cloud, the core of the great cloud astronomers call Orion A, and we see it roughly face on: the ultraviolet light of the young stars at its heart has eaten a hollow into the cloud’s surface and makes the gas glow. Round them is a cluster of about 3,500 stars within about 8 light-years, from a tenth of the Sun’s mass to about 50 times, less than 2 million years old. The nebula is about 24 light-years across.

Radio measurements of 2026 put the cluster about 1,270 light-years away, within about 6. You’ll often read 1,344 light-years, from measurements of 2007 to 2009, and before that, 1,500.

The Trapezium

The Trapezium is four bright young stars at the nebula’s heart, and three of them show here as separate points in the white core. Most of the ultraviolet light that makes the nebula glow comes from one of them, θ¹ Orionis C, a pair of massive stars of about 44 times the Sun’s mass in all, on an orbit of about 11.3 years that brings them close every time round. Galileo saw three of the stars in 1617, but didn’t mention the nebula. Gaia’s measurements suggest θ¹ Orionis C may have thrown a smaller star out of the cluster about 1,100 years ago, as we see it.

The Fish’s Mouth is the dark bay of dust that reaches into the nebula from the east, from below here, its tip almost touching the Trapezium; observers named the bright regions either side of it the wings.

The Orion Bar and the explosion behind

Just right of the Trapezium, to the south-east, is the Orion Bar, a ridge where the Trapezium’s light is eating into the edge of the dark cloud, so that the boundary between glowing gas and cloud is seen side on. It’s about 0.7 light-years from θ¹ Orionis C, and the James Webb telescope’s early science programme took it as the model of such a boundary: its pictures show sharp, sculpted edges, with detail down to a few hundred times the Earth’s distance from the Sun. Here it’s subtle, the faint line where the white core turns magenta. θ² Orionis A, just beyond it, lights the nebula outside the Bar.

About an arcminute to the left of the Trapezium and a little up, to the north-north-west, buried in the cloud behind the nebula, is BN/KL, where a group of massive young stars broke apart about 500 years ago, as we see it. It flung out at least three stars, at up to about 55 kilometres a second, and more than a hundred streamers of gas. It’s hidden by dust, and only infrared and radio telescopes see it; nothing of it shows here. And all through the bright nebula, the Hubble telescope found young stars with discs of gas and dust round them, the stuff of planets, evaporating in the Trapezium’s light: 178 of these “proplyds” in its survey. At this scale they’re far too small to show.

The bubble

The ring of pink arcs above and right of the core seems to be the edge of a bubble blown by θ¹ Orionis C’s wind, about 13 light-years across. It has swept up about 2,600 times the Sun’s mass of gas, and it’s expanding at about 13 kilometres a second, so it’s about 200,000 years old; X-ray telescopes found it filled with gas at a million degrees. It’s about a hundred times fainter than the core.

M43

M43, De Mairan’s Nebula, is the Orion Nebula’s small round neighbour, about 1.6 light-years across, cut off from it by the dark lane of the Fish’s Mouth. Unlike M42 it’s lit by a single star, NU Orionis, near its middle, and its bubble, expanding at about 6 kilometres a second and about 20,000 years old, is pushed by its own heated gas rather than a star’s wind. Part of its light is M42’s, scattered by its dust. Jean-Jacques d’Ortous de Mairan described it as a separate nebula in 1731.

The Running Man

On the left, to the north, the Running Man is dust reflecting the blue light of 42 Orionis and its neighbours, with patches of glowing hydrogen; dark lanes across it make the running figure. In 2016 seven young stars round 42 Orionis were found trailing envelopes of evaporating gas, the first proplyds found round a star of its kind, a B star, much cooler than θ¹ Orionis C. William Herschel found its brightest part, NGC 1977, on 18 January 1786. 45 Orionis, the bright star in front of it, isn’t part of it: it’s only about 370 light-years away.

ι Orionis and the clouds

ι Orionis, on the right, is the brightest star in the picture, a tight pair whose stars swing close every 29 days on a very eccentric orbit, squeezing each other into tidal “heartbeats”. About 2.5 million years ago an encounter involving it seems to have thrown two other stars, AE Aurigae and μ Columbae, out of the young cluster in opposite directions. The blue dust round it and NGC 1980, the cluster round it, is lit by its stars; whether NGC 1980 is an older cluster in front of the nebula or part of the same young population is still argued.

Between the Running Man and the nebula, the brown, streaky dust hides more of the cloud, OMC-2 and OMC-3, where many smaller stars are forming, one of the densest nests of jets from young stars in the sky. Where the jets hit the cloud, they make small glowing knots, Herbig–Haro objects: HH 41 and 42 are small red knots below and left of M43, and HH 309, below ι Orionis, is probably one of a family of giant flows from a young cluster beyond the right-hand edge, one of which reaches about 20 light-years from its source.

History

Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc saw a cloudiness in Orion’s sword on 26 November 1610, but his note lay unnoticed in his papers until 1916. Johann Baptist Cysat saw it within a few years and published it in 1619; Giovanni Battista Hodierna made the first known drawing of it in 1654; and Christiaan Huygens drew its centre in 1656, and was long credited with finding it. Charles Messier catalogued it, with M43, on 4 March 1769. In 1789 William Herschel called it an “unformed fiery mist, the chaotic material of future suns”. In 1865 William Huggins’s spectroscope showed that it’s glowing gas.

It’s also where astronomical photography of nebulae began. On 30 September 1880 Henry Draper photographed it with an 11-inch refractor, in 51 minutes: the first photograph of any nebula. And in early 1883 Andrew Ainslie Common, in Ealing, took eleven photographs of it with his 36-inch reflector, with exposures of up to 68 minutes, which for the first time showed stars and detail the eye couldn’t see; they won him the Royal Astronomical Society’s Gold Medal in 1884.

Why two lengths of exposure

The nebula’s core is one to three thousand times brighter than the faintest dust in the frame, about 8 magnitudes. Long exposures that show the faint dust fill up in the core and turn it white; short ones that hold the core miss the dust. So each filter’s frames were taken at 60 seconds and at 600, ten times as long, and blended: the long frames for the dust and the outer nebula, the short ones for the core. Even so, the core is held just below white, and the Trapezium shows as separate stars.

Far beyond

The top third of the picture, to the west, is beyond the cloud, where the dust thins, and it’s the only part through which anything beyond our Galaxy shows. The farthest thing in the frame is there, and it shows: SDSS J053043.74−062656.6, a faint point near the top right, a quasar found in 2017, whose light set out about 12 billion years ago, when the universe was about 1.8 billion years old. Six galaxies in the same part of the sky have measured distances, their light about 750 to 890 million years old: LEDA 3080941, near the top edge, is the clearest, a small round fuzzy galaxy, and 2MASX J05310885−0502295, at the top left, the farthest of them.

Something farther still once flashed here. On 16 January 2006 the Swift satellite caught a gamma-ray burst, in the lower middle, whose light, by its fading afterglow’s colours, set out either about 12.3 or about 13 billion years ago; a spectrum was too faint to decide. Nothing of it is left to see. The nearest star in the frame, Wolf 1451, near the top, is only about 93 light-years away.

The colours

The colours are natural: red, green, and blue filters give them, with hydrogen’s red light strengthened by the hydrogen-alpha frames. The pinks are hydrogen’s glow; the blue haze round the nebula, over the Running Man, and round ι Orionis is starlight scattered by dust, blue for the same reason the sky is; and the brown is dust lit faintly by the stars round it.

The picture

T71 is a Takahashi Epsilon-180ED, a 180 mm Newtonian made for photography, at f/2.8, with a ZWO ASI2600MM Pro, a cooled monochrome camera whose pixels each cover 1.55 arcseconds, at Deep Sky Chile in the Río Hurtado Valley. The picture is 1 hour 27 minutes each through red, green, and blue, and 2 hours 10 minutes through hydrogen-alpha, each from frames of 60 seconds and 600: 6 hours 30 minutes in all. It’s 4,000 by 6,024 pixels. I processed it in PixInsight, with Affinity Photo and Photoshop as it needed.
