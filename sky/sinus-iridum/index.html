Sinus Iridum

The Bay of Rainbows and Plato

June 2024, 10 min read

On this page

  4. Up to the edge

Sinus Iridum, the Bay of Rainbows, a great crater drowned in lava with only its arc of mountains left, and Plato, a dark-floored crater on the shore of the Sea of Cold: the lone peaks of Mare Imbrium, the Moon's north-western edge, and the places where Lunokhod 1, Yutu and Chang'e 5 came down. From my garden in Oxfordshire, through an infrared filter.

This picture is from my own telescope, the 250 mm Newtonian in the converted bike shed in my garden in Oxfordshire, without its corrector, at its full 1,176 mm, with a small ZWO ASI678MC planetary camera and an infrared filter, on the night of 18 June 2024, the same night as my pictures of Gassendi and Aristarchus. It looks across the north-west of the face we see, out to the Moon’s edge. The Moon has its own gallery.

What’s in the frame

Lunar north is up, tilted to the left, so east, towards the Sea of Crises, is to the right and west is to the left; the curved edge at the upper left is the Moon’s north-western limb. Sinus Iridum, the Bay of Rainbows, is the smooth bay at the lower left, ringed by the bright arc of Montes Jura, which ends in two capes, Promontorium Laplace at its upper right and Promontorium Heraclides at the bottom. Plato is the dark oval right of the middle, on a bright band of highland, with the smooth grey belt of Mare Frigoris, the Sea of Cold, above it. The lower right is Mare Imbrium, the Sea of Showers, with lone peaks standing out of it: Mons Pico below Plato, and Montes Teneriffe and Montes Recti to its left. At the left, towards the limb, is the big worn ring of J. Herschel. And four spacecraft came down in the frame: Luna 17, Chang’e 3, Chang’e 5 and Resilience. Each pixel covers about a kilometre round the bay, and more towards the limb, where the ground is seen at a slant.

The Sun was rising over this part of the Moon, low in the east, on the right, so shadows fall to the left, and inside each crater the shadow lies against its right-hand wall. It was 28 degrees above Plato, 18 above the bay and 15 over the Jura, and lower still further left, until the line of sunrise runs down the left of the frame, with only a few hilltops lit beyond it. The Moon was tipped towards us at the north and the west that night, by about 5 degrees each way, which turned this corner a little our way; the north pole itself is just inside the edge, near the top. From Oxfordshire the Moon was low, never more than 17 degrees up, in a midsummer sky that never got fully dark, and it was 89 per cent lit, three days before full.

The Bay of Rainbows

Sinus Iridum was a crater about 250 km across, made by an impact some time after the one that made the Imbrium basin, roughly 3.8 billion years ago. Then the lavas that filled Mare Imbrium flooded it too, drowning its south-eastern wall, so it opens onto the sea like a bay. What’s left of the wall is the arc of Montes Jura, 420 km round, with peaks up to about 3.8 km above the bay’s floor. A couple of days before this picture, with the line of sunrise crossing the bay, the Jura’s peaks would have been lit while the floor was still dark, a bright arc in the night that observers call the golden handle. Here the Sun is higher, and it’s the mountains’ slopes facing into the bay that make the arc.

The north-eastern cape, Promontorium Laplace, is named for Pierre-Simon Laplace, the French mathematician and astronomer. The south-western one, Promontorium Heraclides, is named for Heraclides Ponticus, a Greek of the 4th century BC best known for saying that the Earth spins on its axis. On Giovanni Domenico Cassini’s map of the Moon, presented in Paris in 1679, this cape is drawn as a woman’s head in profile, looking out across the bay: the Moon Maiden. Some take her for Cassini’s wife, but nobody really knows. North of the Jura, three neighbouring craters are named for the men who measured the shape of the Earth in the 1730s: Bouguer and La Condamine, who went to Quito, near the Equator, and Maupertuis, whose expedition to Lapland showed that the Earth is flattened at the poles.

Plato and the peaks of Imbrium

Plato is 101 km across, and round, but we see it at a slant, so it’s squeezed into an oval: here it’s about six tenths as tall as it is wide. Lava flooded its floor, which is why it’s darker than the bright highland round it. It’s 1.5 to 2 km deep (the sources differ). Its floor has a few small craters about 2 km across, a well-known test of a telescope and the air; at this scale they’d be two or three pixels, too close to the size of the JPEG’s own blocks to be sure of. After Aristarchus, Plato has more reports of strange glows and hazes than anywhere else on the Moon, most likely tricks of the air and the light.

Out on Mare Imbrium, the Sea of Showers, lone mountains stand up through the lava: Mons Pico, a single peak about 2.4 km high below Plato, the scattered Montes Teneriffe, and the short line of Montes Recti, the Straight Range. They’re the tops of the Imbrium basin’s inner ring, the rest of it buried. After the Ocean of Storms, Imbrium is the largest of the Moon’s seas, about 1,150 km across, filled by eruption after eruption of lava between about 3.5 and 2 billion years ago.

Pico and Teneriffe are named after Tenerife, in the Canary Islands, and its volcano, Pico del Teide. That makes a nice coincidence, because the small crater to the right of Mons Pico is named for Charles Piazzi Smyth, Astronomer Royal for Scotland, who in 1856 took telescopes up Tenerife to see whether the air was steadier on a mountain. It was, and while he was up there he made the first positive detection of heat from the Moon.

Up to the edge

Above Plato is Mare Frigoris, the Sea of Cold, a narrow belt of lava about 1,450 km long across the far north. Beyond it, towards the limb, the ground is more and more foreshortened, and the craters there are named for ancient Greek philosophers: Anaxagoras, Anaximander, Anaximenes, Philolaus, Pythagoras, Timaeus, Archytas and Epigenes. Some of the names have moved since Riccioli gave them in 1651: his Anaximander is now called Carpenter, and his Anaxagoras is Goldschmidt. Pythagoras, at the left, is on the line of sunrise, only its eastern rim lit. J. Herschel, 154 km across, is so old that its wall has worn down to a ring of low hills. It’s named for Sir John Herschel, and his aunt, Caroline Herschel, has a small crater, C. Herschel, near the bottom of the frame.

Four spacecraft

Four spacecraft came down in this frame. At this scale none of them can be seen, nor even the craters beside them, so the layer only marks where.

Luna 17 landed on 17 November 1970 on the plain about 70 km south of Promontorium Heraclides, and let down Lunokhod 1, the first rover on another world, driven from Earth. It was meant to work for three lunar days and lasted eleven, driving 10.5 km. It carried a French laser reflector, and after contact was lost in September 1971 nobody knew exactly where it was to aim at it, until the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter photographed the rover in 2010. A telescope in New Mexico then got an echo straight away.

Chang’e 3 landed on 14 December 2013, about 150 km south-east of Promontorium Laplace, the first soft landing on the Moon since 1976, with its little rover Yutu, the Jade Rabbit. Yutu drove only 114 m, but its radar saw three layers of lava, to 345 m down, under about 5 m of soil.

Chang’e 5 came down on 1 December 2020 at the lower left, close to the line of sunrise in this picture, and brought home 1,731 g of rock, the youngest lunar lava yet dated, about 2 billion years old. And in Mare Frigoris, south-west of the crater Timaeus, ispace’s Resilience, a Japanese commercial lander, crashed on 5 June 2025, almost a year after I took this picture.

The picture

The telescope is an Orion Optics UK CT10, a 250 mm Newtonian reflector, used without its corrector at its own focal length of 1,176 mm, f/4.7. The camera is a ZWO ASI678MC, a small planetary camera that records video, and in front of it was an infrared filter, to keep the atmosphere a bit more still. I captured it in SharpCap, stacked the sharpest frames in AutoStakkert, sharpened them with wavelets, and finished it in Photoshop. It’s in shades of grey. It’s 1,710 by 1,923 pixels.
