Why heavier stars are brighter

How hot the inside must be. A star holds itself up when the pressure of its hot gas balances its weight. For a star of mass MMM and radius RRR, the energy of a particle of gas in its middle must be about as large as the energy it would take to lift it out against gravity:

kTc∼GMμmHR,k T_c \sim \frac{G M \mu m_\text{H}}{R},kTc​∼RGMμmH​​,

where kkk is Boltzmann’s constant, mHm_\text{H}mH​ the mass of a hydrogen atom and μ\muμ the average mass of the gas’s particles in those units (about 0.6 for a star like the Sun, whose atoms are broken into nuclei and electrons). So the core’s temperature goes as M/RM/RM/R. Heavier stars are bigger, but not in proportion: on the main sequence R∝M0.6R \propto M^{0.6}R∝M0.6 or so above the Sun’s mass, so Tc∝M0.4T_c \propto M^{0.4}Tc​∝M0.4, and a star of ten Suns has a core about 100.4≈2.510^{0.4} \approx 2.5100.4≈2.5 times hotter than the Sun’s fifteen million degrees (detailed models give about twice).

How fast its heat leaks out. Inside a star the energy is carried outwards by light, which is absorbed and given out again by the gas, over and over, and so wanders out slowly. The energy stored in that light is about aT4a T^4aT4 in every cubic metre, where a=7.57×10−16a = 7.57 \times 10^{-16}a=7.57×10−16 J m⁻³ K⁻⁴; the light travels a distance ℓ=1/(κρ)\ell = 1/(\kappa\rho)ℓ=1/(κρ) between one absorption and the next, where ρ\rhoρ is the density and κ\kappaκ the gas’s opacity; and a random walk of steps ℓ\ellℓ takes about (R/ℓ)2(R/\ell)^2(R/ℓ)2 steps to cover a distance RRR, a time of about R2/(ℓc)R^2/(\ell c)R2/(ℓc). The brightness is roughly the light stored inside divided by the time it takes to get out:

L∼aT4R3R2/(ℓc)=ac T4Rκρ.L \sim \frac{a T^4 R^3}{R^2/(\ell c)} = \frac{a c\, T^4 R}{\kappa \rho}.L∼R2/(ℓc)aT4R3​=κρacT4R​.

Put in T∝M/RT \propto M/RT∝M/R and ρ∝M/R3\rho \propto M/R^3ρ∝M/R3:

L∝(M/R)4 RM/R3=M3.L \propto \frac{(M/R)^4\, R}{M/R^3} = M^3 .L∝M/R3(M/R)4R​=M3.

The radius drops out, and if the opacity is about the same in every star, the brightness goes as the cube of the mass. In real stars the opacity changes with temperature and density, and the measured relation is closer to L∝M3.5L \propto M^{3.5}L∝M3.5 near the Sun’s mass, flattening for the heaviest stars, whose light pushes on their own outer layers. Twice the mass gives 23.5≈112^{3.5} \approx 1123.5≈11 times the brightness.

The thermostat. The core’s fusion rate is not what sets the brightness: the leak is. If the core made energy faster than it leaked away, the extra heat would make it expand and cool until it didn’t (the ninth journey’s thermostat). So the core burns exactly as fast as LLL.

How long the fuel lasts. Fusing hydrogen into helium turns about 0.7 per cent of its mass into energy, and a star burns about a tenth of its hydrogen in its core before the core runs out. The Sun’s supply is then

E≈0.1×0.007×M⊙c2=0.1×0.007×1.99×1030×(3.00×108)2≈1.25×1044 J,E \approx 0.1 \times 0.007 \times M_\odot c^2 = 0.1 \times 0.007 \times 1.99 \times 10^{30} \times (3.00 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 1.25 \times 10^{44}\ \text{J},E≈0.1×0.007×M⊙​c2=0.1×0.007×1.99×1030×(3.00×108)2≈1.25×1044 J,

and at its power of 3.83×10263.83 \times 10^{26}3.83×1026 watts that lasts

t⊙=1.25×10443.83×1026≈3.3×1017 s≈10 billion years.t_\odot = \frac{1.25 \times 10^{44}}{3.83 \times 10^{26}} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{17}\ \text{s} \approx 10\ \text{billion years}.t⊙​=3.83×10261.25×1044​≈3.3×1017 s≈10 billion years.

A star’s fuel goes as MMM and its spending as L∝M3.5L \propto M^{3.5}L∝M3.5, so its life goes as

t∝ML∝M−2.5.t \propto \frac{M}{L} \propto M^{-2.5}.t∝LM​∝M−2.5.

A star of ten Suns, about 5,500 times as bright, has ten times the fuel and lasts about 10/550010/550010/5500 of the Sun’s ten billion years, about 18 million years; the stellar models give about 22 million, since heavy stars burn a larger share of their hydrogen. The figures use the models’ lifetimes, rounded: a tenth of a Sun, about six trillion years (small stars stir their whole insides and burn nearly all their hydrogen); the Sun, ten billion; five Suns, about a hundred million; ten Suns, about twenty-two million; twenty-five Suns, about seven million; sixty Suns, about three and a half million.

Reading a cluster’s age

All a cluster’s stars are the same age, ttt. A star of mass MMM is still on the main sequence if its life there, tMS(M)t_\text{MS}(M)tMS​(M), is longer than ttt. So the heaviest star left on the main sequence, where the band bends, has

tMS(Mbend)=t.t_\text{MS}(M_\text{bend}) = t .tMS​(Mbend​)=t.

With the rough rule tMS≈10 billion years×(M/M⊙)−2.5t_\text{MS} \approx 10\ \text{billion years} \times (M/M_\odot)^{-2.5}tMS​≈10 billion years×(M/M⊙​)−2.5,

Mbend≈(10 billion yearst)0.4M⊙.M_\text{bend} \approx \left(\frac{10\ \text{billion years}}{t}\right)^{0.4} M_\odot .Mbend​≈(t10 billion years​)0.4M⊙​.

For the Pleiades, about 120 million years old, the rough rule gives about (83)0.4≈6(83)^{0.4} \approx 6(83)0.4≈6 Suns; the models’ lifetimes, which fall faster than the rule above a few Suns, give about 4.5 Suns, and that’s what the figure uses. Read the other way, the bend’s height is the cluster’s age: in practice, astronomers fit the whole curve of computed stars of one age, an isochrone, to the cluster’s plotted stars.

For M13, about 12 billion years old, a cluster of the Sun’s make-up would bend at about 0.95 Suns. But M13’s stars have only about a fortieth of the Sun’s share of iron and other heavy elements, and with fewer of them the gas inside is more transparent: the heat leaks out faster (a smaller κ\kappaκ in the brightness above), so such a star is brighter and hotter at the same mass, and lasts less long. A star of 0.8 Suns of M13’s make-up lasts about 12 to 13 billion years, against about 23 billion for the Sun’s make-up, so M13 bends at about 0.8 Suns, four-fifths of the Sun’s mass. (The figures model this with the stars poor in heavy elements 1.6 times as bright, 15 per cent hotter and lasting 1.8 times less than stars of the same mass of the Sun’s make-up.)

Chandrasekhar’s limit

The electrons’ push. In a white dwarf the electrons are packed nnn to a cubic metre. Confined to a space about n−1/3n^{-1/3}n−1/3 across, each must move with momentum about p∼ℏ n1/3p \sim \hbar\, n^{1/3}p∼ℏn1/3 (the uncertainty principle’s squeeze), and no two can share a state, so they fill states up to a momentum pF=ℏ(3π2n)1/3p_F = \hbar (3\pi^2 n)^{1/3}pF​=ℏ(3π2n)1/3. Moving slowly compared with light, their energy is p2/2mep^2/2m_ep2/2me​ and their pressure

Pslow=(3π2)2/35ℏ2me n5/3.P_\text{slow} = \frac{(3\pi^2)^{2/3}}{5}\frac{\hbar^2}{m_e}\, n^{5/3}.Pslow​=5(3π2)2/3​me​ℏ2​n5/3.

Moving at nearly the speed of light, an electron’s energy is pcpcpc instead, and the pressure grows more slowly with squeezing:

Pfast=(3π2)1/34 ℏc n4/3.P_\text{fast} = \frac{(3\pi^2)^{1/3}}{4}\, \hbar c\, n^{4/3}.Pfast​=4(3π2)1/3​ℏcn4/3.

The weight. To hold up a star of mass MMM and radius RRR, the pressure in its middle must be about GM2/R4G M^2 / R^4GM2/R4. The electron density is n≈ρ/(μemH)n \approx \rho/(\mu_e m_\text{H})n≈ρ/(μe​mH​) with ρ∼M/R3\rho \sim M/R^3ρ∼M/R3, where μe=2\mu_e = 2μe​=2 for carbon and oxygen (two particles of the nucleus for every electron).

With slow electrons, P∝(M/R3)5/3=M5/3R−5P \propto (M/R^3)^{5/3} = M^{5/3} R^{-5}P∝(M/R3)5/3=M5/3R−5, and setting that equal to GM2/R4GM^2/R^4GM2/R4 gives

R∝M−1/3:R \propto M^{-1/3}:R∝M−1/3:

a heavier white dwarf is smaller. That squeezes its electrons harder, until they move at nearly the speed of light. Then P∝(M/R3)4/3=M4/3R−4P \propto (M/R^3)^{4/3} = M^{4/3} R^{-4}P∝(M/R3)4/3=M4/3R−4, and the balance becomes

M4/3R−4∝GM2R−4,M^{4/3} R^{-4} \propto G M^2 R^{-4},M4/3R−4∝GM2R−4,

in which the radius cancels. Only one mass balances, whatever the size; any heavier, and squeezing it smaller doesn’t help. Solving the star’s structure properly (a sphere of gas whose pressure goes as density to the four-thirds),

MCh=3π2 ω3(ℏcG)3/21(μemH)2≈5.83μe2 M⊙,M_\text{Ch} = \frac{\sqrt{3\pi}}{2}\, \omega_3 \left(\frac{\hbar c}{G}\right)^{3/2} \frac{1}{(\mu_e m_\text{H})^2} \approx \frac{5.83}{\mu_e^2}\, M_\odot ,MCh​=23π​​ω3​(Gℏc​)3/2(μe​mH​)21​≈μe2​5.83​M⊙​,

where ω3≈2.018\omega_3 \approx 2.018ω3​≈2.018 comes from that solution. For μe=2\mu_e = 2μe​=2, MCh≈1.46 M⊙M_\text{Ch} \approx 1.46\, M_\odotMCh​≈1.46M⊙​; with smaller corrections, about 1.4 Suns.

How big, and how dense. A fit to Chandrasekhar’s models (Nauenberg, 1972) gives a white dwarf’s radius as

R≈0.0112 R⊙(MMCh)−2/3−(MMCh)2/3,R \approx 0.0112\, R_\odot \sqrt{\left(\frac{M}{M_\text{Ch}}\right)^{-2/3} - \left(\frac{M}{M_\text{Ch}}\right)^{2/3}},R≈0.0112R⊙​(MCh​M​)−2/3−(MCh​M​)2/3​,

about 8,700 kilometres for a typical white dwarf of 0.6 Suns (the Earth’s radius is 6,371), and none at all at the limit. That one’s average density is

ρ=0.6×1.99×1030 kg43π (8.7×106 m)3≈4.4×108 kg m−3,\rho = \frac{0.6 \times 1.99 \times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi\, (8.7 \times 10^6\ \text{m})^3} \approx 4.4 \times 10^8\ \text{kg m}^{-3},ρ=34​π(8.7×106 m)30.6×1.99×1030 kg​≈4.4×108 kg m−3,

so a teaspoon, five millilitres, weighs about 2,200 kilograms: nearly The Van’s two and a half tonnes. Sirius B, about a Sun’s mass in a radius of about 5,800 kilometres, is denser still: about 2.5×1092.5 \times 10^92.5×109 kg m⁻³, some twelve tonnes to the teaspoon.

Why iron is the end

A nucleus weighs less than the protons and neutrons it’s made of. The difference, times c2c^2c2, is its binding energy: the energy it would take to pull it apart, and the energy given out when it’s put together. What matters for fusion is the binding energy for each particle in the nucleus, B/AB/AB/A:

NucleusB/AB/AB/A (million electronvolts)
Hydrogen-2 (deuterium)1.11
Helium-47.07
Carbon-127.68
Oxygen-167.98
Silicon-288.45
Iron-568.79
Nickel-628.79
Tin-1208.50
Gold-1977.92
Uranium-2387.57

Fusion gives out energy whenever its product has more binding for each particle than what went in. Four protons into helium-4 gives out about 26.7 MeV, the Sun’s 0.7 per cent of the mass. Silicon-28 burned to the iron group gives out a little: (8.79−8.45)≈0.34(8.79 - 8.45) \approx 0.34(8.79−8.45)≈0.34 MeV for each particle. But iron-56 and nickel-62 are at the top. (Nickel-62 is very slightly more tightly bound for each particle, 8.795 MeV against iron-56’s 8.790; iron-56 has the least mass for each particle, and there’s far more of it.) Join two iron-56 nuclei into one of 112 particles, and the best a nucleus of that size manages is about 8.5 MeV each: about 0.3 MeV less for each particle, about 30 MeV less in all. Making it would cost that much. Splitting iron costs energy too. In the core at three to five billion degrees, the nuclei swap particles until they’ve settled into the mix with the most binding, which is iron and nickel; from there, no reaction gives out any more.

ρ=1.4×1.99×103043π (1.2×104)3≈3.8×1017 kg m−3,\rho = \frac{1.4 \times 1.99 \times 10^{30}}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi\, (1.2 \times 10^4)^3} \approx 3.8 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg m}^{-3},ρ=34​π(1.2×104)31.4×1.99×1030​≈3.8×1017 kg m−3,

a little more than an atom’s nucleus, about 2.3×10172.3 \times 10^{17}2.3×1017. A teaspoon, 5×10−65 \times 10^{-6}5×10−6 cubic metres, of matter at the nucleus’s density weighs about 1.2×10121.2 \times 10^{12}1.2×1012 kilograms, more than a billion tonnes. A doughnut of it, about 150 cubic centimetres, would weigh about 3.5×10133.5 \times 10^{13}3.5×1013 kilograms, 35 billion tonnes; everyone on Earth together, about 8.2 billion people of about 62 kilograms on average, weighs about 5×10115 \times 10^{11}5×1011 kilograms, some seventy times less.

The Sun gives out 3.83×10263.83 \times 10^{26}3.83×1026 watts for ten billion years, 1.2×10441.2 \times 10^{44}1.2×1044 J in all, so the collapse releases about 210 times the Sun’s lifetime of light. How fast it falls. A ball of density ρ\rhoρ falling freely under its own weight collapses in about

The core is held back by its electrons’ push at first, and the models take a few tenths of a second from the start of the collapse to the bounce, the inner core reaching about a quarter of the speed of light.
