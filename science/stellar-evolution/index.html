Two clusters on the table

Cal had been up half the night with John’s pictures.

When John came down to the café in the morning, Cal was at their table by the window with his coffee going cold and his phone propped against the sugar bowl. On the screen were two of John’s photographs, side by side, and he turned the phone round so that John could see.

“These are the same kind of thing,” he said. “Aren’t they? Clusters.”

They were. On the left was the Pleiades, the Seven Sisters, which John had photographed with a telescope he used remotely: a knot of stars about 440 light-years away, wrapped in a haze of blue dust. On the right was M13, the Great Globular Cluster in Hercules, a ball of several hundred thousand stars about 24,200 light-years away, from John’s own telescope in his garden in Oxfordshire. Both were families of stars born together, out of one cloud, at one time, as the eighth journey had found every star is.

“The Pleiades are young,” said Cal. “About a hundred and twenty million years old, it says on your page. M13’s stars are about twelve billion. And look at the brightest stars in each.”

In the Pleiades, the brightest stars were blue-white, brilliant, hot. In M13, the brightest were orange.

“So where have M13’s blue stars gone?” said Cal.

John looked from one picture to the other. Then he did what he usually did with a question he couldn’t answer, which was to answer a different one.

“The blue ones are the big ones,” he said. “The heavy stars. We found that out yesterday: the Sun is a middling, ordinary star, and there are much bigger ones. So the big ones have more fuel. They’ll last longest. Perhaps M13 never had any.”

“That’s what I thought,” said Cal. “Then I read the rest of it.” He scrolled down. “A star ten times as heavy as the Sun lasts about twenty million years. The Sun lasts ten billion.”

“That’s backwards.”

“Five hundred times shorter.”

“With ten times the fuel?”

“With ten times the fuel,” said Cal. “The heaviest stars live fastest. Nearly every star the Pleiades started with is still there. M13 has lost every star heavier than about four-fifths of the Sun.”

John thought about it, and about The Van, which drank fuel on the observatory road as if it had a grudge against the mountain.

“A lorry has a tank ten times the size of The Van’s,” he said slowly, “and it goes about as far on it. Because it burns about ten times as much. If a star ten times as heavy lasts five hundred times less, it must be burning its fuel five thousand times as fast.”

“About five thousand times as brightly,” said Cal. “Same thing.”

“Why would anything do that?”

Albert, who had come over with a fresh pot of coffee and had been reading the phone upside down, put the pot on the table.

“Because it is heavy,” he said, “and it has to hold itself up.”

Why the heaviest burn fastest

Start with what a star is, because the answer is hiding in it.

In the sixth journey, John and Cal watched a cloud of gas fall in on itself under its own weight, heat up as it was squeezed, and light, when its core grew hot enough for fusion. From then on, a star is a balance. Its own weight presses in from every side, and the hot gas in its core, pushing out, holds the weight up. In the ninth, they found that the Sun keeps that balance with a thermostat: a hotter core would burn faster and swell, and in swelling cool down; a cooler one would burn slower and shrink, and in shrinking heat up. So the core sits at just the temperature at which it makes energy exactly as fast as the energy leaks away from the surface as light.

Now make the star heavier, and follow it through one step at a time.

  • A heavier star weighs more, so the gas in its middle has more weight to hold up. To push harder, it has to be hotter. The Sun’s core is about fifteen million degrees; the core of a star of ten Suns is about twice as hot.
  • A hotter star leaks its heat faster. Heat flows out of anything hot towards anything cooler, the faster the hotter it is, as a fryer full of hot oil cools faster than a cup of warm tea. Inside a star the heat is carried out by light, wandering from atom to atom, and the hotter and bigger the star, the faster that wandering light carries it away. The result is steep: twice the mass, about ten times as bright.
  • The thermostat makes the core burn exactly as fast as the heat leaks away. Ten times the leak means ten times the burning.

So a star of ten Suns shines about five thousand times as brightly as the Sun, and it burns its fuel five thousand times as fast. It has only ten times as much to burn. It runs out in about a five-hundredth of the time: about twenty million years, against the Sun’s ten billion.

Is there a way out? Perhaps a heavy star has fuel of a better kind, more energy to each kilogram. It doesn’t: in the first journey, the light of the stars showed that they’re all made of much the same thing, mostly hydrogen and helium, and a kilogram of hydrogen fused into helium gives out the same energy wherever it’s burned. Perhaps a heavy star burns more slowly than its brightness suggests, and makes its light some other way. It can’t: nothing else in it could keep up that light for a million years, let alone twenty million. Or perhaps it saves its fuel, and burns only a little at a time. But the thermostat leaves it no choice. A star can’t turn itself down; if its core burned more slowly than its heat leaked away, it would shrink and heat until it didn’t.

The heavier a star, then, the brighter it shines, and the sooner it’s gone. It isn’t a small effect. The heaviest stars there are, of fifty or a hundred Suns, last only three or four million years, a blink in the history of a galaxy. The smallest, of a tenth of a Sun, so dim that not one of them can be seen from Earth without a telescope, will go on for trillions of years, far longer than the universe has existed. What they’ll be doing when everything brighter has gone out is a story for the seventeenth journey.

“Then the Pleiades are a clock,” said Cal. “Their heaviest stars haven’t had time to go out yet.”

“And M13’s have,” said John. “All of them.”

“Every cluster is a clock,” said Albert. “You only need to know how to read it.”

A chart of the stars

Nobody can watch a star for ten billion years, or for twenty million. So how could anyone know any of this? The answer was found by two astronomers, working separately, who had the idea of putting stars on a chart.

The idea is to plot each star by two things the light gives you. One is its colour, which, as the first journey’s kit showed, is its surface temperature: blue-white stars are hot, yellow ones like the Sun are middling, and red ones are cool. The other is how much light it gives out, its true brightness. That second one is the hard part, because a star that looks faint might be a dim star close by or a brilliant one far away. In 1911 the Danish astronomer Ejnar Hertzsprung got round it in the simplest way possible: he used clusters. Every star in the Pleiades is at much the same distance from us, so if one looks brighter than another, it really is brighter. He plotted the stars of the Pleiades, and of another cluster, the Hyades, by colour and brightness, and the stars weren’t scattered anywhere they liked. Most lay along a single band. In 1913 Henry Norris Russell, at Princeton, showed the same for stars whose distances had been measured directly, all over the sky.

Hertzsprung-Russell diagram

A chart of stars by colour against brightness: hot, blue-white stars at one side and cool, red ones at the other; faint stars at the bottom and bright ones at the top. It is named for Ejnar Hertzsprung and Henry Norris Russell, who first drew it, in 1911 and 1913. Stars don’t fall just anywhere on it: most lie along one band, the main sequence, and the rest in a few groups, each a different stage of a star’s life.

That band is where stars spend most of their lives. A star on it is doing what the Sun does now, burning hydrogen into helium in its core, and where it sits along the band is set by one thing, its mass. The lightest stars are at the bottom right, faint and red. The heaviest are at the top left, brilliant and blue-white. The Sun is in the middle.

Main sequence

The band on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram along which stars lie while they are burning hydrogen into helium in their cores, about nine tenths of their lives. A star’s place on it is set by its mass: the heaviest are the hottest, bluest and brightest, at the top, and the lightest the coolest, reddest and faintest, at the bottom. The Sun is about half-way through its time there.

Put that together with the chart, and a cluster becomes a clock you can read off a picture. All the stars in a cluster were born at the same time, with every mass from small to large. The heaviest burn out first. So as a cluster ages, its main sequence is eaten away from the top: first the stars of fifty Suns go, then those of twenty, then of ten, and so on down. Each star, as its core runs out of hydrogen, swells into a giant and moves off the band towards the upper right of the chart (why it swells is the next section’s subject), and then, soon afterwards, it disappears from the chart altogether. What’s left is a main sequence that stops part of the way up, with a bend where the stars are leaving it. The lower the bend, the older the cluster.

The figure below is a cluster of a thousand stars, all born together, with every mass from half the Sun’s to forty Suns, in the proportions stars are born in: a great many small ones and a few big ones. At first you see them as they’d look in the sky, bright and faint, blue-white and orange, all mixed together. Play it, or press “Plot them”, and each star flies to its place on the chart, by its colour and its brightness: the main sequence appears out of the muddle. Then age the cluster with the slider, or let it play, and watch the top of the band. The heavy stars at the top go first, a few at a time, swinging away to the upper right and vanishing, and the bend creeps down. The table says which stars are still burning hydrogen, and how many have gone. Press “The Pleiades” to see John’s young cluster, and “M13” to see his old one. (M13’s stars are poor in heavy elements, with a fortieth of the Sun’s share of iron, which makes them a little hotter and brighter, and so shorter-lived, than stars of the Sun’s make-up; the figure allows for it.)

The Pleiades are about 120 million years old, and their main sequence still runs up to stars of about four and a half Suns: blue-white, brilliant, and doomed, with only a few tens of millions of years left. Plotted, their stars lie along the band almost to the top.

M13 is a hundred times older. Its main sequence stops at stars of about four-fifths of the Sun’s mass, and every star heavier than that has used up the hydrogen in its core. Some of them are the orange giants in John’s picture, the brightest stars in it. Others are the blue-white stars mixed in among them, and those aren’t young at all: they’re old stars that have been through the giant stage once and are now burning helium in their cores, a stage the next section comes to. And most of M13’s heavy stars aren’t on the chart, or in the picture, at all. They went out billions of years ago.

There’s one curious exception, which proves the rule rather nicely. In 1953 Allan Sandage found, in the globular cluster M3, a handful of stars sitting on the main sequence above the bend, where by the cluster’s age no star should still be. They’re called blue stragglers, and they turn out to be stars that have taken mass from a companion, or merged with one: a small old star made into a bigger one late in life, which looks younger than it is. M3 is in John’s collection too.

When the hydrogen runs out

What happens to a star when the hydrogen in its core runs out? John asked the simulator to show them the Sun’s, and the simulator, which had spent most of this series running history backwards, consented to run it forwards for once.

THE SUN IN FIVE BILLION YEARS: RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

The confidence was earned. Nobody can watch the Sun for five billion years, but every stage of what the simulator was about to show is on the chart, in clusters of every age, millions of stars of the Sun’s mass caught at every point in their lives; and the physics of each stage is the physics of the sixth and ninth journeys, worked out in detail.

In about five billion years the Sun’s core will be nearly all helium, the ash of its hydrogen. Helium can fuse too, but it needs far more heat: each helium nucleus carries twice a proton’s charge, so the electric hill between two of them, the hill of Through the Wall, is far higher. With no fuel burning in the middle, nothing holds the core up, and it shrinks under its own weight and heats, as everything squeezed does. Round it, in a shell, the hydrogen just outside the core gets hotter and denser too, and burns faster than the core ever did. The star grows brighter, and the extra heat pushing out from the shell lifts the outer layers. They swell, and as they swell they cool. The Sun, looked at from outside, becomes enormous, and red.

Red giant

A star near the end of its life, swollen to tens or hundreds of times its former width and cooled at its surface to a red or orange glow, though far brighter than before. It happens when the hydrogen in a star’s core runs out: the core shrinks and heats, hydrogen burns fiercely in a shell round it, and the outer layers swell. The Sun will be one in about five billion years. The heaviest stars become red supergiants, even bigger: Betelgeuse, in Orion, is one.

On the dashboard screen, the Sun grew. It grew to more than a hundred times its width, so big that from where the Earth is it would fill a good part of the sky, and it turned from yellow-white to a deep orange. (Whether it swallows the Earth on the way is one of the questions of the seventeenth journey.)

Then, when its shrinking core reaches about a hundred million degrees, the helium starts to burn. It fuses into carbon, and carbon and helium into oxygen. How three helium nuclei manage to make one of carbon, when the third journey found the gaps at five and eight so hard to cross, is a story of its own, and Albert said that he had a friend who told it better than he did. For a hundred million years or so the Sun settles down again, smaller, burning helium in its core. Those are M13’s blue-white helium burners, in John’s picture: old stars of a little less than the Sun’s mass at this stage, which, being poor in heavy elements, are hotter at their surfaces than the Sun will be.

When the helium in the core runs out in its turn, it all happens again, faster. The core of carbon and oxygen shrinks; shells of helium and hydrogen burn round it; the star swells a second time, even brighter, even bigger, and so loosely held at its edges that it starts to lose them. Over a few tens of thousands of years its outer layers drift away into space, and the hot core left in the middle lights them up from inside, so that they glow in the colours of their atoms. William Herschel, who found many of these glowing shells, small round discs in his telescope, called them planetary nebulae, because they looked to him like planets, and the name has stuck, though they have nothing to do with planets at all. There’s one in John’s picture of the Bubble Nebula, a tiny red dot near a corner, its outer lobes too faint to see; and two of the stars in his globular clusters, Barnard 29 in M13 and vZ 1128 in M3, are old stars caught just after shedding their outer layers, their hot cores laid bare.

On the dashboard, the Sun’s outer layers lifted away, and what was left in the middle shrank to a point. The simulator thought about this for a moment.

A star the size of the Earth

The simulator zoomed in, a long way, and there it was: a tiny star, white-hot, about the size of the Earth, holding about half of the Sun’s mass.

One of the first to be found was found by its pull, before anyone saw it. In 1844 the astronomer Friedrich Bessel, measuring the position of Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, year after year, found that it wobbled from side to side, as if something unseen were swinging it round. On 31 January 1862 the American telescope maker Alvan Graham Clark, testing a new lens, saw a faint point beside Sirius, just where Bessel’s companion should be. It was a star with about the Sun’s mass, ten thousand times fainter than Sirius. Faint, it seemed, because it was cool. Then, in 1915, Walter Adams at Mount Wilson managed to spread its light into its colours, and found that it was white, and hotter at its surface than the Sun.

That made no sense, and the reason is the first journey’s kit. A hot surface gives out a great deal of light from every square metre. A star that hot and that faint can only have very few square metres: it must be tiny. Sirius B, as it’s called, holds about as much mass as the Sun in a ball a little smaller than the Earth. A teaspoonful of a typical white dwarf would weigh about two tonnes, nearly as much as The Van.

White dwarf

What is left of a star like the Sun when it dies: its core, of carbon and oxygen, typically about half to two-thirds of the Sun’s mass, packed into a ball about the size of the Earth. It no longer burns anything; it shines only because it is still hot, and over billions of years it slowly cools and fades. It is held up against its own weight not by heat but by its electrons, which can’t be squeezed into the same states. It can’t be heavier than about 1.4 Suns.

What holds it up? Not heat, as in an ordinary star, because nothing in it burns any more. It’s cooling, and will go on cooling for billions of years. Something else is holding up the weight of half a Sun.

“The electrons,” said Cal. “They won’t share.”

He was right. In Room for Only Two, John and Cal found that no two electrons can be in the same state, and that’s why you can’t put your hand through a table: to squeeze atoms together, electrons would have to climb into higher states, and they push back. Near its end, that journey promised that some dying stars are held up by nothing else, for billions of years, and that a later series would come back to them. A white dwarf is one. Its atoms have been crushed together so far that their electrons no longer belong to any one atom, and every low state is full; to squeeze it further, electrons would have to climb into states of more and more energy, and their refusal to share is a push, which holds up the star. In 1926, the year after the rule was found, the Cambridge physicist Ralph Fowler showed exactly this. The push doesn’t depend on temperature at all. A white dwarf can cool all the way down to darkness and stay the same size.

Chandrasekhar’s voyage

In 1930 a young physicist of nineteen, Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, sailed from India to England to study at Cambridge, and on the voyage, a couple of weeks at sea, he worked out what Fowler had left out.

In a white dwarf the electrons are squeezed so tightly that the squeezing makes them move. In Exactly Seventeen Miles an Hour, John and Cal found the rule: confine something to a small space and it must jiggle, and the smaller the space, the faster. In a heavier white dwarf, which is smaller and denser still, the electrons move faster, until they’re moving at nearly the speed of light. And there, as the relativity series found, things change. Nothing can go faster than light: squeezing an electron harder gives it more energy, but hardly any more speed. So the electrons’ push, which had been growing faster than the weight pressing on it, grows more slowly once they’re near light’s speed. Add more mass, and the weight wins.

Chandrasekhar found that there is a limit. No white dwarf can be heavier than about 1.4 times the Sun’s mass. Below it, the electrons’ push holds the star up; above it, nothing he knew of could, and the star must go on collapsing. Arthur Eddington, the most famous astronomer in Britain, ridiculed the idea at a meeting of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1935, and for years most astronomers took his side. Chandrasekhar was right, and in 1983 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on the structure of stars. Every white dwarf ever weighed is lighter than his limit.

“So what happens to a star whose core is too heavy?” said John.

“It goes on collapsing,” said Albert. “As he said. For a long time nobody wanted to know what happens next.”

Living fast

A star of more than about eight Suns never makes a white dwarf. It lives fast and dies differently, and John’s pictures are full of them.

Heavy stars are so bright that their own light pushes on their outer layers, and they blow a wind into space for their whole lives. The star inside John’s Bubble Nebula, in Cassiopeia, is about 27 times as heavy as the Sun and about 250,000 times as bright, and its wind, blowing at about two thousand kilometres a second, carries away nearly half the Earth’s mass every year. It has swept the gas round it into a shell about nine light-years across.

Teleport to NyxThe Bubble Nebula and M52The Bubble Nebula, a shell blown by one heavy star’s wind

A heavy star near its end may have lost so much that it’s stripped right down to its hot interior. Such stars are called Wolf-Rayet stars, after the two French astronomers who first noticed their strange light, and there’s one at the heart of John’s picture of the Crescent Nebula, in Cygnus. It was born about four and a half million years ago, with something like 25 to 40 times the Sun’s mass. Then it swelled into a red supergiant and shed its outer layers in a slow, dense wind, and now, stripped bare, its fast wind is catching up with the slow one, sweeping it into the crumpled shell in the picture. Nearly all the gas in the nebula, about 25 Suns of it, was once part of the star. It’s expected to explode, perhaps within a hundred thousand years.

Thor’s Helmet, another of John’s, in Canis Major, is a bubble blown by another such star, though most of its gas is swept up from round it.

Teleport to NyxThor's HelmetThor’s Helmet, blown by another dying star stripped to its interior

Inside, a heavy star goes through its fuels one after another, each faster than the last. After hydrogen, helium, as in the Sun. But a heavy star’s core, squeezed by far more weight, gets hot enough to go on: when the helium is gone it shrinks and heats again, and the carbon starts to burn, into neon and magnesium; then, hotter still, the neon; then the oxygen, into silicon and sulphur; and at last, at about three billion degrees, the silicon, into iron. Each fuel gives out less energy than the one before, and each has to be burned at a higher temperature, where the core loses most of its energy not as light but as neutrinos, which fly straight out of it. So each fuel burns faster than the last. A star of 25 Suns burns hydrogen for about seven million years, helium for about seven hundred thousand, carbon for about six hundred years. It gets through its neon in about a year, its oxygen in about six months, and its silicon in a day. The star ends up like an onion, its shells of older ash round a core of iron, each shell burning its own fuel at its base.

And at iron it stops.

Fusion gives out energy because the nucleus it makes is more tightly bound than the ones it was made from: its protons and neutrons sit deeper in the grip of the strong force, and the difference comes out as energy, by E = mc², as The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe found. That works all the way from hydrogen up to iron. But iron’s nucleus is the most tightly bound there is, give or take its neighbour nickel. Join two iron nuclei, and the result is less tightly bound than they were: fusing iron doesn’t give out energy, it costs energy. Iron is ash that can’t be burned. (The next journey draws the valley this makes, with iron at the bottom.)

For the mathematically inclinedWhy iron is the end

So the iron core just grows, as the silicon shell round it keeps adding to it, and nothing burns in it to hold it up. Only its electrons do, refusing to share, exactly as in a white dwarf. The core of a heavy star at the end of its life is, in effect, a white dwarf, a white dwarf made of iron, sitting in the middle of a supergiant. And it’s growing, by the day, towards Chandrasekhar’s limit.

The last second

When the iron core reaches about 1.4 Suns, it collapses. The simulator slowed everything down by a factor it didn’t name, and showed them.

CORE COLLAPSE: 0.25 SECONDS. PLEASE DO NOT BLINK

The core, a ball of iron a few thousand kilometres across, falls in on itself at up to a quarter of the speed of light. As it’s crushed, its electrons are forced into its protons, turning them into neutrons and giving off neutrinos, and with fewer electrons to push back, it falls faster. In about a quarter of a second, the whole core, more massive than the Sun, has fallen to a ball of neutrons about twenty kilometres across, as dense as the nucleus of an atom. There it can’t go any further. The neutrons won’t share either, and at that density the strong force itself pushes back. The infalling core stops dead, and rebounds.

Neutron star

The collapsed core of a heavy star that has exploded: more massive than the Sun, packed into a ball about twenty kilometres across, made almost entirely of neutrons, as dense as an atom’s nucleus. A teaspoonful would weigh about a billion tonnes. Neutron stars spin fast, some hundreds of times a second, and are magnetised far more strongly than anything on Earth. If the collapsing core is too heavy even for the neutrons to hold up, it becomes a black hole instead.

“How much would a doughnut of it weigh?” said Cal, who had one in his hand.

“About thirty billion tonnes,” said Albert. “Some seventy times as much as everyone on Earth put together.” He looked at the doughnut. “It would also not stay a doughnut for long, out here, with nothing squeezing it. That one is quite heavy enough.”

What happens next took astronomers decades to work out, and some of it is still being argued over. The rebound sends a shock wave out into the rest of the star, which is still falling in. The shock stalls. And then the neutrinos come into it. The collapse has turned an enormous amount of energy loose, about two hundred times what the Sun will give out in its whole ten-billion-year life, and nearly all of it, about 99 per cent, is carried off by neutrinos, which the ninth journey found pass through almost anything. Almost: in the incredible density round the new neutron star, a small share of them is caught, and that share is enough to heat the gas behind the shock and drive it on. The rest of the star, everything outside the core, is blasted into space at thousands of kilometres a second. Only in the last decade or so have most of the biggest computer models of it managed to blow the star apart, and the simulator said so.

Supernova

The explosion of a star, which for a few weeks can shine as brightly as hundreds of millions of Suns. Most come from heavy stars, of more than about eight Suns, whose iron cores collapse at the end of their lives, leaving a neutron star or a black hole and blasting the rest of the star into space. Others come from white dwarfs, in pairs of stars, which gain matter until they blow themselves apart. Nearly all a collapsing star’s energy leaves as neutrinos; about one per cent goes into the blast, and the light, for all its brilliance, is a few thousandths of one per cent.

“All that energy,” said John. “From where? Nothing’s burning.”

“From falling,” said Albert. “A ball of iron as heavy as the Sun has fallen from the size of a small planet to the size of a town. You know what falling does, with water down a mountain. This is the same, only rather more so.”

He looked, John thought, quite pleased.

“And you can weigh it,” he went on. “The neutron star at the end weighs about a tenth less than the core it was made from. The energy carried off has mass, as it always does. Something like a seventh of a Sun’s worth of mass, gone as energy, in a quarter of a second.” He picked up the coffee pot. “Every star runs on E = mc² in the end.”

Two dozen neutrinos

That’s the story worked out from physics, and for a long time there was no way to check its most important part: the neutrinos. Then, on 23 February 1987, a star exploded in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy that orbits our own, about 162,000 light-years away.

At 7.35 in the morning, Greenwich time, three detectors deep underground, two of them built to look for something quite different, caught a burst of neutrinos within a few seconds of each other: eleven or twelve in Kamiokande II, in a mine in Japan, eight in a detector called IMB, in a salt mine in Ohio, and five at Baksan, in the Caucasus. About two dozen, out of an unimaginable number, in about thirteen seconds. Two or three hours later the light arrived, because the neutrinos had come straight out of the collapsing core, while the light had to wait for the blast to reach the star’s surface. The next night, at Las Campanas in Chile, Ian Shelton noticed a new star on a photograph he’d just taken, and then went outside and saw it with his own eyes. It was SN 1987A, the first supernova seen by eye since 1604.

NEUTRINOS FROM SN 1987A: OBSERVED. ABOUT TWO DOZEN

“Two dozen,” said John. “That’s all?”

“Two dozen caught,” said Cal. He had found the number on his phone. “It says a few hundred trillion of them went through each of us. Through everyone on Earth. And straight out the other side.”

“I don’t remember it,” said John.

“You wouldn’t,” said Albert.

The exploding star had been catalogued long before as Sanduleak −69° 202, a blue supergiant of about twenty Suns, and it’s gone: the first supernova whose star had been seen before it exploded. Astronomers have been watching the wreck ever since, and in 2024 the James Webb Space Telescope found the best evidence yet of the neutron star at its centre. Lighthouses

In 1967 Jocelyn Bell, a research student at Cambridge, was searching through miles of chart paper from a radio telescope she had helped build, and noticed a small mark she called “scruff”. When it came back, and the chart was run fast enough to see it properly, it was a train of radio pulses, one every 1.33 seconds, as regular as a clock. Nothing natural anyone knew of did that, and for a while, half in jest, the source was called LGM-1, for “little green men”. It was the first pulsar.

It was a neutron star, spinning. A collapsing core spins faster as it shrinks, as water spins faster as it nears the plughole, and its magnetism is squeezed with it; a neutron star sends out beams of radio waves along its magnetic poles, and if one of them sweeps past the Earth as it turns, we see a pulse once a turn, like a lighthouse. In 1974 the Nobel Prize in Physics went to her supervisor, Antony Hewish, and to Martin Ryle, and not to her, an omission many astronomers have regretted ever since.

The year after, in 1968, astronomers in Sydney found a pulsar spinning eleven times a second in the middle of the Vela supernova remnant: the wreck of a star which exploded about eleven thousand years ago, as we see it, about 940 light-years away, one of the nearest there is. With another pulsar found that year in the Crab Nebula, it was the first direct evidence that supernovae make neutron stars. John has photographed the whole of the Vela wreck. Its blast wave has spread across eight degrees of sky, sixteen times the width of the full Moon, and where it runs into gas, the gas glows.

Its brightest edge is the Pencil Nebula, which John has photographed closer in; and in Cygnus, his Eastern Veil is the edge of an older wreck, about twenty thousand years old, of a star of about fifteen Suns.

Not every core stops at a neutron star. If the collapsing core is heavy enough, even the neutrons can’t hold it up, and it goes on collapsing until nothing can stop it at all. That’s a black hole, as One Way In found: Cygnus X-1, about 21 Suns of it, is what was left of one such star. And pairs of neutron stars, left by two stars that once orbited each other, sometimes spiral together and collide, as Echoes of Spacetime heard in gravitational waves; the next journey finds out what such collisions make.

Back to Teide

By evening the sky over the plateau was clear and very dark, and the three of them took their coffee out to the terrace wall.

Low in the east, over the ridge, the Pleiades were rising, a small, misty knot of stars that Cal could pick out at once now that he knew what it was: about 120 million years old, young enough that its brightest stars still had a little of their hydrogen left. Low in the west, going down behind the mountain, was Hercules, with M13 somewhere in it, a smudge Cal couldn’t find without binoculars, its heavy stars long since gone. A young cluster rising and an old one setting, on opposite sides of the same sky.

“Orion’s up later,” said John. “After midnight. Betelgeuse.”

Betelgeuse, at Orion’s shoulder, is a red supergiant, a star of perhaps eighteen Suns about 550 light-years away, burning helium in its core. It will explode one day: by one recent estimate in about a hundred thousand years, perhaps sooner. When it does, for a few weeks it will be bright enough to see in daylight.

Cal was quiet for a while, looking at the Pleiades.

“So all those stars that blew up,” he said at last. “The ones in M13, and the ones whose wrecks are the Veil and Vela. Everything they’d made inside them, the carbon and the oxygen and the silicon. It all got thrown out.”

“Into the gas between the stars,” said John. “And the next stars were made from the gas.”

Cal felt in his shirt pocket, where the napkin with the grain of sugar in it was folded up, with the words on the outside, as it had been since the end of the last series, and didn’t take it out.

Albert collected the cups.

“A friend of mine is coming for tea in the morning,” he said. “You met him. He has a story about carbon.” He considered the sky. “He will tell it to you whether you ask or not. I would ask.”
