Albert's Bet

Could secret instructions explain it? A test, and nature's answer

Albert bets that his photons carry secret instructions, and Niels, in for tea, takes the bet, in doughnuts. A count simple enough for a napkin settles it: instructions of any kind make the two detectors agree at least five times in nine. Nature manages a half. Experiments from Berkeley to the telescopes of La Palma, their settings chosen at last by quasars, say who pays.

Tea for Niels

The café had a teapot. It was kept at the back of the cupboard behind the fryer, and it came out only when Niels did.

Albert found it a little after ten, washed it with the care of a man performing an old ceremony whose meaning he had forgotten, and spent some time looking for the tea. By the time he had found that too, in a tin marked FLOUR, Niels was at the door.

He was Albert’s old friend, and in the sixth journey and the seventh John and Cal had learned to listen to him carefully, because he spoke so softly, and qualified everything so thoroughly, that the end of a sentence often turned out to be the opposite of how it began. He took off his coat, accepted his tea, and turned at once to the black box on its tripod by the terrace wall.

Albert explained it to him: the twins, the waiting room, John’s journey to the Moon, the two lists that matched whenever the settings were alike, and the gloves. He had kept the receipt from the till, and John’s napkin with the curve on it, and he laid them on the table between the cups.

“So you see,” he finished. “Each pair is packed before it leaves. Every photon carries an answer for every setting, and its twin carries the same. Nothing travels. Nobody needs to go first. There is no spooky action at all. Only instructions.”

Niels looked at the receipt for a long time. Then he looked at the napkin for even longer.

“I would not wish to say,” he said at last, so quietly that John and Cal both leaned forwards, “that it is wrong to speak of the photons as carrying something. I would say only that we should be very careful. It may be that what we can say about a photon cannot be separated from the whole arrangement we use to ask it.”

“He means he disagrees,” said Albert.

“I mean,” said Niels, “that I may disagree.”

“Then let us settle it.” Albert sat down opposite him. “I say my photons carry instructions. If anyone can show that they do not, I will give you a dozen doughnuts.”

Niels considered this, and his tea.

“And if they do?”

“Then you give me a dozen.”

“I accept,” said Niels.

They shook hands across the table. Cal, who had been watching all this with a doughnut halfway to his mouth, put it down.

“Whose doughnuts?” he said.

“Mine, naturally,” said Albert.

“So if you lose, you pay Niels a dozen of your doughnuts from your counter. And if he loses, he pays you a dozen of your doughnuts from your counter.”

“That,” said Albert, “is the beauty of running a café.”

“I am content with the arrangement,” said Niels.

Bell’s socks

“How will you settle it?” said John.

“A man named Bell found a way,” said Albert. “In 1964.”

“Another John,” said Cal, who had looked him up.

He was John Bell, a physicist from Belfast who worked at CERN, the great laboratory near Geneva where particles are smashed together. Most of his working life went on the physics of those particles. But in 1964, during a visit to America, he published a short paper on the question Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen had raised in 1935, in a small new journal that hardly anyone read and that soon closed. In it he showed that the question Albert and Niels had just bet on, which most physicists had taken to be a matter of philosophy, could be settled by an experiment.

Bell had a favourite picture of his own for links like the twins’, and it wasn’t gloves. A colleague of his, the physicist Reinhold Bertlmann, always wore odd socks, a different colour on each foot. Which colour would be on which foot, on any given day, nobody could predict, but if you caught sight of one sock and it was pink, you knew at once that the other wasn’t. In 1981 Bell wrote a paper called “Bertlmann’s socks and the nature of reality” asking whether entangled particles were simply like Bertlmann’s socks.

“Socks,” said Albert, “are a little undignified. But he was a very great physicist, and he can be forgiven.”

What instructions would mean

Before an argument can settle the bet, Albert’s side of it has to be said exactly, and slowly, because everything depends on it.

Albert’s claim is that each photon leaves the box carrying instructions: an answer for every setting a detector might have, decided before it leaves. Picture the instructions as a card, tucked into each photon, with a line for every setting: at upright, say YES; at ten degrees, YES; at twenty, NO; and so on round the dial. A detector doesn’t decide anything. It reads the line for its own setting off the card of the photon in front of it, and sends the photon to whichever counter the card names.

Three things follow, and none of them is a choice: they come straight from what John and Cal saw.

First, the two photons of a pair must carry the same card. At the same setting, the twins always agree. If their cards differed on any line, then whenever both detectors were set to that line, the twins would disagree, and they never do.

Second, the answers must be fixed before the photons leave. Could a photon toss a coin of its own when it reaches the detector, instead of carrying a fixed answer? Then, at the same setting, the two photons of a pair would be tossing two separate coins, on the Moon and at the café, and sometimes their coins would come down differently. They never do. So the answers can’t be left to chance at the detectors: they must be on the card.

Third, nothing else is needed, and nothing else is allowed. Each detector reads only the card of the photon in front of it. What the other detector is set to, far away, can’t reach it in time, because the settings are chosen at the last moment and nothing travels faster than light. The cards are written when the pair is made, before anyone has chosen a setting, so they can’t be written to suit the settings.

Albert can choose the cards however he likes. Different pairs can carry different cards, in any mixture, in any proportions, and the box can pick each pair’s card at random. That’s what makes each list on its own look like coin tosses. The idea is as flexible as it could possibly be, and that’s what makes the next step so remarkable.

Local hidden variables

Albert’s “instructions”: properties a particle would carry with it from its source, fixing in advance the result of every measurement it might meet. Hidden, because quantum mechanics has no place for them, so they would be extra, out of sight. Local, because each detector’s result would depend only on what’s at that detector: its own setting and the properties of the particle in front of it, never on anything happening far away. A pair of gloves posted in two boxes is a local hidden variable: the hand of each glove is fixed when the boxes are packed, and opening one box changes nothing in the other.

Three settings and a napkin

The clearest way to see what Bell found is not Bell’s own. It’s a version invented in 1981 by the physicist David Mermin, at Cornell, for an article called “Quantum mysteries for anybody”, and he meant the “anybody”. It needs no mathematics at all, only a careful count. This journey’s argument is his, and so is the idea of the figure below.

Mermin’s idea is to give each detector only three settings, the same three on both:

  • setting 1: upright;
  • setting 2: turned 60 degrees;
  • setting 3: turned 120 degrees.

For each pair, each detector picks one of its three settings at random, separately, at the last moment, as the photons are on their way. Then there are nine ways the two settings can fall, 1 and 1, 1 and 2, 1 and 3, 2 and 1, and so on up to 3 and 3, all equally likely. After a great many pairs, count how often the two results agreed.

Now, with only three settings, a card needs only three lines. Albert took a napkin and wrote one out:

1: YES, 2: YES, 3: NO

Then he drew a square of nine boxes beside it, three across for Cal’s setting and three down for John’s, and went through them one at a time, as Mermin did.

The three boxes on the diagonal, where both detectors chose the same setting, agree: both detectors read the same line of the same card. Then there are the two boxes where one detector chose setting 1 and the other setting 2. Both lines say YES, so those agree too. That leaves the four boxes where one detector chose setting 3 and the other chose 1 or 2. Setting 3 says NO and the others say YES, so in those four the results disagree. Five boxes agree, and four don’t. With this card, the two detectors agree five times in nine.

“Then I shall choose a better card,” said Albert.

But there are only two kinds of card. On a card with three lines, each YES or NO, either all three lines are the same, or two are the same and one is different. If all three are the same, every box agrees: nine in nine. If two are the same and one is different, it’s the count Albert has just done, with the names changed, and the answer is five in nine. Whichever two lines match, and whichever answer they share, the same five boxes agree. There’s no third kind of card. And a mixture of cards, one kind for some pairs and another kind for others, can only land somewhere between five in nine and nine in nine, because an average can’t be lower than everything it’s an average of.

So, if the photons carry instructions, however they’re written and however they’re mixed, the two detectors agree at least five times in nine: about 56 per cent of the time, or more.

Albert folded the napkin with an air of finality.

The count assumes remarkably little, though a natural first reaction is to look for a loophole in the cards. Perhaps the instructions could be cleverer: longer, or with lines for the time of day, or the temperature, or anything else a photon might notice. But think about everything a photon could possibly use to decide its answer when it reaches a detector. There are only three places such a thing could come from. It could be something the photon carries with it: then it’s written on the card, however complicated the card is, and the count above covers it, because in the end the card has to give an answer at each of the three settings. It could be something it finds at its own detector: the setting, which the card already allows for, or a coin tossed there, which the perfect agreement at the same settings has already ruled out. Or it could be something from the other detector, far away: and that would need a signal, faster than the settings could be chosen and the light could cross. There’s nowhere else for an answer to come from.

Running the test

There was nothing for it but to try. They moved Albert’s box to the middle of the car park, where both photons had the same distance to go, and put a detector at each end, John by The Van and Cal by the café door. Each detector had a little switch with three positions, 1, 2 and 3.

The settings had to be chosen at random, and separately, for every pair. Cal suggested The Van.

RANDOM SETTING: 17

“Asked for a number from one to three,” said Cal, reading it, “The Van has chosen seventeen.” Nobody trusted The Van to be random after that, and in the end each detector had a little box of its own that flicked its switch to a new setting, at random, a million times a second, so fast that no setting could be known before the photons were already on their way.

They let it run for an hour. Many thousands of pairs came through, matched by their times at the two ends. Albert’s till printed the result.

PAIRS: 60,000; AGREED: 50.1 PER CENT

Albert read it twice.

“A half,” said John.

It was a half, and it shouldn’t have been, if Albert’s photons carried instructions. But it is exactly what quantum mechanics says, and the reason is in the curve on John’s napkin from the last journey: the agreement slides down from always to never as the settings turn apart. In the nine boxes:

  • on the diagonal, the settings are the same, and the twins always agree;
  • in the six boxes off the diagonal, the settings are 60 degrees apart or 120 degrees apart, and at 60 degrees apart the twins agree only a quarter of the time;
  • 120 degrees apart comes to exactly the same, because polarisation repeats every half-turn: a detector turned 120 degrees one way is the same as one turned 60 degrees the other, and the twins agree a quarter of the time there too.

Why a quarter, at 60 degrees? Look at it from one end, as John did on the Moon. Whatever answer John’s photon gives, Cal’s twin then behaves as if it were polarised along John’s setting, or across it. Cal’s detector is turned 60 degrees from that, and by the sunglasses’ rule of the tenth journey, a photon meeting a filter turned 60 degrees from its polarisation gets through only a quarter of the time. So a quarter of the time the twins give the same answer, and three quarters of the time they don’t.

Three boxes that always agree, and six that agree only a quarter of the time: that comes, in all, to agreement half the time. Five in nine, at the very least, with instructions; a half, in nature. The gap looks small, 56 per cent against 50, but it’s the gap between something possible and something impossible. No card can get below five in nine, and no mixture of cards can either. And nature, with many thousands of pairs, comes out at a half, a long way outside what chance could explain.

Try it yourself

The figure below is the test, and the instructions are yours. At the top is the card every pair carries: set its three lines with the buttons, YES or NO. Below it are the nine boxes, Cal’s setting across and John’s down, and each box shows how often the results have agreed when the settings fell that way, filled in as the pairs come through. Under them is a scale for the agreement over all the pairs so far, with two marks on it: five in nine, the least any instructions can manage, and a half, nature’s. The table counts the pairs.

Play it, and try every card there is. Three the same, and every box agrees. Two and one, and five boxes agree: the needle settles on five in nine. Try “Shuffle the cards”, which gives every pair a card at random, as Albert’s box might: the needle settles higher still. Then press “Real photons”, and play the pairs as nature does. Look at the boxes off the diagonal: a quarter, every one of them, while the diagonal stays at always. No card, and no mixture of cards, can do that, and the needle falls below the line, to a half.

Bell’s theorem

That is the heart of what John Bell found in 1964, though his own version used different settings and a little algebra. It isn’t a fact about Albert’s box, or about photons, or about quantum mechanics as such. It’s a fact about instructions. Any world in which particles carry their answers with them, and nothing passes between distant detectors faster than light, has a limit on how strongly distant results can be correlated. Quantum mechanics predicts correlations stronger than the limit. So no theory of instructions, however clever, can ever give all of quantum mechanics’ predictions, and an experiment can tell which kind of world we’re in.

Bell’s theorem

John Bell’s result of 1964: no theory of local hidden variables, in which each particle carries its own instructions and nothing travels faster than light, can reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics for entangled particles. In any such theory the results at two distant detectors obey a limit, known as a Bell inequality: in Mermin’s version, they must agree at least five times in nine. Quantum mechanics breaks the limit, so experiment can decide between them. The theorem doesn’t say which side nature is on: only experiments can.

The experiments that followed mostly use a different count from Mermin’s, worked out in 1969 by John Clauser, Michael Horne, Abner Shimony and Richard Holt. It uses two settings a side instead of three, and combines the results at the four ways the settings can fall into a single score. Instructions can score at most two on it. Quantum mechanics, with the settings chosen well, scores about 2.8. It’s harder to explain without a little algebra, but it’s sturdier against the imperfections of real apparatus, and the logic is exactly the same.

The ways out

“There must be a catch,” said Albert. He said it with dignity, but he said it.

There are three, and they’re exactly where the experiments had to work hardest. Each is a way in which instructions could fake the half.

  • A signal. Suppose that, somehow, the setting of John’s detector could reach Cal’s photon, or Cal’s detector, before Cal’s photon was measured. Then the cards needn’t be fixed: Cal’s photon could be told what John chose, and adjust. The cure is to choose the settings at the very last moment, while the photons are in flight, so late that not even light could carry news of one setting to the other end in time.
  • Missing photons. Most photons in a real experiment are never caught, like John’s on the Moon. If the ones that went missing were mostly the awkward ones, the pairs whose cards would have pushed the agreement up, then the pairs caught could seem to break the limit when all of them together don’t. The cure is to catch nearly every photon.
  • Settings that aren’t free. Suppose whatever chooses the settings, the little random boxes on the switches, were itself somehow in league with the cards, so that the cards were written in advance to suit the settings to come. Then all bets are off. The cure is to choose the settings by something that can’t possibly have conspired with the photons.

The first two are ordinary engineering. The third is stranger, and an experiment can only push it further away, never remove it entirely.

Nature’s verdict

Niels finished his tea. Albert poured him another. And John, at Cal’s suggestion, read out on his phone what the experiments had found.

The first was done in 1972, at Berkeley, by Stuart Freedman and John Clauser, with photons given off in pairs by calcium atoms. Their counts took many days to gather. The correlations broke the limit, by the amount quantum mechanics predicted.

In 1982, in a laboratory at Orsay, near Paris, Alain Aspect, Jean Dalibard and Gérard Roger closed most of the first way out. Their two detectors were twelve metres apart, about forty billionths of a second of light, and each had a fast switch that sent the photons to one of two polarisers, changing every ten billionths of a second, while the photons were in flight. The switches changed in a regular rhythm rather than at random, but they changed far too fast for any signal at light speed to carry the news. Quantum mechanics won again.

In 2015, three experiments closed the first two ways out at once, for the first time. In Delft, in the Netherlands, the entangled particles were electrons in two tiny diamonds, 1.3 kilometres apart, measured in their spin instead of their polarisation; in Vienna and in Boulder, Colorado, they were photons, caught with detectors so good that almost none went missing. All three broke the limit.

Then the third way out. On 30 November 2016, about a hundred thousand people around the world played an online game for twelve hours, pressing keys as unpredictably as they could, and made 97,347,490 random choices between them. The choices went, as they were made, to thirteen experiments in twelve laboratories on five continents, to set their detectors. It was called the Big Bell Test. If the settings had been in league with the photons, then so were a hundred thousand people. The limit broke anyway.

“I’d have pressed the same key every time,” said Cal.

“Then your settings would have been very boring,” said Albert, “but it would not have mattered, as long as your boredom was not in league with the photons.”

Settings chosen by quasars

The last experiment John read out was done just across the sea.

On the night of 11 January 2018, on the Roque de los Muchachos on La Palma, a team led from Vienna borrowed two of the biggest telescopes on the mountain, the William Herschel Telescope and the Telescopio Nazionale Galileo, which stand about a kilometre apart. Between them, beside a third telescope, was a source of entangled photons, sending one photon of each pair about half a kilometre through the night air to each.

The telescopes weren’t there to catch the twins. Each was pointed at a quasar: the blazing core of a galaxy so far away that its light had been travelling for billions of years. And each photon of quasar light that arrived chose a setting. It was sent one way if it was redder than a certain colour, the other way if it was bluer, and which way it went set the detector for the next twin, within a few millionths of a second. A photon’s colour was decided when it left its quasar, and nothing on its way could have known what it would be needed for.

One quasar’s light had set out 7.78 billion years ago. The other’s had set out 12.21 billion years ago, when the universe was less than two billion years old. If the settings and the twins’ cards had been arranged in advance to fool the experiment, the arrangement must have been made at least 7.8 billion years ago, long before the Sun or the Earth existed. Over seventeen minutes, the limit broke. The chance that instructions could have produced what they saw, assuming the photons they caught were a fair sample of all of them, was less than one in a hundred billion billion.

One of John’s own pictures has light in it as old as that. His picture of the Whirlpool Galaxy, taken from his garden, is full of faint dots that are quasars, and the light of the farthest of them set out about 12.2 billion years ago: as old as the light choosing the settings on La Palma.

The prize

On 4 October 2022 it was announced that the Nobel Prize in Physics would go to three of the people John had been reading about: Alain Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger, whose group in Vienna had sent the twins between the islands, from La Palma to the observatory up the road, and chosen settings by quasars. It was awarded “for experiments with entangled photons, establishing the violation of Bell inequalities and pioneering quantum information science”.

John put the phone down.

Albert pays

For a while nobody said anything. Outside, a coach was unloading at the edge of the car park, and someone was laughing at the view.

Albert sat looking at the napkin with its nine boxes.

“I always hoped,” he said at last, “that someone would find the instructions.” He turned the napkin over, as though they might be on the back. “I spent a great many afternoons looking out of a window, thinking about where they might be.”

Niels put a hand on his arm, very lightly, and said nothing at all, which for Niels was a great deal.

Then Albert got up, went behind the counter, and took down a plate. He counted twelve doughnuts onto it from the tray, slowly, rolling each in a little more lemon sugar as he went, and set it in front of Niels.

“You need not,” said Niels.

“Debts,” said Albert, “are a matter of principle.”

Niels took one, and considered it for a long time.

“It was exactly the right question to ask,” he said softly, so that everyone leaned in. “It took the rest of the century to learn how to answer it.”

What the bet settled

It’s easy to claim too much about what Albert lost, in either direction.

What is ruled out is every theory of local instructions: every theory in which each particle carries its answers with it from the start, nothing passes between distant detectors faster than light, and the settings are chosen freely. That’s a statement about nature, not about quantum mechanics. Bell’s limit is broken in the experiments themselves, so any theory that replaces quantum mechanics one day, however different it may be, must break it too. Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen’s argument was a good one, but something it took for granted has turned out to be false: whatever is going on, the twins don’t carry their answers with them, the way gloves do.

What isn’t ruled out comes in three kinds.

  • Theories in which something does link the two particles across any distance, instantly or as good as. In 1952 the physicist David Bohm worked out one, in which particles have definite places and paths, guided by a wave spread across all of space; a later journey in this series meets it properly. Bell admired it, and it was by studying it that he came to ask whether every theory of its kind had to link distant places, which is the question his theorem answered. Even in such theories, though, the link can’t be used to send a message, for the reasons John found on the Moon: each side alone still sees a fair coin.
  • Theories in which the settings were never free: in which everything, the quasars’ light and a hundred thousand people’s fingers included, was arranged from the beginning of the universe to fool us. It can’t be disproved. Most physicists find it harder to believe than anything else on this list, since every experiment ever done assumes that the experimenter’s choices aren’t secretly arranged in advance, though a few take it seriously. The quasars pushed any such arrangement back at least 7.8 billion years.
  • Quantum mechanics as it stands, which predicts the half, and which in all of these experiments has never once been wrong. In quantum mechanics, as it is usually read, a photon of the pair simply has no answer to a question until it is asked, and the pair is one thing, however far apart its photons are.

If Albert’s gloves were to describe the twins as quantum mechanics does, they would have to be very strange gloves indeed: gloves with no hand at all while they sat in their boxes, each of which became a left or a right only when its box was opened, and always the opposite of its partner, whichever box was opened first, and in whatever order anyone flying past said they were opened. No glove behaves like that, which is the whole point. The everyday pictures, gloves and socks and packed boxes, are all pictures of instructions, and instructions are what the experiments have ruled out.

What survives is the speed limit for messages. Nothing anyone has done with entangled particles has ever sent a message faster than light, and quantum mechanics says nothing ever will: whatever links the twins, it can’t be used to signal, so relativity’s order of cause and effect is safe. But the correlations themselves can’t be explained by anything carried along with the particles at or below the speed of light. Some physicists call that non-locality. Niels, when Cal asked him what he would call it, thought for a long time, and said he would prefer to say that the experiment must be considered as a whole.

Bell himself thought the instructions were the reasonable explanation, and said later that it was a pity they didn’t work.

Back to Teide

By evening Niels had gone. He had shared his dozen with everyone in the café, and insisted on leaving the last two for John and Cal. Albert had put the black box back in the cupboard, between the currant dough and the oven gloves, and the tripod in the corner.

The stars came out over the plateau. Somewhere across the sea to the west, on La Palma’s dark mountain, the big telescopes were opening their domes for another night’s work.

Cal picked up one of the two doughnuts and looked at it in the light from the café window.

“So a photon doesn’t have an answer until it’s asked,” he said.

“So it seems,” said John.

“And before then it’s not one thing or the other.” Cal turned the doughnut round in his fingers. “Then why do I never see a doughnut in two states at once? A doughnut is always just a doughnut, sitting here, in one place, uneaten.” He considered it. “For the moment.”

Albert, collecting the cups, stopped beside the counter and looked at the glass with the grain of sugar under it, and then at the stack of empty doughnut boxes on the shelf.

“That,” he said, “is a question for a box.” He took one down and set it on the table, its lid closed. “In the morning.”
