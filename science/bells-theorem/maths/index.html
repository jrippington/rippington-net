Mermin’s count

This is N. David Mermin’s argument, from “Bringing home the atomic world: Quantum mysteries for anybody” (American Journal of Physics 49, 940, 1981), in the photon version the article uses.

The settings. Each detector has three settings, the same three on both sides: θ1=0°\theta_1 = 0°θ1​=0°, θ2=60°\theta_2 = 60°θ2​=60°, θ3=120°\theta_3 = 120°θ3​=120°. For every pair, John’s detector picks a setting iii and Cal’s a setting jjj, each at random and independently, so each of the nine ordered pairs (i,j)(i, j)(i,j) has probability 19\tfrac1991​.

Why both photons carry the same card. Suppose each photon carries, from the source, an answer xk∈{YES,NO}x_k \in \{\text{YES}, \text{NO}\}xk​∈{YES,NO} for each setting kkk (John’s photon) and yky_kyk​ (Cal’s). At equal settings the results always agree, so xk=ykx_k = y_kxk​=yk​ for every kkk: one card, carried by both.

Why the answers can’t be left to chance at the detectors. Suppose instead that each photon, on reaching a detector at setting kkk, said YES with some probability qkq_kqk​, independently of the other photon (anything local the two share is already on the card). Then at equal settings the two agree with probability

qk2+(1−qk)2=1−2qk(1−qk),q_k^2 + (1 - q_k)^2 = 1 - 2q_k(1 - q_k),qk2​+(1−qk​)2=1−2qk​(1−qk​),

which is less than 1 unless qkq_kqk​ is 0 or 1. But they always agree, so every qkq_kqk​ is 0 or 1: the answers are fixed. (In general, any local model with perfect agreement at equal settings is a mixture of fixed cards.)

The gap. Instructions give at least 59≈55.6\tfrac59 \approx 55.695​≈55.6 per cent; nature gives 505050 per cent: a gap of 118\tfrac1{18}181​, about 5.6 percentage points. With NNN pairs, the agreement measured by chance scatters about its true value by roughly

σ≈p(1−p)N,\sigma \approx \sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{N}},σ≈Np(1−p)​​,

about 0.2 percentage points for N=60,000N = 60{,}000N=60,000 and p=12p = \tfrac12p=21​. The gap is then more than 25 times the scatter: not something chance could produce.

Bell’s theorem, in this form. Any theory in which the results are fixed by what the photons carry (local hidden variables), each detector’s result depends only on its own setting, and the settings are chosen independently of what the photons carry, gives P(agree)≥59P(\text{agree}) \ge \tfrac59P(agree)≥95​ here. Quantum mechanics gives 12\tfrac1221​. So no such theory reproduces quantum mechanics.

The test the experiments use

The CHSH inequality. John Clauser, Michael Horne, Abner Shimony and Richard Holt (1969) used two settings a side, a,a′a, a'a,a′ for one detector and b,b′b, b'b,b′ for the other. Score each result +1+1+1 for YES and −1-1−1 for NO, and let E(a,b)E(a, b)E(a,b) be the average of the product of the two scores at settings aaa and bbb: +1+1+1 if they always agree, −1-1−1 if they never do. Then form

S=E(a,b)−E(a,b′)+E(a′,b)+E(a′,b′).S = E(a, b) - E(a, b') + E(a', b) + E(a', b').S=E(a,b)−E(a,b′)+E(a′,b)+E(a′,b′).

The limit for instructions. If the four answers A,A′,B,B′=±1A, A', B, B' = \pm 1A,A′,B,B′=±1 are fixed in advance, then

A(B−B′)+A′(B+B′)=±2,A(B - B') + A'(B + B') = \pm 2,A(B−B′)+A′(B+B′)=±2,

because one of the brackets is 0 and the other is ±2\pm 2±2. Averaging over any mixture of cards, ∣S∣≤2|S| \le 2∣S∣≤2.

The quantum value. For the twins, E(a,b)=P(agree)−P(disagree)=cos⁡2(a−b)E(a, b) = P(\text{agree}) - P(\text{disagree}) = \cos 2(a - b)E(a,b)=P(agree)−P(disagree)=cos2(a−b). With a=0°a = 0°a=0°, a′=45°a' = 45°a′=45°, b=22.5°b = 22.5°b=22.5° and b′=67.5°b' = 67.5°b′=67.5°, each of the four terms is ±cos⁡45°\pm\cos 45°±cos45° with the signs that add, and

S=4cos⁡45°=22≈2.83,S = 4 \cos 45° = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83,S=4cos45°=22​≈2.83,

the most quantum mechanics allows (Tsirelson’s bound).

What the experiments found. Between La Palma and Tenerife in 2007, S=2.508±0.037S = 2.508 \pm 0.037S=2.508±0.037, more than 13 standard deviations above 2 (Ursin et al.). In the cosmic Bell test of 2018 the measured values were 2.65 and 2.63 for its two runs; after allowing for the settings that might have come from local light rather than the quasars, the chance that a local-realist mechanism produced the first run’s correlations was at most 7.4×10−217.4 \times 10^{-21}7.4×10−21, less than one in a hundred billion billion (Rauch et al., Physical Review Letters 121, 080403, 2018). Its quasars had redshifts of 0.964 and 3.911, so their light set out 7.78 and 12.21 billion years ago; any local mechanism that could have arranged the settings and the photons’ answers must have acted no more recently than 7.78 billion years ago.
