The Sun’s power and its loss of mass

The Sun gives out L⊙=3.828×1026L_\odot = 3.828 \times 10^{26}L⊙​=3.828×1026 watts (the International Astronomical Union’s nominal value). By E=mc2E = mc^2E=mc2, light carrying away energy at that rate carries away mass at

about four and a quarter million tonnes a second, the figure of The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe.

The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old, so it’s about halfway through. In all that time it has lost to light

three hundredths of one per cent of its mass.

Why it’s so slow. The first step, p+p→2H+e++νep + p \to {}^2\text{H} + e^+ + \nu_ep+p→2H+e++νe​, needs a proton to turn into a neutron during the brief encounter, by the weak force. Its rate at the Sun’s centre is so small that it has never been measured in a laboratory; it’s calculated, and checked against the weak decay of tritium. The answer is that a proton in the core waits on average about nine billion years before it takes part. Every later step of the proton chain takes seconds to about a million years, so this one sets the pace.

The thermostat

What holds the Sun up. Each layer of the Sun is pressed down by the weight of the layers above it, and held up by the pressure of the gas below. Balance at every depth (the sixth journey’s balance) means

The long walk out

A random walk. Light in the Sun’s inner layers goes a short way, is caught by the gas, and is given out again in a random direction. Take steps of length ℓ\ellℓ, each in a direction chosen at random and independent of the last. After NNN steps the walk’s displacement d=∑iℓi\mathbf{d} = \sum_i \boldsymbol{\ell}_id=∑i​ℓi​ has

⟨∣d∣2⟩=∑i⟨∣ℓi∣2⟩+∑i≠j⟨ℓi⋅ℓj⟩=Nℓ2+0,\langle |\mathbf{d}|^2 \rangle = \sum_i \langle |\boldsymbol{\ell}_i|^2 \rangle + \sum_{i \ne j} \langle \boldsymbol{\ell}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{\ell}_j \rangle = N \ell^2 + 0,⟨∣d∣2⟩=i∑​⟨∣ℓi​∣2⟩+i=j∑​⟨ℓi​⋅ℓj​⟩=Nℓ2+0,

since steps in random directions are as likely to point one way as the other, and their products average to nothing. So the typical distance is ℓN\ell \sqrt{N}ℓN​: twice as far takes four times as many steps, and to get a distance RRR from the start takes about N=(R/ℓ)2N = (R/\ell)^2N=(R/ℓ)2 steps. (For steps of fixed length, the average number of steps to first reach a distance RRR is exactly (R/ℓ)2(R/\ell)^2(R/ℓ)2, in two dimensions or three.)

Light’s walk. For light in a gas the free paths are of random length, averaging ℓ\ellℓ, and the walk spreads like heat through a bar or ink through water, with a diffusion coefficient D=ℓc/3D = \ell c / 3D=ℓc/3. The time for it to spread across a distance RRR is then about

the time scale R. Mitalas and K. R. Sills (1992) use. A step’s length is the light’s free path, ℓ=1/(κρ)\ell = 1/(\kappa \rho)ℓ=1/(κρ), where κ\kappaκ is how strongly the gas catches light (its opacity, per kilogram) and ρ\rhoρ its density.

How the Sun’s sound bends

The speed of sound. In a gas, sound travels at

At the Sun’s centre, T=15.7T = 15.7T=15.7 million degrees and μ≈0.85\mu \approx 0.85μ≈0.85 (the core has turned much of its hydrogen into helium), which gives about 500 kilometres a second; at the bottom of the convection zone, T≈2.2T \approx 2.2T≈2.2 million degrees and μ≈0.6\mu \approx 0.6μ≈0.6 give about 223 kilometres a second, which helioseismology measures; at the surface, at 5,772 degrees, it’s about 8 kilometres a second. Sudbury’s two counts. In heavy water, the Sun’s highest-energy neutrinos (from boron-8) can do two things. A neutrino of the Sun’s type can turn the deuteron’s neutron into a proton, νe+d→p+p+e−\nu_e + d \to p + p + e^-νe​+d→p+p+e−, which counts only that type. A neutrino of any of the three types can knock the deuteron apart, νx+d→p+n+νx\nu_x + d \to p + n + \nu_xνx​+d→p+n+νx​, which counts all of them. In 2002 the two counts gave, in millions per square centimetre per second,

Φνe≈1.76,Φall≈5.09 (±0.6),against 5.05 predicted (Bahcall and colleagues, 2001).\Phi_{\nu_e} \approx 1.76, \qquad \Phi_\text{all} \approx 5.09\,(\pm 0.6), \qquad \text{against } 5.05 \text{ predicted (Bahcall and colleagues, 2001)}.Φνe​​≈1.76,Φall​≈5.09(±0.6),against 5.05 predicted (Bahcall and colleagues, 2001).

A third of the Sun’s type, and all of them, together, as predicted.

Why they change. A neutrino made as the Sun’s type, νe\nu_eνe​, is a superposition of two kinds of definite mass (the third barely takes part here), with mixing angle θ\thetaθ:

∣νe⟩=cos⁡θ ∣ν1⟩+sin⁡θ ∣ν2⟩.|\nu_e\rangle = \cos\theta\,|\nu_1\rangle + \sin\theta\,|\nu_2\rangle.∣νe​⟩=cosθ∣ν1​⟩+sinθ∣ν2​⟩.

Each kind’s arrow turns at a rate set by its energy, and for the same momentum ppp the two energies differ by about (m22−m12)c4/2pc(m_2^2 - m_1^2)c^4 / 2pc(m22​−m12​)c4/2pc. Over a distance LLL the arrows drift apart, and the chance of still finding the Sun’s type is, in empty space,

If the two masses were equal (Δm2=0\Delta m^2 = 0Δm2=0) nothing would change, so seeing the change proves that neutrinos have mass. For the boron-8 neutrinos, the Sun’s own dense core changes the mixture as they leave (the Mikheyev-Smirnov-Wolfenstein effect), and they come out almost entirely as ν2\nu_2ν2​, which is the Sun’s type with a chance of sin⁡2θ≈0.31\sin^2\theta \approx 0.31sin2θ≈0.31, with θ≈34°\theta \approx 34°θ≈34°: about a third, as Davis and Sudbury found. The measured mass difference, Δm2≈7.5×10−5 eV2\Delta m^2 \approx 7.5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{eV}^2Δm2≈7.5×10−5 eV2, gives no masses themselves; the KATRIN experiment’s limit, of 2025, is that the neutrino’s mass is less than 0.45 eV, so more than a million times less than the electron’s, 511,000 eV.
