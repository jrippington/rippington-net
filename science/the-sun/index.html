The Van flies to the Sun, as close as any spacecraft has been, behind a folding sunshade made for car parks. The kettle boils by itself. The surface seethes like Albert's fryer, the energy in its light set out from the core while there were still mammoths, and the Sun turns out to keep its own thermostat, to ring like a bell, and to send ghostly particles straight out of its heart, which for thirty years nobody could count.

Destination: the Sun

The dashboard was still saying it in the morning.

DESTINATION: THE SUN. PLEASE CONSULT YOUR DEALER

Nobody had ever found The Van’s dealer, so John and Cal consulted Albert instead, who gave the matter some thought over his first coffee.

“Every star you have looked at in the last eight journeys,” he said, “you have known by its light alone. The Sun is the only one close enough to visit.” He went to his cupboard of props and came back with the oven gloves, the ones nobody could have felt a peppercorn through, and handed them to Cal. “You will want these.”

“Aren’t you coming?”

“I have a café to run,” said Albert. “And someone must be here to tell you what you have seen.”

Cal spent the next half hour going through The Van’s cupboards, and came back with the only sun protection on board: the folding silver sunshade from the windscreen, kept for hot days in the car park. He fitted it across the front of The Van, where it crackled in the breeze. The dashboard considered it.

SUNSHADE: DEPLOYED. RATED FOR CAR PARKS

They went anyway. The Van rose from the car park as it had on the day John took it to the Moon, in A Pair of Gloves, out of the blue of the morning and into the black, and the speedometer, which reads true now except when it has nothing to measure against, went back to its old habit and said 17 mph.

The Sun is about 150 million kilometres from the café, and The Van was going as close as any spacecraft ever has. On 24 December 2024, NASA’s Parker Solar Probe swept past it about 6.1 million kilometres from its surface, at about 690,000 kilometres an hour, the fastest anything people have built has ever moved. It survived behind a heat shield nearly two and a half metres wide and about eleven centimetres thick, of carbon foam between sheets of carbon, while its instruments, in the shield’s shadow, were kept at about the temperature of a warm room. It’s named after Eugene Parker, who worked out in 1958 that the Sun must be blowing a wind of gas out into space in every direction, which few believed until spacecraft flew through it; he was the first person ever to watch the launch of a spacecraft named after him, on 12 August 2018, eight years to the day before John and Cal’s eclipse. The Van had the sunshade from the car park.

The Sun grew, and grew. It’s about 1.4 million kilometres across, 109 times the width of the Earth, and so big that more than a million Earths would fit inside it; it holds 99.8 per cent of all the mass in the Solar System. From the plateau it’s a disc about half a degree across, half the width of your little finger held at arm’s length. At six million kilometres it was more than twenty times wider, a blazing wall filling a great patch of the sky, and its light, nearly five hundred times as fierce as on the plateau at noon, came through The Van’s windows round the edges of the sunshade in hot white blades.

In the galley, something clicked, and then roared.

KETTLE BOILING. KETTLE NOT SWITCHED ON

Cal put the oven gloves on.

A boiling surface

Through the probe’s darkest filter, the one with a symbol like a pair of sunglasses, the Sun’s surface wasn’t smooth at all. It was grained all over, like the top of a pan of porridge on the boil: bright cells packed together, each with a dark rim, every cell slowly swelling, breaking up and being replaced by another, the whole surface seething.

SURFACE: OBSERVED. CLOSE UP

“It’s the fryer,” said Cal.

It is. When Albert heats the oil in the fryer, the oil at the bottom, nearest the flame, gets hot first, and hot oil is a little lighter than cool oil, so it rises. At the top it gives up its heat to the air and cools, and cool oil, heavier, sinks again at the sides. Watch hot oil closely, or a pan of soup, and the surface breaks up into cells, rising in the middle of each and sinking round the edges, turning over and over and carrying the heat up from the bottom as it goes. The Sun does exactly that with its outer layers. Each of the bright cells on its surface is a column of hot gas welling up from below, at a kilometre or two a second; each dark rim is where gas is sinking again, having given up its heat to space. They’re called granules, and each is about a thousand kilometres across, about the width of Spain. Each lasts about ten minutes.

Convection

Carrying heat by moving the hot stuff itself. Heated from below, a liquid or gas expands and gets lighter, so it rises; at the top it gives its heat away, cools, gets heavier and sinks; and the circulation carries heat upwards far faster than the heat could seep through on its own. It’s what turns over the oil in a fryer, the air above a radiator and the outer third of the Sun, whose surface is covered in the tops of rising columns of hot gas, each about a thousand kilometres across.

Here and there among the granules were sunspots, dark patches often bigger than the Earth, where the Sun’s magnetic field bunches up and blocks the hot gas rising from below, so that the surface there is a couple of thousand degrees cooler than the 5,500 degrees round it. Cooler, but still white hot: they only look dark against the rest. Their numbers rise and fall over a cycle of about eleven years, and the latest cycle reached its peak in October 2024. They were among the first things anyone saw through a telescope. In 1612 Galileo drew them day after day, projecting the Sun’s image onto paper, and watched them march across its face and vanish round its edge, which showed that the Sun turns, about once a month; since it’s gas, not a solid ball, its equator turns faster than its poles.

And off the Sun’s edge, standing up into its thin outer atmosphere, were prominences: great arches and clouds of glowing gas, held up off the surface by the magnetic field, many times taller than the Earth is wide. John had photographed one. On 12 August 2026 he and Cal went to Soria, in Spain, for a total eclipse of the Sun, when the Moon covered it completely for a minute and forty-one seconds, and his picture shows a prominence about 110,000 kilometres long, more than eight times the Earth’s width.

The golden glow round the Moon in John’s picture is the corona, the Sun’s outer atmosphere, normally lost in the glare and only seen when the Moon covers the Sun. It’s one of the Sun’s puzzles. The visible surface is about 5,500 degrees. The corona above it is one to two million. Heat doesn’t normally flow from something cooler to something hotter, so something else must be heating the corona, probably the magnetic field, twisting and snapping and shaking the gas, but exactly how is still being worked out, and finding out is one of the reasons Parker Solar Probe was sent.

Total eclipses have done important science before. In 1919, one was used to measure the bending of starlight passing the Sun, in Starlight Bent by the Sun, and in 1868, as the first journey found, one gave away a new element in the Sun’s light: helium.

How it shines

All this is the outside. The Sun’s power comes from deep inside, from its core, the inner quarter of its radius, where it’s about fifteen million degrees and the gas, squeezed by the weight of everything above it, is about 150 times as dense as water.

There, as Through the Wall found, protons, the nuclei of hydrogen atoms, crash into each other hard enough, now and then, to tunnel through the electric push between them and fuse. Through a few steps, four protons become one nucleus of helium, which weighs about seven tenths of one per cent less than the four did. The difference comes out as energy, by E = mc². The Sun turns about four million tonnes of itself into light every second.

The fusion is also astonishingly slow, and Through the Wall left the reason for a later series. When two protons touch, they make, for a moment, something that can’t last: two positive charges with nothing to hold them. They fly apart again almost every time. Just occasionally, in that moment, one of the two protons turns into a neutron, giving off a positron and a tiny particle called a neutrino, and then the pair can stick, as a nucleus of heavy hydrogen, a proton and a neutron together. Turning a proton into a neutron is the work of a force called the weak force, and it’s well named: it acts so rarely that a proton in the Sun’s core waits, on average, about nine billion years before it happens to it. The rest of the steps to helium are quick. That one step sets the pace of the whole Sun.

It’s the reason the Sun has lasted. If protons fused every time they touched, the Sun would have burned through its fuel almost at once. Instead it has been burning steadily for about four and a half billion years (the twelfth journey dates it, from the oldest grains of rock in meteorites), and it’s about halfway through.

The Sun’s thermostat

That still leaves a puzzle. If the Sun’s fusion goes so steeply with temperature, as Through the Wall found it does, why doesn’t it get out of hand? A little hotter, and it would burn a lot faster; burning faster, it would make more heat, and get hotter still. Every fire on Earth works like that, until it runs out of fuel. Why doesn’t the Sun flare up, or die down?

The answer is the balance the sixth journey found in every star: the gas pushing outwards, with its heat, exactly holding up the weight of everything above it, pressing in.

That weight is colossal. At the Sun’s centre, the pressure of all the layers above is about 250 billion times the pressure of the air at sea level. The core can only hold that up by pushing back just as hard, and a gas pushes harder the denser and the hotter it is. So the weight of the Sun decides how hot its core must be: about fifteen million degrees, for a star of the Sun’s mass. Fusion doesn’t choose that temperature. Anything cooler simply couldn’t hold the Sun up. (What that means for stars heavier or lighter than the Sun is the next journey’s story.) Follow what happens if the core gets a little too hot:

  • the hotter gas pushes harder, and the core swells;
  • a swollen core is less squeezed, and gas that expands cools, as air rushing out of a bicycle tyre’s valve does;
  • so the core cools down, and the fusion slows back down to where it was.

And if the core gets a little too cool:

  • the cooler gas pushes less hard, and the weight above squeezes the core smaller;
  • squeezed gas heats up, as it does in a bicycle pump;
  • so the core warms up again, and the fusion picks back up.

So a star does the opposite of a fire. Give a fire extra heat and it burns hotter. Give a star’s core extra heat, and the core swells and ends up cooler; take heat away, and it shrinks and ends up hotter. The weight of the Sun sets the temperature of its core, and fusion has no choice but to run at whatever rate that temperature allows, exactly the rate at which the Sun’s light carries energy away. The Sun has a thermostat, and it’s gravity.

Is there a way out? Only if the core weren’t held in by its own weight. Put the same fusing gas in an imaginary box with unbreakable walls, so that it couldn’t swell or shrink, and the thermostat would be gone: a little extra heat would stay as heat, fusion would quicken, and the box’s contents would run away, hotter and hotter, as a fire does. That’s roughly what a bomb is. The Sun doesn’t explode because it isn’t in a box. It’s held together by nothing but its own gravity, and gravity gives way.

Nudge the core hotter or cooler and let it go: held by its weight, it swells and cools, or shrinks and warms, and settles back to the Sun’s fifteen million degrees, most of the way within a few million years. Then put it in a box, and nudge it again.

For the mathematically inclinedThe thermostat

That’s why the Sun is so steady, and why life has had billions of years of steady sunlight. It isn’t perfectly steady: as its hydrogen turns to helium, the core very slowly shrinks and heats, and when the Sun formed it shone only about seven tenths as brightly as it does now. What that means for the Earth’s future is for the seventeenth journey.

A long way out

Cal, sweltering in the oven gloves, looked out at the Sun’s surface, seething a few million kilometres away.

“So the light coming in through the window,” he said, “was made in the core. How long ago?”

“Eight minutes,” said John. “Or twenty seconds, from here.”

“From the surface. I meant from the middle.”

That turns out to be a very long time. The energy made in the core sets off as light, but the inside of the Sun is so dense that the light can’t get far: in the core, it travels less than a millimetre before it’s caught by a particle of the gas, which soon gives it out again, in a new direction, chosen at random. Then it’s caught again, and given out again, in another direction. The energy does a random walk, a drunkard’s walk, each step a fraction of a millimetre, each step in any direction at all, often backwards. A random walk gets somewhere, but slowly: to get twice as far from where it started, it needs four times as many steps. To get from the core to the outer third of the Sun, the energy takes about two million billion billion steps.

The best calculation, made in 1992 by the astronomers R. Mitalas and K. R. Sills, using the Sun’s real density and the way its gas catches light at each depth, gives about 170,000 years. (Older estimates said tens of thousands of years; you will sometimes read “millions”, which is wrong.) No single bit of light makes the whole trip. The light is caught and given out again countless times, and each time the energy is handed on, so what wanders out of the Sun is the energy, passed from one particle of light to the next.

Then it gets to the outer third. There the gas is cool enough for its atoms to keep hold of some of their electrons, and atoms with electrons catch light far more readily than bare nuclei do, so the light is held back even harder and the heat piles up underneath. The gas does what Albert’s oil does when it’s heated faster than it can pass the heat on: it starts to turn over, in great convection cells, and the boiling carries the energy the rest of the way up in a matter of weeks, to the granules on the surface. From there, as light again, it takes eight minutes and twenty seconds to cross the 150 million kilometres to the café.

So the sunlight warming the plateau this afternoon set out from the Sun’s core about 170,000 years ago, when there were mammoths in Europe and our own species was young, in Africa. It has spent almost all that time inside the Sun, and only the last eight minutes crossing to the Earth.

How we see inside

“But nobody’s been in there,” said Cal. “So how does anyone know it’s fifteen million degrees in the middle? Or that it boils in the outer third?”

The surface hides everything below it. There are, though, two ways of seeing inside the Sun, and The Van, parked a few million kilometres from it, was in no better position to use them than an astronomer on the plateau.

INSIDE OF THE SUN: NEVER SEEN. HEARD, AND COUNTED

The Sun rings

The first is sound.

In 1960, at the Mount Wilson Observatory in California, Robert Leighton, Robert Noyes and George Simon found that patches of the Sun’s surface are rising and falling, all the time, each patch going up and down about every five minutes, at a few hundred metres a second. In 1970 Roger Ulrich, and then others, worked out why: the Sun is full of sound waves, trapped inside it and ringing, and the rising and falling is the surface moving to the sound. A bell rings at notes set by its shape and what it’s made of, and so does the Sun.

Then somebody had to hear the notes. In 1979 a team from the University of Birmingham, with a colleague from the Canaries’ own institute of astrophysics, measured the movement of the whole face of the Sun at once, from the observatory at Izaña on Tenerife, the observatory up the road from the café. They found that the Sun’s slow breathing wasn’t noise. It was made of sharp notes, a whole ladder of them, evenly spaced, the notes of the whole Sun ringing as one. Their paper in Nature that December was one of the beginnings of a new science, helioseismology: the study of the inside of the Sun by its sound, as seismologists study the inside of the Earth by its earthquakes.

How can sound tell you what’s inside? Sound travels faster through hotter gas, and the Sun gets hotter towards the middle, so a sound wave heading down into it at a slant speeds up as it goes deeper, and the deeper part of its front outruns the shallower part, which turns it round, and back up towards the surface. You can hear the same thing on a still, cold night, when the air near the ground is colder than the air above, and a train or a church bell several kilometres away sounds strangely close: its sound, setting off upwards, is bent back down to you. In the Sun, a wave that sets off steeply downwards dives deep, nearly to the core, before it’s bent back; a wave that sets off at a shallow slant turns back just under the surface. Each wave reaches up to the surface, bounces back down off it, and goes round and round the Sun, and each note the Sun rings is made by sound waves travelling to particular depths and back. How high each note is depends on how fast sound travels at the depths it reaches, and so on the temperature there.

The Sun rings with millions of notes at once, and they’ve been measured, from the ground at stations round the world, some of them on Tenerife, and from spacecraft, to better than a part in a hundred thousand. Together they map the inside. They show where the boiling outer third begins, 71.3 per cent of the way out from the centre; how the inside turns (the outer layers turn faster at the equator than near the poles, as the surface does, but the deep inside turns like a solid ball); and how fast sound travels all the way down to the core, in agreement with the calculations of the Sun’s inside to within a fraction of a per cent.

Ghost particles

The second way in is straight through.

Every time the Sun makes a nucleus of helium, two of its protons have turned into neutrons, and each of those turnings sets free a neutrino, which is about as close to nothing as a particle can be and still exist.

Neutrino

A particle with no electric charge and a mass so tiny it has never been measured, only limited: at least a million times lighter than an electron. Neutrinos feel neither the electric force nor the strong force, only the weak force and gravity, so they pass through almost anything: most of the Sun’s neutrinos fly through the whole Earth without touching a single atom. They come in three types, and as a neutrino travels it can change from one type to another. The Sun makes them in its core, about two for every nucleus of helium.

A neutrino made in the Sun’s core doesn’t do a random walk. It flies straight out through the whole Sun as if it weren’t there, in a little over two seconds, and eight minutes later it’s at the Earth. About sixty-five billion of them pass through every square centimetre of your skin every second, the area of your thumbnail, day and night: at night they come up at you through the whole Earth, which barely slows them. They are the only messengers to come to us straight from the Sun’s core, and they say what’s happening there now, not 170,000 years ago.

The trouble is catching them. In 1968 Raymond Davis, at the Homestake gold mine in South Dakota, put a tank of 615 tonnes of dry-cleaning fluid about a kilometre and a half underground, where the rock above kept out everything else from space. The fluid is rich in chlorine, and once in a long while, about once every two days in the whole tank, a neutrino would hit a chlorine nucleus and turn it into a nucleus of argon, and every month or two Davis flushed the tank and counted the argon atoms: a handful at a time, out of a tank the size of a house. The calculations of the Sun’s core, by John Bahcall, said how many there should be. Davis found about a third.

For thirty years, nobody could say why. Perhaps the experiment was wrong, though it was checked and checked again. Perhaps the Sun’s core was cooler than the calculations said, though the Sun’s ringing, as it came to be measured, said it wasn’t. Or perhaps something happened to the neutrinos on the way.

It was the neutrinos. Davis’s experiment could only catch one of the three types of neutrino, the type the Sun makes. In 2001 and 2002 the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory, in a nickel mine in Ontario, a sphere of a thousand tonnes of heavy water two kilometres underground, counted the Sun’s neutrinos two ways at once: the ones still of the Sun’s type, and all three types together. The Sun’s type came to about a third. All three together came to what the calculations said the Sun was making. The neutrinos weren’t missing. Two thirds of them had changed type on the way to the Earth, into kinds Davis’s tank couldn’t see.

NEUTRINOS: ALL ACCOUNTED FOR. TWO THIRDS IN DISGUISE

How can a particle change what it is? A Clock Hand for Every Path has the answer. A neutrino made in the Sun is made as one type, but each type is really a superposition of three underlying kinds with very slightly different masses, each with its own arrow, and the arrows of things with different masses turn at different rates as they travel. As the arrows drift out of step, the mixture changes, and with it the chance of finding the neutrino as one type or another. (For the Sun’s most energetic neutrinos, the ones Sudbury counted, most of the change happens on the way out through the Sun’s own dense gas, which nudges the mixture, but it’s the same superposition underneath.) Neutrinos changing type are superposition on the scale of the Solar System. Davis and Masatoshi Koshiba shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2002, and Arthur McDonald, who led Sudbury, shared it in 2015 with Takaaki Kajita of Japan’s Super-Kamiokande.

And because the mixture can only change if the three kinds have different masses, it proved that neutrinos have mass, however tiny, which nobody had been sure of. The Sun’s core, seen by its sound and counted by its ghosts, was just as the calculations said: fifteen million degrees, and fusing.

The observatory up the road

They came home in the afternoon, The Van’s paint a little blistered round the edge of the sunshade, the kettle cooling, the oven gloves scorched. On the way down to the café they went past the observatory, on its ridge above the plateau, where several of its white domes and towers aren’t for the night sky at all. They’re for the Sun.

The observatory up the road is given over mainly to watching the Sun, with some of the best solar telescopes in Europe: GREGOR, whose mirror is a metre and a half across, the largest solar telescope in Europe, inaugurated in 2012; the Vacuum Tower Telescope, from the late 1980s, which looks at the Sun through a tube emptied of air so that the Sun’s own heat can’t make the air inside shimmer; and THEMIS, built by France and Italy to measure the Sun’s magnetic fields. They’re here because the air above the plateau is some of the steadiest anywhere, and steady air is everything when you’re trying to see detail on something as fierce as the Sun.

That evening, with the Sun going down over the ridge, John showed Albert his picture of the eclipse on his laptop, the corona and the long pink prominence, and the video of the trip he and Cal had made to Soria to see it.

Warning

Never look at the Sun, or point a camera, binoculars or a telescope at it, without a proper solar filter on the front. It will damage the camera, and through a viewfinder or eyepiece it will blind you instantly. At a total eclipse the filter comes off only during totality itself, and goes back on before the Sun begins to reappear.

Albert listened to the whole of it: the granules, the sunspots, the thermostat, the 170,000 years, the ringing, and the neutrinos in disguise.

“Four million tonnes a second,” he said at last, with the quiet satisfaction of a man whose equation is holding up the sky. “For four and a half billion years. And it will do it for as long again.”

“It’s quite a star,” said John.

“It is a very ordinary star,” said Albert. “That is what is so remarkable about it.”

He was right. The Sun is a middling star, of a common kind, middle-aged. Most of the stars in the galaxy are smaller and fainter than the Sun; some are enormous, hundreds of thousands of times brighter. It has nothing special about it except that it’s ours, and close enough to visit, and so it’s the one star whose inside we can hear, and count, and check. Everything the night sky’s other stars are doing, the Sun does too, and the Sun is how we know.

Cal had been turning that over.

“The big ones,” he said. “The really bright ones. If the Sun burns so slowly that it lasts ten billion years, how long do they last?”

Albert didn’t answer straight away. It was late, and Orion was rising over the eastern ridge, lying on its side: blue-white Rigel, and beside it Betelgeuse, orange as a coal in a dying fire. He looked at them for a while.

“Look at John’s pictures of clusters,” he said at last. “The Pleiades, and M13. Put them side by side.”

Out in the car park, The Van ticked as it cooled, and the dashboard, having seen the Sun, had something to add.
