Adding waves into a lump

Write a wave of wavelength λ\lambdaλ as cos⁡kx\cos kxcoskx, where k=2π/λk = 2\pi/\lambdak=2π/λ is its wavenumber, the number of radians of wave per metre. Two waves of nearly equal wavenumbers k1k_1k1​ and k2k_2k2​ add to

cos⁡k1x+cos⁡k2x=2cos⁡ ⁣(k1−k22 x)cos⁡ ⁣(k1+k22 x):\cos k_1 x + \cos k_2 x = 2 \cos\!\left(\frac{k_1 - k_2}{2}\, x\right) \cos\!\left(\frac{k_1 + k_2}{2}\, x\right):cosk1​x+cosk2​x=2cos(2k1​−k2​​x)cos(2k1​+k2​​x):

a wave of the average wavenumber under a slowly changing envelope. The envelope’s loud parts recur every 2π/∣k1−k2∣2\pi/|k_1 - k_2|2π/∣k1​−k2​∣: beats, for ever. Adding more waves between k1k_1k1​ and k2k_2k2​ pushes the recurrences further apart, and a continuous spread removes them altogether.

The bell-shaped recipe. Take amplitudes falling off as a bell (a Gaussian) round a middle wavenumber k0k_0k0​,

A(k)=e−(k−k0)2/2s2.A(k) = e^{-(k - k_0)^2 / 2s^2}.A(k)=e−(k−k0​)2/2s2.

Their sum is

ψ(x)=∫A(k)cos⁡kx dk  ∝  e−s2x2/2cos⁡k0x,\psi(x) = \int A(k) \cos kx \, dk \;\propto\; e^{-s^2 x^2 / 2} \cos k_0 x,ψ(x)=∫A(k)coskxdk∝e−s2x2/2cosk0​x,

a wave of the middle wavelength under a bell-shaped envelope whose width is 1/s1/s1/s: the wider the recipe (sss), the narrower the lump.

What the figure measures. The recipe falls to half its height at k−k0=±2ln⁡2 sk - k_0 = \pm\sqrt{2\ln 2}\, sk−k0​=±2ln2​s; call that half-width, as a share of k0k_0k0​, the spread ppp. The envelope falls to half its height at x=±2ln⁡2/sx = \pm\sqrt{2\ln 2}/sx=±2ln2​/s; call that full width, in middle wavelengths λ0=2π/k0\lambda_0 = 2\pi/k_0λ0​=2π/k0​, the length NNN. Then

Np=22ln⁡2/s2π/k0⋅2ln⁡2 sk0=4ln⁡22π≈0.441,N p = \frac{2\sqrt{2\ln 2}/s}{2\pi/k_0} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2\ln 2}\, s}{k_0} = \frac{4 \ln 2}{2\pi} \approx 0.441,Np=2π/k0​22ln2​/s​⋅k0​2ln2​s​=2π4ln2​≈0.441,

so the spread in per cent times the length in wavelengths is 44.144.144.1, whatever sss is. At ±10\pm 10±10 per cent the lump is about 4.4 wavelengths long; at ±0.2\pm 0.2±0.2 per cent, about 220; at ±30\pm 30±30 per cent, about 1.5. (For small spreads a share of the wavenumber is the same share of the wavelength, since δλ/λ=δk/k\delta\lambda/\lambda = \delta k/kδλ/λ=δk/k.)

The least it can be. Physicists measure spreads by standard deviations of the squares: ∣ψ∣2∝e−s2x2|\psi|^2 \propto e^{-s^2x^2}∣ψ∣2∝e−s2x2 has σx=1/(2 s)\sigma_x = 1/(\sqrt{2}\,s)σx​=1/(2​s), and ∣A∣2|A|^2∣A∣2 has σk=s/2\sigma_k = s/\sqrt{2}σk​=s/2​, so

σx σk=12.\sigma_x\, \sigma_k = \tfrac{1}{2}.σx​σk​=21​.

For any other recipe the product is larger: σxσk≥12\sigma_x \sigma_k \geq \tfrac{1}{2}σx​σk​≥21​ for every wave whatever, with equality only for the bell. This is a theorem about waves (about Fourier transforms) with no physics in it. The same holds in time: a pulse lasting σt\sigma_tσt​ is made of angular frequencies spread by σω≥1/(2σt)\sigma_\omega \geq 1/(2\sigma_t)σω​≥1/(2σt​), which is why a click has no pitch.

Hear it plays the figure’s lump as sound: a note of 220 Hz under the same envelope, so that its length in the note’s own waves matches the lump’s length in wavelengths. At the narrowest spread it lasts about a second (220 waves); at the widest, about seven thousandths of a second (1.5 waves), which the ear hears as a click.

The uncertainty principle

De Broglie’s rule (the fourth journey) is that a particle’s momentum p=mvp = mvp=mv and its wavelength are linked by Planck’s constant:

p=hλ=ℏk,ℏ=h2π=1.055×10−34 J s.p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \hbar k, \qquad \hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi} = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}.p=λh​=ℏk,ℏ=2πh​=1.055×10−34 J s.

So a spread in wavenumber is a spread in momentum, σp=ℏ σk\sigma_p = \hbar\,\sigma_kσp​=ℏσk​, and the theorem above becomes Kennard’s form (1927) of Heisenberg’s principle:

σx σp≥ℏ2.\sigma_x\, \sigma_p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}.σx​σp​≥2ℏ​.

For a particle of mass mmm, the least spread in speed for a spread σx\sigma_xσx​ in place is σv=ℏ/(2mσx)\sigma_v = \hbar / (2 m \sigma_x)σv​=ℏ/(2mσx​).

An electron in an atom. With σx\sigma_xσx​ the Bohr radius, a0=5.29×10−11a_0 = 5.29 \times 10^{-11}a0​=5.29×10−11 m, and m=9.11×10−31m = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}m=9.11×10−31 kg,

σv=1.055×10−342×9.11×10−31×5.29×10−11≈1.1×106 m/s,\sigma_v = \frac{1.055 \times 10^{-34}}{2 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 5.29 \times 10^{-11}} \approx 1.1 \times 10^6\ \text{m/s},σv​=2×9.11×10−31×5.29×10−111.055×10−34​≈1.1×106 m/s,

about a thousand kilometres a second.

The Van, to a millimetre. With m=2,500m = 2{,}500m=2,500 kg and σx=10−3\sigma_x = 10^{-3}σx​=10−3 m, σv=2.1×10−35\sigma_v = 2.1 \times 10^{-35}σv​=2.1×10−35 m/s. A proton is about 1.68×10−151.68 \times 10^{-15}1.68×10−15 m across, so at that speed The Van would take 8.0×10198.0 \times 10^{19}8.0×1019 seconds, 2.5×10122.5 \times 10^{12}2.5×1012 years, to cover one: about 180 times the age of the universe, 13.8 billion years.

Why nothing is perfectly still

The least energy in a box. Between hard walls LLL apart, the waves that fit are sin⁡(nπx/L)\sin(n\pi x/L)sin(nπx/L), with wavelengths 2L/n2L/n2L/n, momenta nh/2Lnh/2Lnh/2L and energies

En=p22m=n2h28mL2,n=1,2,3,…E_n = \frac{p^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8 m L^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldotsEn​=2mp2​=8mL2n2h2​,n=1,2,3,…

The lowest, E1=h2/8mL2E_1 = h^2/8mL^2E1​=h2/8mL2, is not zero: there is no n=0n = 0n=0, since a wave of infinite wavelength is flat, zero everywhere, and so no particle at all. Halve LLL and E1E_1E1​ goes up four times. For an electron in a box a nanometre wide, E1≈0.38E_1 \approx 0.38E1​≈0.38 eV. More generally, whatever holds a particle in, its average energy of motion is at least

⟨p2⟩2m≥σp22m≥ℏ28mσx2\frac{\langle p^2 \rangle}{2m} \geq \frac{\sigma_p^2}{2m} \geq \frac{\hbar^2}{8 m \sigma_x^2}2m⟨p2⟩​≥2mσp2​​≥8mσx2​ℏ2​

in each direction. It doesn’t depend on temperature, and cooling can’t remove it.

Helium. Helium boils at 4.2 K (Kamerlingh Onnes liquefied it in 1908) and, at ordinary pressure, never freezes. Keesom first froze it in 1926, at about 26 atmospheres at 1.1 K; near absolute zero the freezing pressure is about 25 atmospheres. Neutron scattering measures the atoms’ energy of motion in the liquid near absolute zero: about 14 kB14\,k_B14kB​ per atom (kBk_BkB​ is Boltzmann’s constant; this is the energy of motion an ideal gas has at about 9 K), almost all of it zero-point motion. Pulling an atom out of the liquid costs about 7.2 kB7.2\,k_B7.2kB​. So the atoms’ attraction for one another comes to about 14+7=21 kB14 + 7 = 21\,k_B14+7=21kB​ per atom, and the jiggle undoes about two thirds of it. A rough check: a helium atom (6.6×10−276.6 \times 10^{-27}6.6×10−27 kg) whose place is spread by about 0.057 nm in each direction, about a sixth of the 0.36 nm between neighbours in the liquid, has at least

Bohr’s answer to the box

This is Bohr’s argument as he gave it in 1949, in standard deviations.

Energy and time. A pulse of light lasting σt\sigma_tσt​ is made of angular frequencies spread by at least 1/2σt1/2\sigma_t1/2σt​, and each photon’s energy is E=ℏωE = \hbar\omegaE=ℏω, so

σE σt≥ℏ2.\sigma_E\, \sigma_t \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}.σE​σt​≥2ℏ​.

The weighing. The box, mass MMM, hangs on a spring in gravity ggg. Its height qqq is read from the pointer to within σq\sigma_qσq​. By the uncertainty principle its momentum is then unknown to within σp≥ℏ/2σq\sigma_p \geq \hbar/2\sigma_qσp​≥ℏ/2σq​. The box loses mass Δm=E/c2\Delta m = E/c^2Δm=E/c2 when the light leaves, and to see a change σm\sigma_mσm​ in its mass during a weighing that lasts TTT, the extra impulse that change makes, g σmTg\,\sigma_m Tgσm​T, must be at least the momentum’s uncertainty:

g σm T≥σp≥ℏ2σq⇒σq≥ℏ2g σmT.g\, \sigma_m\, T \geq \sigma_p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2\sigma_q} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma_q \geq \frac{\hbar}{2 g\, \sigma_m T}.gσm​T≥σp​≥2σq​ℏ​⇒σq​≥2gσm​Tℏ​.

The clock. Two clocks a height Δq\Delta qΔq apart run at rates differing by gΔq/c2g\Delta q/c^2gΔq/c2 (Time Runs Faster Upstairs). The clock in the box, its height unknown to within σq\sigma_qσq​ for the time TTT, has its reading unknown to within

σt=g σqc2 T≥gTc2⋅ℏ2g σmT=ℏ2 σmc2=ℏ2 σE.\sigma_t = \frac{g\, \sigma_q}{c^2}\, T \geq \frac{g T}{c^2} \cdot \frac{\hbar}{2 g\, \sigma_m T} = \frac{\hbar}{2\, \sigma_m c^2} = \frac{\hbar}{2\, \sigma_E}.σt​=c2gσq​​T≥c2gT​⋅2gσm​Tℏ​=2σm​c2ℏ​=2σE​ℏ​.

So σEσt≥ℏ/2\sigma_E \sigma_t \geq \hbar/2σE​σt​≥ℏ/2: the box can do no better than the waves allow. ggg and TTT cancel. Weighing for longer allows a sharper height, but the clock drifts for longer, by the same factor.

What’s argued over. Bohr’s account is in “Discussion with Einstein on Epistemological Problems in Atomic Physics” (in Albert Einstein: Philosopher-Scientist, ed. P. A. Schilpp, 1949). Time isn’t an observable in quantum mechanics the way position is, so the relation between energy and time has a different standing from Kennard’s, and the argument mixes a quantum rule for the box with general relativity for its clock. Some analyses defend Bohr’s reasoning (for example V. Hnizdo, 2002); others argue that Einstein’s argument and Bohr’s reply were both flawed (de la Torre, Daleo and García-Mata, 2000), or that the box is better read as an early case of the correlations of the twelfth journey (H. Nikolić, 2012).
