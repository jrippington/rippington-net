The Van's speedometer has been mended at last. Now that it knows exactly how fast The Van is going, The Van has no idea where it is, and it turns out to be right. By the end of the day Albert has brought out his box again, Niels has come back for tea, and one of them has lost an argument to his own theory.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Field Guide to the Very Small, part 7 of 16

Mended

The Van’s speedometer had been mended.

For as long as anyone could remember it had read 17 mph. It had read 17 mph on the coast road and the observatory road, uphill and downhill, in the car park with the engine off, and, on one occasion, far out in empty space, with nothing whatever to measure The Van’s speed against. Nobody remarked on it any more. For nearly as long, the dashboard had been advising them, whenever anything went wrong, to consult their dealer, and nobody had remarked on that either. Then, the evening before, after Niels had finished his tea and gone home, Cal had looked at The Van for a long time and said, “Right.”

The dealer turned out to be a small, silent man in a garage at the bottom of the mountain. He looked at The Van from every side, opened the bonnet, closed it again without comment, and replaced the speedometer.

So the next morning, before the first coaches had come up from the coast, John and Cal drove up the observatory road to try it out. The road climbs away from the plateau in long bends between the old lava flows, with the white domes of the telescopes waiting at the top, and it is not a road for hurrying. John held The Van at a careful, even speed, watching the new needle, until it settled.

SPEED: EXACTLY 17 MPH

“It still says seventeen,” said Cal.

“But this time we’re doing seventeen,” said John. “Exactly. Look at it. It’s never been so sure of anything.”

The satnav beside it was less happy. Its map of the island had been showing a small arrow on the observatory road, where The Van was. As the speedometer settled, the arrow blurred. The blur spread along the road, then across the plateau, then over the whole of Tenerife and out to sea. The satnav thought about this, and then gave up.

POSITION: ANYWHERE

“That’s new,” said Cal.

John tapped the screen, which did nothing, and then the speedometer, which still said exactly seventeen. He slowed down for the next bend. The needle dropped, became an ordinary, slightly wobbly needle again, and the arrow came back on the map, more or less where it should be.

POSITION: OBSERVATORY ROAD, ROUGHLY

“The new speedometer’s upset the satnav,” said John.

“It knows exactly how fast we’re going,” said Cal, “and it has no idea where we are. When it’s less sure of the speed, it’s surer of the place.” He looked out at the domes. “That sounds like something Niels would say.”

“Niels was talking about electrons,” said John. “This is a van.”

They drove back down to the café. Albert was rolling the first doughnuts of the day in their lemon sugar. The grain of sugar was still on its saucer at the end of the counter, under its upturned glass, with its napkin propped against it, DO NOT EAT. Beside it, today, was something else: something about the size of a shoebox, under a tea towel.

They told him about the speedometer and the satnav. He listened with great attention, and when they had finished he wiped his hands on his apron.

“Your van,” he said, “has picked up a bad habit from the electrons. It is a habit that suits an electron better than a van.” He nodded at the thing under the tea towel. “Niels is coming back for tea this afternoon. Yesterday he told you that measuring more cleverly would be harder than you think. I have been thinking about that.”

“What’s under the cloth?” said Cal.

“This afternoon,” said Albert.

A note that goes on for ever

Albert took a clean wine glass from the shelf behind the counter and tapped it with a spoon. It rang: a clear, pure note that hung in the air of the café and slowly faded.

Then he put the glass down and clapped his hands, once.

“Which of those had a pitch?” he said.

“The glass,” said John. “You could sing it. The clap was just a clap.”

“Why?”

It turns out to be the question behind The Van’s satnav, and a good deal more besides.

In Chasing a Beam of Light, John and Cal found that a sound is a wave: the air shuffling back and forth, the pattern travelling. The distance from one crest of the wave to the next is its wavelength, and how many crests pass you each second is its frequency, which your ear hears as the pitch. A high note has short waves, close together; a low note, long ones. The note Albert’s glass made has waves about as long as your forearm.

A perfectly pure note has exactly one wavelength, and that’s all it has: crest after crest after crest, every one the same, for as long as the note lasts. Now ask: where is it? Not in any one place. It’s everywhere the crests are, all the same, and if it’s perfectly pure, it can’t stop anywhere, because stopping is a change in the wave, and a wave that changes is no longer the same crest after crest. A truly pure note would have to go on for ever, in both directions, filling everything. Albert’s glass came close: it rang on, the same, for a few seconds.

The clap is the opposite. It’s in one place, at one moment: a single crack of air, and nothing before or after. And it has no pitch at all. You can’t sing a clap.

So how do you make a wave that’s in one place? Two pure notes show the way.

Two notes, slightly out of tune

Anyone who has tuned a guitar has heard this. Play two strings that are meant to give the same note but are slightly out of tune, and you don’t hear two notes. You hear one note, swelling and fading, loud and soft and loud again: wah, wah, wah. Musicians call it beating, and they tune by listening for it to slow down and stop.

What’s happening is the adding up of waves that John and Cal met at the two slits in the fourth journey. The two notes’ waves are slightly different lengths, so they slowly drift in and out of step. Where they’re in step, crest on crest, they add and the sound is loud. Where they’ve drifted half a wave apart, crest on trough, they cancel and the sound is soft. Then they drift back into step, and it’s loud again. (How far in or out of step two waves are is what the fifth journey called their phase.)

Two notes, then, make lumps of sound: but a whole row of them, one after another, for ever. Add a third note, between the first two, and something changes. Now all three have to be in step at once for a loud lump, which happens less often, so the lumps move further apart, and in between them there’s more cancelling. Add a fourth, a fifth, a hundredth, every wavelength across a whole range, and the lumps move further and further apart until only one is left: the place where every one of the waves is in step. Everywhere else, for every crest there is now some other wave with a trough to cancel it. What’s left is a single lump of waves, in one place, with nothing on either side.

That’s what a clap is: a great many pitches at once, all in step at one place and one moment, cancelling everywhere else. A drum’s tap is the same. A long, pure note is the other end: very nearly one pitch, and very nearly everywhere.

The shorter the lump, the wider the spread

How long is the lump? That depends on how different the waves in it are.

Waves whose lengths are nearly the same drift out of step very slowly: they stay in step for crest after crest, and only far away do they finally cancel. So a narrow range of wavelengths makes a long lump, a long note. Waves whose lengths are very different get out of step within a few crests, so they cancel almost at once on either side, and the lump is short: a click. Follow it through, one step at a time:

  • To be in one place, a wave must be strong there and nothing everywhere else.
  • Nothing everywhere else means cancelling: for every crest, a trough.
  • One wavelength on its own can’t cancel itself anywhere. It’s the same everywhere, which is the pure note.
  • So a lump needs waves of different lengths, and the shorter the lump, the sooner they must cancel, so the more different their lengths must be.

There’s no way round it. A lump made of one wavelength would be the pure note, everywhere. A short lump with a narrow spread of wavelengths is impossible, because nearly equal waves can’t get out of step in so short a distance. Make the lump shorter and the spread must grow; narrow the spread and the lump must grow longer. Halve one, and the other doubles. You can trade one for the other, but you can never make both small.

Wave packet

A lump of waves, strong in one place and nothing elsewhere, made by adding together waves of many different wavelengths, all in step at that place and cancelling everywhere else. The shorter the packet, the wider the spread of wavelengths it has to be made of: halve its length, and the spread must double. A single, perfectly pure wavelength can’t make a packet at all: it is the same everywhere, for ever.

The figure below builds one. At the top are five of the many waves being added, chosen from across the spread, all in step at the middle (the amber line). In the middle is what they add up to: the lump, with a dimension giving its length in wavelengths. At the bottom is the recipe: how much of each wavelength goes in. Slide the spread. Narrow it and the five waves stay in step across the whole sheet, and the lump stretches out into a long note with no particular place. Widen it and they fall out of step at once, and the lump shrinks to a click. Watch the ledger: the spread and the length change, and the two multiplied together never do. Hear it plays the same lump of waves as sound.

A place or a speed

None of that is quantum mechanics. It’s true of sound, of ripples on a pond, of every kind of wave there is. It becomes quantum mechanics with one more step, which John and Cal took in the fourth journey. Matter is a wave too. A single electron goes through both of two slits, as a wave, and arrives at the screen as a single dot. And its wavelength is set by how it’s moving: the faster it goes, the shorter its waves. (Strictly, by its momentum, its mass times its speed, the quantity they met in The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe. For one particular particle that comes to the same thing: its speed sets its wavelength.)

Now put the two together.

  • An electron with exactly one speed has exactly one wavelength. Its wave is a pure note: the same everywhere, for ever. It’s nowhere in particular.
  • An electron in one place is a lump of waves. A lump has to be made of a spread of wavelengths, and a spread of wavelengths is a spread of speeds. So an electron in one place has no single speed.
  • In between, it’s a trade. Squeeze where it can be, and the spread of its speeds grows. Narrow the spread of its speeds, and where it can be spreads out.

“So the satnav was right,” said John slowly. “Exactly seventeen miles an hour, position anywhere.”

“For an electron,” said Albert.

There is one way out that everybody tries. Perhaps the electron really is in one place, moving at one speed, all along, and the wave is only our way of saying that we don’t know which place and which speed? Then the trade would be about our knowledge, not about the electron.

The two slits shut that door. If the wave were only a statement of what we don’t know, then each electron would really go through one slit or the other, and, as the fourth journey found, electrons that go through one slit or the other can’t make stripes. They make stripes. Something about each electron goes through both slits, and that something is its wave, which is why the wave can’t simply be a list of our guesses. And whatever anyone believes lies underneath it (a question this series comes back to, honestly, in the fifteenth journey), no experiment has ever made a particle whose place and speed were both sharper than its waves allow. The limit is a fact about waves, and electrons are waves.

Uncertainty principle

The rule that a particle’s place and its motion can’t both be sharp at once. A particle in one place is a wave packet, made of many wavelengths, and its wavelength is its motion, so it has many possible speeds; a particle with one exact speed has one exact wavelength, and is spread out everywhere. The more sharply one is fixed, the more the other spreads. It isn’t a limit on how carefully we can measure: no particle has ever been found, or made, with both sharper than the rule allows. Werner Heisenberg found it in 1927.

How big is the trade? That’s set by Planck’s constant, the size of nature’s lumps, which John and Cal met in the second journey, and Planck’s constant is very small. For an electron, it matters enormously. Squeeze an electron into the space of an atom, and the spread of its speeds is about a thousand kilometres a second. For anything you can see, it doesn’t matter at all. If The Van’s satnav knew where The Van was to within a millimetre, The Van’s speed would be uncertain by so little that, moving that slowly, The Van would take about two hundred times the age of the universe to cover the width of a single proton.

“So the satnav’s wrong after all,” said Cal. “By a factor of something enormous.”

“By a factor of something enormous,” said Albert.

“Then I’d like to see one that isn’t,” said Cal.

Planck’s constant: increased

The dashboard screen was showing a kettle. Cal hit it, firmly, in the place that usually worked, and the kettle went away. Beside the screen, where they were quite sure there had never been anything before, was a small new switch, with a label in very small letters.

“Quantum mode,” Cal read out. “The dealer must have put it in.”

“Or it was always there,” said John, “and nobody looked.”

He flicked it. The screen dimmed, and The Van said, in its usual way:

PLANCK’S CONSTANT: INCREASED. PLEASE DRIVE CAREFULLY

The simulator had rebuilt the café car park, with its bays and its white lines and its low wall, and The Van in its corner, and had then turned Planck’s constant up. It had turned it up a long way. In quantum mode, The Van at exactly 17 mph has a wavelength of about five metres, a little less than its own length. To do that to the real world, Planck’s constant would have to be more than a hundred billion billion billion billion times bigger than it is. Nothing real is remotely like this. That’s what simulators are for.

John drove the simulated Van out of its corner and round the simulated car park, holding it, as he had on the observatory road, at exactly seventeen miles an hour.

The Van spread out. On the screen, where a moment before there had been one van, there was a long ripple of faint vans, one behind another, five metres apart, crest after crest, reaching from one wall of the car park to the other and on out of the picture. None of them was brighter than the rest. It was a pure note in the shape of a van.

POSITION: ANYWHERE

“Where are we?” said John.

“Yes,” said Cal.

John eased off and let the speed wander a little either side of seventeen, so that it was no longer exact, and the ripple gathered itself in from the edges into a single blurred lump of van, somewhere near the middle of the car park, a few vans long. The more he let the speed wander, the more the lump shrank, until it settled, more or less, into one Van. Then Cal took over, and steered it carefully into a parking bay, between two white lines.

In the bay, The Van couldn’t keep still. It rattled from one white line to the other and back, shuddering, as if it were trying to get out. The speedometer swung wildly from one side of its dial to the other.

SPEED: 0 MPH, GIVE OR TAKE 17

“We’re parked,” said John, “and we’re doing seventeen miles an hour.”

Cal started to laugh. “You said that,” he said. “The very first time. The speedometer said we were doing seventeen while we were parked, and you said perhaps we were.”

“And now we are,” said John. “In either direction.”

It’s the uncertainty principle again, from the other side. Squeezing The Van into a bay two and a half metres wide is pinning down where it is, so the spread of its speeds has to grow. In quantum mode, the bay is exactly the right size for half of one of The Van’s five-metre waves, so the spread comes to seventeen miles an hour either way: a van that is, on average, going nowhere, while never being still.

The real Van, in the real bay, does exactly the same. Being held between two lines makes it jiggle too. But the real jiggle is so small that, to creep the width of a single proton at that speed, The Van would need about seventy thousand times the age of the universe.

Heisenberg’s microscope

“Who found this?” said John, when they were back inside.

“A young man called Heisenberg,” said Albert. “Werner Heisenberg. He was twenty-five. He was working in Copenhagen, at the institute of a physicist who happened, that February, to be away skiing in Norway.” He refilled their cups. “When the skier came back, he found a mistake in it.”

“In the uncertainty principle?”

“In the picture Heisenberg used to explain it.”

Heisenberg’s picture, in 1927, was a microscope. It’s the way most people still think of the uncertainty principle, and it’s right about almost everything except the one thing that matters most.

Suppose you want to see exactly where an electron is. To see something, you bounce light off it. But as John and Cal found in the first journey, you can’t see anything much smaller than the waves of the light you’re using, so to see the electron’s place sharply, you need very short waves: far shorter than visible light, X-rays or gamma rays. And as they found in the third journey, light comes in lumps, and the shorter its waves, the more energy each lump carries. A single lump of very short light, bouncing off an electron, gives it a kick, the way a cue ball kicks the ball it hits. You can’t know exactly which way the lump went off into your microscope, so you can’t know exactly how hard the electron was kicked, or which way. So: the sharper you see its place, the shorter the light; the shorter the light, the harder and less predictable the kick; the harder the kick, the less you know about its speed afterwards.

Every step of that is true. It’s still not the uncertainty principle.

The trouble is what it suggests: that the electron really does have an exact place and an exact speed all the time, and that we spoil one of them by looking clumsily. If that were so, a gentler way of looking ought to do better, and nothing ever has. The physicist who came back from skiing objected, among other things, that the picture left out the part the waves play, and Heisenberg added a note to his paper, owning to the mistake and pointing his readers to the objection. The sixth journey found the same thing at the slits: marking which way an atom went, with no kick worth the name, still wiped out the stripes. And the wave packet shows why. A lump of waves in one place is made of many wavelengths before anyone looks at it. There is no exact place and exact speed waiting underneath for a clumsy microscope to disturb. The limit is in the waves themselves.

Try it: the uncertainty principle at your fingertips

You can see this for yourself, with nothing between you and the light but your own two fingers. Find a small, distant light: a streetlight across the road at night, or a lamp at the far side of a dark room. Hold two fingers up in front of one eye, side by side and almost touching, so that you’re looking at the light through the narrow gap between them. Then squeeze them slowly closer together.

As the gap narrows, the light doesn’t simply get dimmer. It smears out sideways, across the gap, into a streak, often with faint dark lines in it, and the narrower the gap, the wider the streak.

Warning

Never look at the Sun this way, or in any other way. A distant lamp or streetlight is all you need.

Nothing is kicking the light. All you have done is squeeze where it can be, from side to side, into the width of the gap, and that has spread out how it’s moving from side to side. It’s the uncertainty principle, for light, done with no microscope at all: squeeze the place, and the motion spreads. (It’s the same spreading that threw a row of spots across the wall from a single hair in the fourth journey.)

Nothing is perfectly still

The Van in its bay held one more lesson, and it may be the strangest.

Anything held in a small space is a wave held in a small space, and its wave has to fit. The longest wave that fits in a box is twice the box’s width, half a wave across it, as with The Van in its bay. A longer wave would mean moving more slowly, but no longer wave fits, so there’s a slowest the thing can go, and it isn’t zero. It can’t sit still. And the smaller the box, the shorter the longest wave that fits, and the faster it has to jiggle. Squeeze something, and it jiggles harder.

This jiggle isn’t heat. Heat is the random jostling of atoms, and you can take it away by cooling things down. The jiggle from being held in place is the shape of the wave itself, and nothing takes it away. The coldest anything can be is absolute zero, about 273 degrees below the freezing point of water, the temperature at which, by the old rules, every bit of jostling would stop and everything would be perfectly still. By the quantum rules, even there, everything held in place still jiggles.

Helium proves it.

In 1908, in Leiden, Heike Kamerlingh Onnes cooled helium gas until, at about four degrees above absolute zero, it became a liquid, the last gas ever to be liquefied. Then he tried to freeze it. He pumped away its vapour to cool it further, to little more than a degree above absolute zero, and it stayed a thin, clear liquid, and in the years that followed it still wouldn’t freeze. Helium never freezes on its own, however cold it gets. It took his successor, Willem Keesom, until 1926 to make solid helium at all, and he managed it only by squeezing it, at about 25 times the pressure of the air.

The reason is the jiggle. Helium atoms are very light, and they barely attract one another at all. Each is hemmed in by its neighbours, and an atom held in so small a space has to jiggle; for anything as light as helium, the jiggle is enormous. Measured in the liquid near absolute zero, it undoes about two thirds of the pull between the atoms: not quite enough to boil them away, but far too much for them ever to settle into the neat, still rows of a solid. So helium atoms keep sliding past one another, a liquid for ever, unless they’re squeezed hard enough to be pushed into place.

Cal looked at his doughnut, which was sitting very quietly on its plate.

“So this isn’t perfectly still,” he said.

“Pinned to the plate to within a centimetre,” said John, who had been doing sums on a napkin, “it’s jiggling at a speed that would take it a few thousand times the age of the universe to move the width of one atom.”

“Then it’s still.”

“Very nearly,” said Albert, “which is not the same thing.”

Albert’s box

Niels arrived at four.

He came in quietly, as he had the day before, a large, gentle man who seemed to apologise for the space he took up, and he asked, if it wasn’t too much trouble, for a cup of tea. This time Albert was ready. The teapot, rescued the day before from its years as a home for teaspoons, had been washed again and put away at the back of the cupboard, behind the tray of currant dough, and he made a pot with the air of a man performing an operation he had now done once. Niels thanked him warmly and drank it as though it were excellent.

Then Albert took the tea towel off the thing on the counter.

It was a box. John and Cal knew it at once. It was the small white box of the sort Albert used for doughnuts to take away: the box with a lamp at each end that had shown them, in The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe, where E = mc² comes from. It had been altered. The lamps had gone. Its inside was lined with mirrors and, Albert said, full of light. An old alarm clock was fixed inside it, wired to a small shutter in one side. And the whole box hung from the hook of the spring balance Albert used for weighing flour, which hung in its turn from a broom handle clamped across the top of the café’s coat stand. A pointer stuck out from the side of the box, and a ruler was taped to the coat stand beside it.

“Yesterday,” said Albert to Niels, “you told them that you can have the path or the stripes, never both. This morning they found that you can have the place or the speed, never both. I should like to show them a case where you can have both.”

Niels inclined his head, very slightly, and waited.

Energy and time

What Albert’s box was built to beat is the uncertainty principle’s other half, and John and Cal worked it out before he had finished pouring.

“It’s the clap again,” said Cal. “A flash of light that’s very short is a lump of waves in time, not in space. So it has to be made of lots of different frequencies, like the clap is.”

“And each lump of light carries an energy set by its frequency,” said John. “That was the second journey and the third. Bluer, more energy. So a very short flash has to be made of lumps whose energies are spread out. And a flash with one exact energy would have one exact frequency, one exact colour, and it would have to go on for ever, like the pure note.”

So energy and time trade off exactly as place and speed do. The more sharply you know when a flash of light happened, the less sharply you can know its energy, and the reverse. It follows from waves, by the same argument as before.

Albert’s box claims to beat it. The clock, at a moment set as precisely as you like, opens the shutter for an instant, and one flash of light escapes. So you know when the light left, as precisely as the clock can tell you. And you know its energy too, because you can weigh the box before and after. In The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe they found that a box that gives out light gets lighter, by the light’s energy divided by the speed of light squared. So the box’s loss of weight tells you the light’s energy, as precisely as you can weigh it.

“When it left, as precisely as you like,” said Albert. “Its energy, as precisely as you like. Both.” He sat back. “Your uncertainty principle is broken.”

John and Cal looked at the box for a long time. John suggested that the clock might be inaccurate, and Albert pointed out that a better clock could be bought. Cal suggested that the shutter might rattle, and Albert pointed out that a better shutter could be made. Every objection they could think of was about some part of the box being badly made, and the box could always be made better.

Niels said nothing at all. He looked at the box, and drank his tea, and looked at the box again. The light outside began to turn gold.

Something very like this happened in Brussels, in October 1930, at the sixth Solvay conference, a meeting where many of the founders of quantum theory gathered every few years. Einstein described a box full of light, a clock that opened a shutter, and a spring balance to weigh it, and asked how the uncertainty principle could survive it. Niels Bohr, the great defender of the new theory, had no answer. The physicist Léon Rosenfeld, who was there, remembered him that evening going from one colleague to another, deeply unhappy, insisting that it couldn’t be true, that if Einstein were right it would be the end of physics, and unable to say why Einstein was wrong. By the next morning, as the story is usually told, after a night with very little sleep, he had the answer.

In the café it took rather less than a night.

“It is a very beautiful box,” said Niels at last, so softly that John and Cal found themselves leaning across the table. “May I ask, if it is not impolite, how you weigh it?”

“You read the pointer against the scale,” said Albert.

“Then the box must be free to move up and down,” said Niels. He reached out one finger and touched the spring, very gently, so that the box bobbed. “On the spring.”

He said nothing more. He didn’t have to.

The answer is in the spring

John and Cal worked it out a step at a time, with Niels nodding, very slightly, at each one.

Weighing the box means watching where it hangs. When the light escapes, the box is lighter, so the spring pulls it up a little, and to find out how much lighter, you watch where the pointer settles against the ruler.

But the box obeys the uncertainty principle too. It’s a thing with a place and a speed, like The Van in its bay. If you pin down its height very sharply, its up-and-down motion becomes very uncertain: it might be drifting up or down at some speed you don’t know, and a drift you don’t know about can hide the tiny change in weight you’re trying to see. The tiny extra pull of the spring, once the box is lighter, has to show through that unknown drift. It shows through better the longer you watch, and the less sharply you pin down the height, since a blurrier height leaves a smaller drift. So a precise weighing needs a blurry height, or a long wait, or both. Either way, during the weighing, the box spends some time at a height nobody knows exactly, and the more precisely you want to weigh it, the more of that unknown height, for the more time, you have to accept.

And a clock’s rate depends on its height. In Time Runs Faster Upstairs, John and Cal found that a clock higher up runs faster than one lower down: not because anything is wrong with either clock, but because time itself passes faster higher up. The clock in Albert’s box is riding up and down on the spring, at a height nobody knows exactly, all through the weighing. So it gains or loses an amount of time that nobody knows exactly either, and the moment it opened the shutter becomes uncertain.

And it’s uncertain by exactly enough. Weigh the box more precisely, and you must accept more unknown height for more time, so the clock grows less sure, and so does the moment the light left. Bohr worked it through, and the uncertainty in the time came out exactly as large as the uncertainty principle requires, no more and no less. The box measures the light’s energy as precisely as you like, or when it left as precisely as you like, but never both.

“It’s your clocks,” said Cal to Albert slowly. “The ones marked UP and DOWN.”

Albert said nothing.

The drawing exaggerates both enormously, and says so; the ledger gives the real numbers, for a weighing that takes one second and a flash of green light. They are startling. To weigh the box to within a tenth of the energy of the light it let out, its height has to be uncertain by about seventy metres, and in return the clock is unsure by a few of the light’s own waves. And the last line of the ledger never moves: the uncertainty in the energy and the uncertainty in the time, multiplied together, come to exactly the least a wave packet allows.

“It is a very good theory,” said Albert at last, a little heavily.

“You have always said so,” said Niels gently.

“You look,” said Cal to Albert, “like a man who’s just lost an argument to himself.”

“Not for the first time,” said Albert.

What the box was for

There is a postscript to the story, and Albert supplied it himself, a little sulkily, after a second cup of tea.

“In any case,” he said, “I was not really attacking the uncertainty.”

That, oddly, is history too. In July 1931 Paul Ehrenfest, a close friend of both Einstein and Bohr, wrote to Bohr after visiting Einstein in Berlin. Einstein had told him that he had long since stopped doubting the uncertainty relations, and that the box had been meant for something else entirely. Once the light has gone, and is far away, you could still choose what to do with the box: weigh it, and you could predict the light’s energy, or read the clock, and you could predict when it would arrive. You would be choosing, after the light had gone, which of two things you could say about it, without going anywhere near it. If the light, far away, had to be ready to have either, didn’t that suggest the theory was leaving something out? That puzzle, about two things that have flown apart, grew into an argument that comes back in the twelfth journey, and Albert and Niels haven’t finished with it.

And whether Bohr’s answer was sound is still argued over. Some physicists and historians have felt that a rule of quantum theory shouldn’t need gravity to rescue it. Others have argued that Einstein’s box was never quite the threat it seemed, or that neither man had it entirely right. Nobody has found a box that beats the uncertainty principle.

“It is possible,” said Niels, so quietly that they all leaned in again, “that we were both a little wrong.”

“That has never stopped either of us,” said Albert, and for the first time that afternoon he smiled.

Back to Teide

Niels stayed until the sun was low, and they talked about other things. When he left, he shook hands with John and Cal, thanked Albert for the tea with every sign of meaning it, and walked slowly out to the road, where the long evening shadows of the lava lay across the car park.

“He’ll be back,” said Albert, watching him go. “He always comes back.”

Out in the car park, The Van sat in its corner with its engine off. Its new speedometer said 0 mph, exactly, and its satnav, consistent to the last, had once again given up.

POSITION: ANYWHERE

“It’s parked,” said John. “It knows exactly how fast it’s going. Not at all.”

“So it has no idea where it is,” said Cal, and laughed until he had to sit down on the terrace wall.

When he had recovered, he looked back through the café window at the counter, where the grain of sugar sat on its saucer under its upturned glass.

“Squeezing things makes them jiggle,” he said.

“Yes,” said John.

“The electrons in that grain are squeezed into atoms. That’s why they’re jiggling at a thousand kilometres a second.” He frowned. “And on the first day, the rules said they should fall into the nucleus. But the nucleus is tens of thousands of times smaller than the atom. If an electron fell in, it’d be squeezed into a space tens of thousands of times smaller. It would have to jiggle…”

“Enormously,” said John.

“So perhaps it can’t fall in at all.”

Albert, behind them, had begun turning off the lights.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

Through The Van’s windscreen, the dashboard screen had gone back, for reasons of its own, to the message it had shown on the first evening, after the simulator had built an atom by the rules of 1911 and watched it vanish.
