Albert burns a batch of doughnuts, the probe sees straight through the smoke, and by evening John's pictures have arranged themselves into one story.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 8 of 18

The pictures on the table

John had done as he was told. He came in to breakfast with a fat folder under his arm, and before he had even ordered his coffee he had spread its contents across their table by the window, and then across the next table, and then the one after that.

They were his pictures of the clouds of the Milky Way, printed large: every one he had ever taken of a nebula, from his garden in Oxfordshire and from telescopes in Chile and Australia that he had used over the internet. The Horsehead, black against a glowing curtain. The Eagle, with its three dark pillars. The Elephant’s Trunk, curling up through gold light. The blue fans of M78. The Rosette, a ring of glowing gas round a hollow full of stars. The Heart, with its dark pillars and its cavity. The great glowing clouds of Carina and Orion and the Tarantula, and the Pleiades, blue in a haze of dust. The Cygnus Wall, the Iris, the dark tangled clouds of Rho Ophiuchi.

Albert, arriving with the doughnuts, looked for somewhere to put them, found nowhere, and in the end put one on each corner of the Rosette to hold it flat.

“Our galaxy makes about one and a half Suns’ worth of new stars a year,” said John, who had been reading the end of the seventh journey again. “These are where. The nurseries.” He put his finger on the brightest part of the Orion Nebula, where the glow was so fierce his camera had turned it white. “Look at it. That’s where they’re being born. Where it’s brightest.”

“Is it?” said Albert.

John had been asked that question often enough to know what it meant. He looked at his picture again.

It’s what almost anyone would think. The bright, glowing parts of a nebula look alive, full of energy, the obvious place for something to be happening, and the dark parts look like nothing: holes, or gaps, or plain empty sky. It’s exactly backwards. The glow in John’s pictures is the light of stars already born, destroying the cloud they were born from. The births themselves are happening in the dark.

Where the light isn’t

Cal picked up the Horsehead.

The Horsehead is about 1,300 light-years away, a pillar of dust and gas standing up out of a great dark cloud called Orion B, which fills the bottom of John’s picture. Its head is about a light-year and a half across. It isn’t a hole in the glow. It’s something in front of it, so dense that the glowing gas behind can’t shine through it, and on the crown of its head John’s picture catches a faint point of light: a young star still forming, one of two at the tip of the pillar.

The dark clouds are where stars are made, and the first thing to know about them is how cold they are. The gas in them is only about ten degrees above absolute zero, colder than anything on Earth outside a laboratory. That cold matters, because cold gas can be squeezed. At that temperature hydrogen atoms pair up into molecules, two atoms to a molecule, and so astronomers call these molecular clouds. They’re enormous: the biggest hold millions of times the Sun’s mass, across a hundred light-years or more. Yet their gas is so thin that a thimbleful of it holds a few hundred to a few thousand molecules, where a thimbleful of the air in the café holds about twenty-five billion billion.

They’re also very hard to see. Cold hydrogen gives out almost no light at all, of any kind. The way astronomers found them was through a molecule that’s mixed in with the hydrogen in small amounts: carbon monoxide, which hums at a radio wavelength of 2.6 millimetres even when it’s this cold. It was first heard from space in April 1970, from the Orion Nebula and a few other places, by Robert Wilson, Keith Jefferts and Arno Penzias, using a radio telescope in Arizona. Wilson and Penzias were the two men who, six years earlier, had cleaned the pigeons out of their horn aerial and found that the noise was still there: the oldest light, in the fourth journey.

Molecular cloud

A cold, dark cloud of gas in a galaxy, mostly hydrogen with its atoms joined in pairs as molecules, with some helium and about one per cent dust. The gas is only about ten degrees above absolute zero, and the clouds range from a few times the Sun’s mass to millions, across a few light-years to hundreds. They are where every star is born today. Cold hydrogen gives out almost no light, so they’re found by the radio glow of carbon monoxide mixed in with it, and seen as dark shapes against the light behind them.

“So the black bits,” said Cal, “aren’t empty. They’re full.”

“Of the coldest, densest gas in the galaxy,” said Albert. “And of something else.” He rubbed a finger along the edge of the counter and held it up, grey. “Soot.”

Soot

The dark clouds are dark because of their dust.

It’s a strange word for it, because interstellar dust isn’t like the dust on Albert’s counter. Its grains are far smaller, mostly about a tenth of a micrometre across: a thousand of them in a row would just about span the width of a human hair. Some are grains of rock, silicates like fine sand; some are carbon, very like soot. They’re made by stars: in the cool, gentle winds that ageing stars blow off their surfaces towards the end of their lives (the tenth journey comes to those), and in the debris of stars that explode. Every cloud in the galaxy has been enriched with them by the stars that came before. By weight they’re only about one per cent of a cloud, but they block light so well that the dark clouds in John’s pictures are black.

Interstellar dust

Tiny grains of rock and carbon, most of them about a tenth of a micrometre across, mixed through the gas between the stars, about one part in a hundred of it by weight. They’re made in the winds of ageing stars and in the debris of exploding ones. Dust dims the light of everything behind it, blue light more than red, so that distant stars seen through it look fainter and redder; thick enough, it hides them completely. It also helps clouds of gas to cool, which lets them collapse into stars.

Astronomers took a long time to believe in it. In the 1880s and 1890s, photographing the Milky Way with long exposures, E. E. Barnard found dark patches and lanes everywhere among the stars, and for years many people thought they were gaps, places where there simply happened to be no stars. In 1930 Robert Trumpler, at the Lick Observatory in California, measured the distances of a hundred clusters of stars in two ways: from how big they looked, and from how bright. The further away a cluster was, the more the two disagreed: distant clusters were fainter than their size said they should be, and redder. Something between the stars was dimming their light, more and more the further it travelled, and dimming blue light more than red.

You’ve seen dust do exactly that. A low Sun at the end of the day, its light crossing a long way through the air, turns orange and then red, because the air and the haze in it scatter blue light out of the beam more than red. The Sun isn’t redder. Its blue light has been taken out on the way. Starlight crossing a dusty cloud loses its blue in the same way, and if the cloud is thick enough, it loses everything.

The blue that’s taken out has to go somewhere, and John’s picture of the clouds of Rho Ophiuchi, about 450 light-years away, one of the two nearest places where stars are being born, shows where. Round the hot blue-white star at the bottom of his picture, the dust glows blue: it’s scattering the star’s light, and scattering the blue of it most, for the same reason the daytime sky is blue. Round the hottest stars in the frame, the glow is red instead, because their light is fierce enough to strip the hydrogen of its electrons, and stripped hydrogen glows red. And round Antares, a cool red supergiant, the dust glows yellow, because the light it’s scattering is a red star’s. Where the dust is thickest, it blocks everything, and the cloud is black. That black is where more than 300 young stars are hidden, most of them only about 300,000 years old, which no camera that sees visible light will ever show.

Why the dust matters

The dust doesn’t only hide the births. It makes them possible, or at least makes them come out as they do.

In the sixth journey, John and Cal watched the simulator light the very first stars, and the steps were these. A clump of gas pulls itself together by its own gravity. As it falls in, it’s squeezed, and squeezed gas heats up, as the air in a bicycle pump does. Hot gas pushes back. So a cloud can only keep falling in if it can get rid of its heat as fast as the squeezing makes it, and the first clouds, made of nothing but hydrogen and helium, were very bad at that. They stayed warm, a few hundred degrees above absolute zero, and their warmth propped them up, so only very big clumps were heavy enough to fall in anyway. So the first stars were few, and probably huge.

The clouds in John’s pictures are different, because they’re full of dust and of molecules like carbon monoxide, made from the heavier atoms that earlier stars have cast off. As a dusty cloud is squeezed and starts to warm, its dust grains and molecules glow, very faintly, in the far infrared, and that glow escapes the cloud, carrying the heat away. So a dusty cloud stays cold, ten degrees above absolute zero, however hard gravity squeezes it.

And cold gas pushes back only feebly. The push of a gas comes from its molecules battering against whatever holds it in, and the colder the gas, the more slowly they move and the more gently they batter. Put a blown-up balloon in a freezer and it shrinks, because the air inside, chilled, pushes out less hard against the rubber, and the air outside squeezes it smaller. A cloud at ten degrees above absolute zero is a balloon in the coldest freezer there is, with gravity doing the squeezing. The weaker the push, the smaller a clump can be and still collapse under its own weight. In a typical patch of molecular cloud at ten degrees, the smallest clump that can fall in is a few times the Sun’s mass; as the gas gets denser, that smallest size gets smaller still. So as a cold cloud collapses, it can break up: into pieces, and the pieces into smaller pieces, each falling in on its own, until there are dozens or hundreds or thousands of separate clumps, each about the size of a star. The idea that a collapsing cloud breaks up like this was worked out in 1953 by the astronomer Fred Hoyle. (Albert, when the name was mentioned, smiled at something, and didn’t explain.)

The simulator in The Van rebuilt it that morning, and the figure below is its version. A cloud of a thousand Suns’ worth of gas, about thirteen light-years across, falls in on itself. With dust, it stays at ten degrees, and as it gets denser, the smallest clump that can collapse gets smaller, and the cloud breaks up into hundreds of pieces. Each piece becomes a star, and the ledger counts them. Most are smaller than the Sun; a few are much bigger. When the biggest stars light, their fierce light and their winds start to blow away the gas that’s left, and the young cluster ends up sitting in a hollow it has cleared for itself. Switch the dust off, as it was for the first stars, and the cloud stays warm: it breaks into only a few pieces, which make only a few, enormous stars.

That’s why stars are born in crowds. A collapsing cloud doesn’t make one star. It makes a cluster, hundreds or thousands at a time, all of much the same age. Surveys of the nearest clouds have found that most stars, perhaps seven or more in every ten, are born in clusters still buried in the dust they formed from.

Star cluster

A group of stars born together from the same cloud of gas, at much the same time, and held together, at least for a while, by their own gravity. Young clusters are still buried in their clouds; older ones, like the Pleiades, have left their clouds behind. Most break up and drift apart within ten million years or so, but the biggest and densest, the globular clusters, can last for billions. Because all a cluster’s stars have the same age, distance and make-up, clusters are among the best laboratories astronomers have.

And in every crowd, most of the stars are small. In 1955 Edwin Salpeter, nine years before he suggested what quasars are, counted the stars near the Sun and worked out how many of each size must be born. It’s a rule that has held up remarkably well since: the bigger the star, the rarer. About nine in every ten stars are born smaller than the Sun, and for every star of ten Suns or more there are about two hundred like the Sun or smaller.

Pillars

The young stars in a cluster don’t stay hidden for long, and the big ones change everything round them.

A star of twenty or fifty Suns is so hot that most of its light is ultraviolet, and ultraviolet light tears molecules apart and strips the electrons off atoms. Where it falls on the edge of a dark cloud, it heats the gas there and boils it away, and the gas it has stripped glows as its electrons fall back into place: hydrogen’s red, oxygen’s blue-green, the colours the first journey read in the stars and in John’s narrowband pictures. That’s the glow in John’s pictures. It isn’t the light of stars being born. It’s the light of the cloud being eaten.

The Eagle shows how. Its cluster, NGC 6611, is young, most of its massive stars about 1.3 million years old, and its most massive star alone, about 75 to 80 times the Sun’s mass, gives about half the light that makes the nebula glow. Its light has carved what’s left of the dark cloud into pillars. The densest knots of the cloud shade the gas behind them, so while the thinner gas round them boils away, they’re left standing, with their tails behind them: fingers pointing back at the stars eating them. The tallest of the Pillars of Creation is about five light-years from its tip to its base, and together they hold about 200 times the Sun’s mass. At the rate they’re losing it, they’ll last another one to three million years.

And inside them, stars are still forming. The pillars are being eaten from outside while they collapse from within, and the light squeezing their edges may even be what sets some of the births off. The Elephant’s Trunk, in another of John’s pictures, is a pillar about 20 light-years long, its head pointed at the star eating it, and X-ray telescopes have found more than 250 young stars in and round it, younger and younger towards the cloud, as though the star’s light has been setting them off as it pushed in. In the Heart, a V-shaped curtain of dust hangs from the top of the cavity, its tip pointing down at the cluster, and stars are forming in that tip, the youngest nearest the bright rim.

So a nursery isn’t calm. The first big stars born in a cloud start to destroy it, and the destruction squeezes out a last few births before the cloud is gone.

Smoke in the café

“If they’re inside the pillars,” said Cal, “how does anyone know? You said the dust hides everything.”

At that moment the fryer, which Albert had left heating while he was admiring the Eagle, began to smoke. Thick grey smoke rolled up from the oil, curled over the top of the counter and filled the café in a few seconds, and through it nobody could see as far as the door. Albert said something in a language Cal didn’t recognise, turned off the gas and opened every window.

Cal had the probe out of The Van before the smoke had begun to clear. Since Red Hot, White Hot, everyone had known that one of its cameras, the one its handbook called only “warm”, sees in the infrared, and that it would send its pictures to Cal’s phone. He pointed it into the smoke.

On his phone, the smoke had gone. The café was there, plain as day: the tables, the pictures on them, John standing among them, glowing warmly as people do in the infrared, and Albert at the fryer, glowing even more warmly, and looking cross.

This is the trick firefighters use. Their thermal cameras see in the infrared, at wavelengths of about ten micrometres, and they can see through smoke that stops a torch beam dead. The reason is the size of the smoke. A smoke particle is a tiny speck of soot, about a tenth of a micrometre to a micrometre across: about the same size as the waves of visible light, which are about half a micrometre long. A wave meeting something about its own size is knocked about and scattered in every direction, and that’s what makes the smoke opaque. A wave much longer than the speck simply passes round it, hardly noticing it’s there. Water waves do the same. The long, slow swell rolling into a harbour passes a mooring post as though it weren’t there, and closes up behind it; the little ripples from a stone dropped beside the post bounce off it and are scattered in rings.

Interstellar dust is soot of the same kind, its grains about a tenth of a micrometre across: so small that once round one is about the length of a wave of visible light. That’s close enough in size to scatter visible light strongly, and blue light, with the shortest waves, most of all. Infrared waves, several times longer, pass round them. In 1995 the Hubble Space Telescope photographed the Eagle’s pillars in visible light, and they were solid and dark. In 2022 the James Webb Space Telescope photographed them again in infrared light, and the pillars became like smoke that’s thinning: half-transparent, glowing orange, with young red stars shining out of them, stars only a few hundred thousand years old. Webb was built to see in the infrared partly for exactly this.

John’s own pictures have a case of their own. His Cygnus Wall is the brightest edge of the North America Nebula, a front about 40 light-years long where a star’s light is eating into a dense cloud, its face lit from the upper right. For decades nobody could find the star. Astronomers blamed one bright star after another, and by 1958 they suspected that the real one was hidden behind the dark cloud above the wall, which John’s picture shows as nearly black. They were right. It was found in 2005, by its infrared colours: a close pair of very hot stars, of about 70 and 30 times the Sun’s mass, whose light the cloud dims about 7,000 times. Without the dust, it would be one of the brighter stars in that part of the sky. With it, it was invisible for as long as anyone had looked.

The figure below is a pillar like the Eagle’s, with young stars buried in it at different depths and stars far behind it, seen in light of whatever wavelength you choose. Start in visible light, as Hubble saw it, and slide towards the infrared. The ledger says how much of the light from the pillar’s head gets through, and how many of the young stars inside you can see. Watch the dust go clear, and the stars appear, the shallow ones first.

Albert, having got the smoke out, threw the burnt batch away and started again.

“The first batch I have burnt in thirty years,” he said.

Cal looked at the probe’s picture of him on the phone, still glowing. “It’s done the science a lot of good.”

Jets and discs

With infrared and radio telescopes to see into the clouds, astronomers have watched every stage of a star’s birth, though never all of them in one star, since it takes far longer than anyone could watch.

A clump in a collapsing cloud falls in on itself, and in a few hundred thousand years it has a dense, hot core at its centre: a protostar, not yet a star, shining by the heat of its own squeezing. Most of the clump’s gas doesn’t fall straight onto it, though. It swirls round it in a flat, spinning disc, and feeds onto the protostar from the disc’s inner edge. (Why the gas ends up in a flat disc is a story for the twelfth journey, and so is what the disc goes on to make.) Some of it doesn’t make it. Squeezed at the inner edge of the disc, and wound up in the young star’s magnetic field, it’s flung out again in two narrow jets, one from each side, along the star’s spin, at a few hundred kilometres a second.

The jets themselves are often hidden, but where they slam into the gas round them, the shock makes it glow, in little knots of red and pink. George Herbig and Guillermo Haro, in America and Mexico, catalogued the first of them around 1950, and they’re still called Herbig-Haro objects. John’s picture of M78, a pair of blue fans of dust in the same great cloud as the Horsehead, has more than sixty of them in its frame. In one small patch of its dark cloud, a group of at least seven young stars is firing five jets at once.

And the discs can be seen too, in silhouette. In the Orion Nebula, the nearest place where stars of every size are being born, about 1,300 light-years away, the Hubble Space Telescope found, from 1993 on, young stars with dark discs of gas and dust round them, the stuff of planets, some silhouetted against the glowing gas behind, some evaporating in the light of the hot stars nearby. Its survey found 178 of them. In the young cluster round M78, nearly four in every five of the stars still have discs.

The protostar keeps shrinking and heating, and at last its core is hot enough for fusion (the sixth journey’s ignition). For a star like the Sun, the whole journey from a clump of cloud to a steady star takes a few tens of millions of years. For a big star, it’s over in a few hundred thousand.

Clearing out

Once the biggest stars of a cluster are shining, the end of the cloud is in sight.

The Rosette is a cluster that has got there. NGC 2244, about two million years old, sits in the middle of a ring of glowing gas about 110 light-years across, and the middle of the ring is hollow: the cluster’s two hottest stars, each about 50 times the Sun’s mass, have cleared it with their light and with winds blowing at about 2,800 kilometres a second. The ring is the rest of the cloud, being eaten from the inside out, and round its inner edge stand dark trunks and globules, the last dense knots, with their edges lit. The cluster itself is flying apart: Gaia’s measurements show its stars moving outwards, faster the further out they are. The Heart, in another of John’s pictures, is the same story on a bigger scale, its cluster’s stars having blown a cavity about 130 light-years across.

The greatest nurseries are this, many times over. In Carina, about 7,500 light-years away, about 65 of the hottest and most massive kind of star are taking apart the clouds they formed in, among them Eta Carinae, five million times as bright as the Sun. In the Tarantula, in the Large Magellanic Cloud, the cluster at the heart holds the most massive stars known, one of them about 200 times the Sun’s mass.

Teleport to NyxCarina Nebula CoreJohn’s Carina Nebula Core, where about 65 of the most massive stars are tearing apart their cloud

Clearing the cloud is also why only a small part of it ever becomes stars. A cluster turns perhaps a tenth or a third of its clump’s gas into stars before its biggest stars blow the rest away, and the cloud as a whole does far worse, a few per cent. With the gas gone, most young clusters lose the gravity that held them, and fly apart: of all the clusters born buried in clouds, only a few in every hundred survive as clusters for long.

A cluster passing through

The last picture was the Pleiades.

The Pleiades, the Seven Sisters, are about 440 light-years away, the nearest rich cluster of young stars, and you can see them in the winter sky without a telescope. They’re about 120 million years old, long past the stage of hollows and pillars: the cloud they were born from has gone. The blue haze round them in John’s picture looks as though it ought to be what’s left of it. It isn’t. Radio measurements of its gas show it moving at a quite different speed from the stars: the cluster is passing through it by chance, at about eleven kilometres a second. The dust is just reflecting the stars’ blue light as they go by.

The Pleiades won’t last either. They’re already shedding stars into a stream behind them, and over the next few hundred million years to a billion, the pull of the galaxy will scatter most of what’s left. Their stars will drift off round the galaxy on their own. Most stars, the Sun among them, were probably born in clusters, and lost their brothers and sisters long ago.

One story

“So it’s all the same thing,” said Cal slowly, looking along the tables. “Isn’t it? At different ages.”

He started to move the pictures about, and John helped. The Horsehead and Rho Ophiuchi went at one end: dark clouds, with births just beginning inside them, the young stars in Rho Ophiuchi’s cloud only about 300,000 years old, nearly all hidden. Then the pillars, the Eagle’s and the Elephant’s Trunk, being eaten from outside while stars still formed within. Then M78, its young stars firing jets, still in their dust. Then the young clusters with their hollows, the Rosette and the Heart, a couple of million years old. The great nurseries, Carina and Orion and the Tarantula, beside them, where all of it was happening at once on the grandest scale. And at the far end, the Pleiades, 120 million years old, their cloud long gone, drifting through someone else’s dust.

That’s the argument, and it doesn’t leave much room. Nobody can watch a cloud turn into a cluster: it takes millions of years, and photographs have existed for less than two hundred. But every nursery in John’s pictures is the same thing, a dark, cold cloud being eaten from the outside by the light of young stars it has already made, and the differences between them are differences of age. Line the pictures up by age and they’re not a dozen different things. They’re one process, caught at a dozen moments, the way a single afternoon in a park could show you a whole human life, from babies in prams to the old men on the benches, without your having to wait seventy years.

Back to Teide

That evening John gathered his pictures back into their folder, in Cal’s order, the Horsehead first and the Pleiades last. The Rosette had a faint ring of lemon sugar at each corner.

Outside, the sky darkened over the plateau, and the Milky Way came out over the mountain, with the dark lanes of dust running down its middle that John now knew were full: cold clouds of gas and soot, with stars being born inside them, hidden from every eye that only sees visible light. Somewhere in them, at this moment, clumps of gas were falling in on themselves, discs were spinning up, jets were firing, and the light of young stars was eating the clouds away. Low in the east, the Pleiades were rising, a small blue-white knot of stars, on their way out of the dust they’d happened to wander into.

Cal looked from the Pleiades to John’s folder, and then back.

“We’ve watched it a dozen times,” he said. “A star being born. Hundreds of them. But we don’t actually know any of them. Not properly.” He frowned. “Which star do we actually know best?”

“The Sun,” said John.

“Then we’ve hardly looked at it.”

Albert, who was locking the café door, didn’t turn round. “It is eight light-minutes away,” he said, “and you have a van.”

Out in the car park, the dashboard screen lit up, as though it had been listening.
