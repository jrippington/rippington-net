How dust helps a cloud collapse

The push of cold gas. A gas of nnn particles in each cubic metre at temperature TTT pushes with a pressure

p=nkT,p = n k T,p=nkT,

k=1.38×10−23k = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}k=1.38×10−23 J/K being Boltzmann’s constant. At the same density, gas at 10 kelvin pushes 29 times less hard than air at room temperature, 290 kelvin. That’s the balloon in the freezer.

The smallest clump that can collapse. A clump of gas falls in if its gravity beats its pressure. Comparing the two for a ball of gas of density ρ\rhoρ at temperature TTT gives the Jeans mass,

MJ∝T3/2ρ−1/2M_J \propto T^{3/2} \rho^{-1/2}MJ​∝T3/2ρ−1/2. A cloud that stays cold as it gets denser has a smaller and smaller Jeans mass, so it can break into smaller and smaller pieces (Hoyle 1953). Gas twenty times warmer, as the first stars’ gas was, has a Jeans mass about ninety times bigger at the same density.

Why it stays cold. Squeezing heats the gas, and the dust grains and molecules mixed in with it glow the heat away. At 10 kelvin their glow is brightest, by Wien’s law (Red Hot, White Hot), at

λpeak=2.9 mm K10 K≈0.29 mm,\lambda_\text{peak} = \frac{2.9\ \text{mm K}}{10\ \text{K}} \approx 0.29\ \text{mm},λpeak​=10 K2.9 mm K​≈0.29 mm,

in the far infrared, where the cloud is transparent, so the heat escapes. Hydrogen molecules on their own can hardly radiate at all below a few hundred kelvin.

How long it takes. A ball of gas of density ρ\rhoρ, with nothing holding it up, falls in on itself in the free-fall time

How many stars of each size

Salpeter (1955) found that, for stars bigger than the Sun, the number born with masses between mmm and m+dmm + dmm+dm goes as

dNdm∝m−2.35.\frac{dN}{dm} \propto m^{-2.35} .dmdN​∝m−2.35.

Below about half a Sun the rule flattens. Kroupa’s (2001) version, used in the figure, is m−1.3m^{-1.3}m−1.3 from 0.08 to 0.5 Suns and m−2.3m^{-2.3}m−2.3 above, joined so they meet. Integrating it from 0.08 to 120 Suns:

  • the average star is 0.580.580.58 Suns;
  • 90 per cent of stars are born smaller than the Sun, 76 per cent smaller than half a Sun;
  • 0.63 per cent are bigger than 8 Suns, and 0.47 per cent bigger than 10;
  • for every star of 10 Suns or more, about 200 are born with less than 1.5 Suns;
  • yet the stars over 8 Suns hold 22 per cent of the mass.

The figure turns 30 per cent of its thousand Suns of gas into stars, drawn one at a time from this rule (capped at 25 Suns) until they add up to 300 Suns: about 500 stars.

How efficient a cloud is. Clusters still buried in their clouds have turned about 10 to 30 per cent of their gas into stars when it’s blown away; whole giant clouds, a few per cent. Of the clusters born buried, fewer than 4 to 7 in a hundred last to the Pleiades’ age (Lada and Lada 2003).

Why infrared passes through dust

Size against wavelength. How strongly a grain of radius aaa scatters light of wavelength λ\lambdaλ depends on the ratio x=2πa/λx = 2\pi a/\lambdax=2πa/λ, the grain’s circumference in wavelengths. When xxx is about 1 or more, the grain scatters strongly. When xxx is small, the scattering falls off as

σ∝a6λ4,\sigma \propto \frac{a^6}{\lambda^4},σ∝λ4a6​,
