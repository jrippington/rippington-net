John and Cal take The Van into space to find out how fast they are really moving. Unfortunately, the speedometer is broken. More unfortunately, even a working one wouldn't give them the answer they want.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 1 of 14

A question over coffee

There are few places on Earth better suited to thinking about the Universe than the plateau beneath Mount Teide.

The landscape looks as though someone has taken a perfectly respectable planet, removed most of the vegetation, and forgotten to put it back. Great slopes of old lava sweep towards distant ridges. The air is thin and wonderfully clear. In the distance, the mountain rises above everything, apparently indifferent to the human tendency to ask questions about it.

Near the turning for the road that climbs towards the Teide Observatory, John and Cal were sitting outside a café, drinking coffee and considering the last two lemon doughnuts.

They were excellent doughnuts. Everyone agreed on this. The pastry was soft, and the lemon sugar that coated them was sharp and sweet, applied with a generous hand.

Their shape was another matter.

Cal turned his plate through ninety degrees. It didn’t help.

“Don’t,” said John.

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You’re thinking quite loudly.”

Behind the counter, Albert looked up. His hair seemed to have reached an arrangement with gravity that neither party intended to honour.

“Another?” he asked.

“No, thank you,” said Cal rather quickly.

John looked out across the plateau. “It’s astonishing, really. Sitting here, apparently doing nothing, we’re travelling around the Sun at more than a hundred thousand kilometres an hour.”

“Apparently doing nothing?” said Cal. “You’ve eaten three of those.”

“That’s not the point. We’re racing through space, and we can’t feel it.”

Cal took a sip of coffee. “Well, the Earth is moving around the Sun.”

“Exactly.”

“And the Sun is moving around the galaxy.”

“Exactly.”

“And the galaxy is moving relative to other galaxies.”

John nodded, pleased with the direction the conversation was taking.

“So,” said Cal, “how fast are we really moving?”

John opened his mouth. Closed it again.

From the counter, Albert said, “An excellent question. Particularly the word really.”

John gestured towards the car park. “We could find out.”

Cal followed his gaze to the battered vehicle parked beside the café.

“Oh, no,” he said.

The vehicle was called The Van. It looked like a van, in much the same way that an elderly biscuit tin looks like a promising place to store expensive electronics. It had mismatched panels, a rear door that required persuasion, and an engine capable, at least on its better days, of carrying two people across interplanetary distances. Nobody had ever satisfactorily explained how the engine worked. The manufacturer’s handbook had been lost, and the replacement handbook appeared to have been written for a commercial dishwasher.

“The Van has a speedometer,” said John.

“The Van’s speedometer doesn’t work.”

“That’s a minor technical issue.”

“It once told us we were doing seventeen miles an hour while parked.”

“Perhaps,” said John, “we were.”

Albert laughed so suddenly that he had to put down a tray.

The experiment

Ten minutes later, The Van rose from the volcanic plateau with a noise like an enormous cupboard being dragged across a tiled floor.

A red light flashed on the dashboard.

SPEEDOMETER FAULT

“Helpful,” said Cal.

Another light flashed.

OTHER FAULT

“Less helpful,” said John.

They climbed through the atmosphere, leaving Tenerife behind, then the Earth. Eventually they reached an empty region of space, well away from planets and the more obvious distractions of gravity. John switched off the engines.

The sudden silence was rather wonderful.

A loose screw rose gently from the dashboard. Cal caught it before it reached the ventilation grille, which was where important objects generally went to disappear.

“Well?” said John.

“Well what?”

“How fast are we travelling?”

Cal consulted the displays. “Relative to Earth?”

“No. Our actual speed.”

“Relative to the Sun?”

“No. Our absolute speed. Through space itself.”

Cal looked at the speedometer. It was displaying a small drawing of a spanner.

“We may need a better instrument.”

They tried the navigation computer. It willingly supplied their speed relative to Earth. It could give their speed relative to the Sun, or to the centre of the Milky Way, or to almost anything else they cared to name. Every answer was different.

John frowned. “There must be one true answer. Space is space. We’re either moving through it or we’re not.”

Cal thought about this. “How would we tell?”

“By doing an experiment.”

“What experiment?”

John looked around the cabin, where the screw was now floating beside the kettle.

“Something very scientific,” he said.

He picked up a lemon doughnut, which Albert had insisted they take for the journey.

Cal raised a hand. “Not that.”

“Why not?”

“Because if you throw it, I know exactly where it’s going to end up.”

It ended up, after a surprisingly graceful passage across the cabin, in Cal’s hands. It had gone straight across, exactly as it would have done in the café car park.

Cal turned it over thoughtfully. “Well, that’s what it looks like from in here,” he said. “Straight across. I wonder what it looks like from outside.”

John brightened. “The probe!”

The Van carried, in a cupboard behind the kettle, a small observation probe. It was a battered box about the size of a microwave oven, with a camera on every face, a blinking green light, a ruler painted along one side for reasons nobody could remember, and a thruster just strong enough to push it away from The Van and then bring it to a stop. It was the only piece of equipment on board that had never once broken down, possibly because nobody had ever used it.

Cal fed it out through the airlock. It drifted away from The Van’s side, fired its little thruster once, and hung there in the dark a few metres off, blinking, its cameras pointed back at the cabin windows.

For the moment The Van and the probe were simply keeping each other company: neither was moving relative to the other. So John gave The Van’s engine the gentlest nudge he could manage, and switched it off again. The Van began to slide past the probe at about the speed of a brisk walk, and kept on sliding, steadily, in a straight line, with nothing pushing either of them.

“Now,” said John, and threw the doughnut again.

Again it crossed the cabin. Again Cal caught it.

Then they called up the probe’s pictures on the dashboard screen, which for once agreed to show them.

Seen from the probe, the cabin windows slid by, and so did John, Cal and the doughnut. The doughnut had still crossed from John’s hands to Cal’s. But in the second and a half it spent crossing, The Van had carried all three of them about three metres along. In the probe’s pictures, the doughnut’s path wasn’t a straight line across the cabin at all. It was a long diagonal.

“That’s not what happened,” said John. “I threw it straight to you.”

“You did,” said Cal. “In here.”

He pointed at the screen. In the probe’s pictures, the probe was the one keeping still, and The Van was the one moving. Then he pointed out of the window, where the probe, its light blinking, was drifting slowly backwards past them.

“So which of us is moving?” said John. “Us, or the probe?”

Cal didn’t answer straight away. Neither, for once, did the speedometer.

A doughnut in motion

To answer John’s question, it helps to start somewhere much more familiar than a spaceship.

Imagine, for a moment, that you are sitting in a smoothly moving railway carriage. The windows are covered. The train travels in a perfectly straight line at a perfectly steady speed: say 120 kilometres an hour. It doesn’t rattle, brake, accelerate or turn.

You toss a ball to the person opposite you.

Does the ball fall behind because the train is moving forwards? While the ball is in the air, after all, the train is racing ahead at 120 kilometres an hour, and nothing is holding on to the ball. Shouldn’t the carriage move on underneath it, so that it hits the wall behind your companion?

Of course not. If you have ever eaten a meal on a train, or watched a drink being poured on an aeroplane cruising at 900 kilometres an hour, you know that things in a steadily moving vehicle behave exactly as they do at home. The coffee goes straight down into the cup. It doesn’t spray towards the back of the plane.

The reason is that the ball was already moving forwards with the train before you threw it. You, the ball, the air and your companion were all travelling at 120 kilometres an hour together. When you let go, nothing took that forward motion away: the ball kept it. Your throw only added a small sideways motion, across the carriage. So while it crossed, the ball kept pace with the train, and with you and your companion, and it arrived in your companion’s hands.

Why the ball keeps up

That idea, that a moving thing simply keeps moving, is worth stopping on, because everyday experience seems to say the opposite. Kick a football across a field and it slows down and stops. Stop pedalling a bicycle and it coasts to a halt. For about two thousand years, following the Greek philosopher Aristotle, most people believed that this was the nature of motion itself: things move only while something keeps pushing them, and when the pushing stops, so do they.

Galileo, and after him Isaac Newton, realised that this gets things the wrong way round. The football stops because something is pushing against it: the grass rubs on it and the air drags on it. Kick the same ball across smooth ice and it goes much further. Take away the ice and the air altogether, as in empty space, and there’s nothing to slow it at all: it carries on at the same speed, in the same direction, for ever.

Inertia

The tendency of every object to keep doing whatever it is already doing: to stay still if it is still, and to keep moving at the same speed in the same direction if it is moving, until something pushes or pulls on it. The more mass an object has, the harder it is to change its motion: a lorry has far more inertia than a bicycle.

Inertia is what carries the ball forwards with the train. It is also why the screw in The Van floats in mid-air. With the engines off, nothing pushes on the screw or on The Van, so both keep moving exactly as they were, together, and the screw stays where it is, relative to the cabin.

Relative to what?

Now suppose there’s a gap in the blinds, and someone standing in a field beside the track looks in as your carriage goes by. For them, the ball does two things at once. It crosses the carriage, from you to your companion, and all the while it is carried forwards at 120 kilometres an hour with the train. In the half a second or so the throw takes, the train covers more than fifteen metres. So to the person in the field, the ball’s path is a long, shallow diagonal: more than fifteen metres along the track, and a couple of metres across the carriage.

To you, the ball went straight across. To the person in the field, it went along a diagonal. Which of you is right?

Both of you. You are describing the same throw, but measuring it against different things. You measure the ball’s motion against the carriage: its walls, its seats, its floor. The person in the field measures it against the ground. The important phrase is relative to. The ball moves straight across relative to the carriage, and diagonally relative to the ground.

Frame of reference

A point of view from which positions, distances and speeds are measured: imagine rulers and clocks fixed to some object, such as a railway carriage, a field, The Van or a probe. Every speed is a speed relative to some frame of reference. The same motion, described from different frames, can have different speeds and follow different paths, and each description is correct in its own frame.

For John and Cal, The Van was the railway carriage, and the probe was the person in the field. Inside The Van, measured relative to the cabin, the doughnut went straight across. The probe, with its cameras and the ruler painted on its side, measured everything relative to itself: from there, The Van slid past at the speed of a brisk walk, and the doughnut, carried along with The Van while it crossed, followed a diagonal.

There is one important difference from the train. The person in the field is standing on the ground, and the ground feels as though it must be the thing that is really still. The probe is standing on nothing. It is simply another object drifting through space, just as The Van is. Nothing about it marks it out as the one that is “really” still, and nothing about The Van does either. That is exactly why John’s question, which of them is moving, is so hard to answer.

Two views of one throw

The figure below shows the throw twice, side by side: as John and Cal saw it, and as the probe saw it. On the left, everything is measured relative to The Van: John and Cal sit still, the ball goes straight across, and the probe, with its ruler, drifts backwards past them. On the right, everything is measured relative to the probe: the probe keeps still, and The Van slides past, carrying John, Cal and the ball with it. The ball rides along in John’s hand until he throws it, and in Cal’s once he has caught it, and the small dots mark where it was every quarter of a second while it was in the air. Under each picture is what that frame measures.

Try it. Play the throw, or step through it a little at a time. Then change The Van’s speed past the probe. Set it to zero and the two pictures become the same, because then The Van and the probe aren’t moving relative to each other at all. Turn it up, or send The Van the other way, and the probe’s diagonal changes, while the picture from inside The Van doesn’t change at all. And notice what both frames agree on: the ball leaves John’s hand and lands in Cal’s, and the throw takes a second and a half, measured in either frame. (Hold on to that last observation. In the third journey, it will turn out to be less solid than it looks.)

It is very tempting to ask which of the two paths is the real one. Most people’s instinct is to choose the probe’s: the probe is outside, and outside feels like the real world, while the inside of The Van feels like a special case. But the probe is moving too. Relative to the Earth, it and The Van were both racing along at enormous speed; relative to the Sun, faster still. Each of those frames would give the doughnut yet another path, and each would be just as correct. No frame gets to call its description the true one. Every answer is right, as long as it says what it is measured relative to.

“This is good,” said John, retrieving the doughnut. “We can work out how fast it’s really moving by watching it from outside.”

“Outside relative to what?” asked Cal.

John looked at him.

Cal looked back.

The Van’s speedometer beeped and displayed 17 mph.

“It’s mocking us,” said John.

Galileo had a boat

Long before spaceships (or, for that matter, the sort of lemon doughnuts that ought to come with an apology), Galileo imagined a similar experiment.

In 1632 he published a book arguing that the Earth spins, and travels round the Sun. One of the strongest objections to that idea came straight from everyday experience: if the ground beneath us were really rushing along at enormous speed, surely we would notice. A stone dropped from the top of a tower would land some way behind it, as the tower rushed on. Birds would be left behind. The wind would never stop blowing. Galileo’s answer was to invite his readers below decks.

Picture yourself in the cabin of a ship sailing smoothly across calm water. You watch fish swim in a bowl, insects fly about, and drops of water fall into a container. Everything behaves exactly as it does when the ship is moored. The fish don’t crowd at the back of the bowl; the insects don’t struggle to fly towards the bow; the drops land in the container, not behind it. Provided the ship moves steadily and in a straight line, no experiment inside the closed cabin can tell you whether it is moving relative to the shore.

And if a ship can move without anyone below decks being able to tell, so can the Earth.

The shore can tell you about the ship’s speed relative to the shore. But it cannot tell you that the shore represents the universe’s ultimate state of rest: the shore is on the Earth, and the Earth, Galileo was arguing, is moving too.

Galileo was uncovering a principle so important that modern physics still carries it at its heart: the principle of relativity.

The principle of relativity

The laws of physics work in exactly the same way in every inertial frame of reference (see below). So no experiment done entirely inside a closed room that moves steadily in a straight line can tell you how fast the room is moving, or whether it is moving at all. Galileo’s version was about motion and mechanics. Einstein extended it to all of physics, light included, and that is where the rest of this series begins.

Steadily, and in a straight line

That phrase, “steadily and in a straight line”, matters enormously. If Galileo’s ship turns sharply, the water in the fishbowl sloshes to one side. If it suddenly speeds up, plates slide along the table towards the stern. If it runs onto a sandbank, everyone on board finds out at once. If The Van fires its engines, John and Cal feel pressed back into their seats.

Each of these is a change of motion: speeding up, slowing down or turning. Physicists call any change of motion an acceleration. That includes slowing down and going round a corner, not just speeding up, which is how the word is used about cars.

Velocity and acceleration

Velocity is speed in a particular direction: 30 kilometres an hour northwards is a velocity. Acceleration is any change in velocity: speeding up, slowing down or changing direction. A car going round a roundabout at a steady 30 kilometres an hour is accelerating, because its direction keeps changing.

You can feel acceleration, and the reason is inertia. When a car speeds up, your body, by its inertia, tends to carry on at the old speed; the seat has to push you forwards to bring you along, and you feel that push in your back. When the car goes round a corner, your body tends to carry on in a straight line, and the door has to push you round with it. But while a car, a train or a ship travels steadily in a straight line, nothing needs to push you to keep you moving with it. With nothing pushing, there is nothing to feel. That is why you can doze on a smooth train and wake with no idea whether it is moving.

A room that isn’t accelerating, in which anything left alone obeys inertia in this simple way, has a name.

Inertial reference frame

A frame of reference that isn’t accelerating: one that moves steadily in a straight line, without speeding up, slowing down or turning. In it, anything with nothing pushing or pulling on it obeys inertia plainly: it stays still, or keeps moving steadily in a straight line. The cabin of a smoothly coasting spaceship is an inertial frame; a car going round a roundabout is not. Any two inertial frames move steadily relative to each other, and by the principle of relativity, neither is more correct than the other.

The cabin of The Van, coasting with its engines off, was an inertial frame. So was the probe. That is why both descriptions of the throw were equally good, and why no experiment inside either of them could say which one was “really” moving.

(The Earth isn’t quite an inertial frame. It spins, and it travels round the Sun on a curved path, so strictly it is always turning. But over the second or two of a throw it comes so close to one that, as Galileo said, you would never notice. Why we can’t feel even the Earth’s long curve round the Sun turns out to be one of the deepest questions in this series, and it will take until the second half to answer it.)

So you can detect acceleration without looking outside. A steady velocity, by itself, leaves no such telltale mark on a closed laboratory.

The instrument in the cupboard

Back in The Van, John opened a cupboard and found a little spring-mounted instrument labelled ACCELERATION. The needle stood at zero.

Inside it, a small weight hung between two springs. Push the instrument forwards and the weight, by its inertia, lags behind: one spring stretches, the other squashes, and the needle, fixed to the weight, swings over. Carry it at any steady speed you like and the springs have nothing to do, so the needle sits at zero. An instrument like this, an accelerometer, measures changes of velocity, and nothing else.

“Excellent,” said John. “We’re not moving.”

“Not accelerating,” Cal corrected.

John gave the instrument a suspicious look.

They briefly fired a manoeuvring thruster. The Van surged forwards. The loose screw, which nothing was pushing, carried on exactly as it had been going, so The Van moved forwards around it, and relative to the cabin it drifted towards the back wall. The instrument’s needle swung over.

Cal pointed at it. “That we can measure from inside.”

John cut the thruster, and the needle went back to zero, even though The Van was now going faster than before. The screw carried on drifting gently towards the back of the cabin, and Cal caught it, just, before it reached the ventilation grille.

“So we can tell when our motion changes,” John said, “but not how fast we’re travelling through the universe?”

“Not without choosing something to measure it against.”

“That’s deeply inconvenient.”

“Mostly for people selling speedometers.”

The figure below puts you in The Van’s cabin, with the screw, the accelerometer and the thruster, and beside it a record of what the probe outside measures: The Van’s speed and the screw’s, relative to the probe. Let it coast for a while first. The Van is moving past the probe the whole time, but nothing inside shows it: the accelerometer reads zero and the screw hangs still. Then fire the thruster and watch three things at once. The accelerometer’s weight swings back and its reading jumps. The screw drifts towards the back of the cabin. And on the probe’s record, The Van’s speed climbs, while the screw’s stays exactly where it was. Nothing is pushing the screw backwards: The Van is being pushed forwards past it. Switch the thruster off, and the accelerometer goes back to zero, though The Van is now going faster than before.

Then have Cal catch the screw. He holds on to it, and now it goes wherever The Van goes: fire the thruster while he’s holding it and, on the record, the screw’s speed climbs with The Van’s, because his hand has to push it along. Drop it, and it carries on at whatever speed it had at that moment, because nothing is pushing it any more. That is inertia again, and the record shows every catch and drop. (Leave the screw alone after a burn, and it will find the ventilation grille. Cal can fetch it back.)

The speedometer problem

John was not ready to surrender.

“All right,” he said. “But surely there must be somewhere in the universe that counts as properly still?”

“Why?”

“Because otherwise everything is moving and nothing is moving and words have stopped meaning anything.”

Cal was sympathetic. For a moment.

“Stand on the plateau,” he said. “You’re stationary relative to the café. You’re moving relative to the Sun. You’re also moving relative to someone on a spacecraft passing Tenerife. Which of those people gets to decide that their own position is the one that counts?”

John considered several answers and disliked all of them.

It is worth spelling out just how many answers John’s question has, even for someone sitting perfectly still at a café table on Teide. Relative to the café, he isn’t moving at all. Relative to the Earth’s axis, he is being carried round by the Earth’s spin at about 1,470 kilometres an hour. Relative to the Sun, he is travelling with the whole Earth at about 107,000 kilometres an hour. Relative to the centre of our galaxy, the Sun is carrying him at about 800,000 kilometres an hour. Every one of those numbers is correct. None of them is the speed.

A working car speedometer actually counts how fast the wheels turn; on an ordinary road, that gives a useful estimate of the vehicle’s speed relative to the road. Satellite navigation estimates speed relative to the Earth. A spaceship can use the stars, or signals from other craft, to measure its motion relative to a chosen astronomical frame.

All useful numbers. None is an absolute speed through space.

Astronomers can even measure our motion relative to the faint microwave glow left over from the early universe, which fills the whole of space: relative to that glow, the Sun is moving at about 1.3 million kilometres an hour. It is a very useful reference, the nearest thing the universe offers to a common backdrop. But it isn’t a place of absolute rest. Physics works in exactly the same way for something at rest relative to the glow as for something rushing through it, and either could use their own frame with equal right.

So the problem wasn’t merely that The Van’s speedometer was broken.

The problem was that John was asking it a question with no uniquely defined answer.

The mountain moves

They turned The Van around and headed home. The return journey was short, at modest speeds, with nothing more dramatic than an intermittent smell of hot wiring and a warning on the dashboard:

THE KETTLE IS NOT A STRUCTURAL COMPONENT

By the time they descended onto the Teide plateau, the afternoon light had softened. The rock formations cast long shadows. The observatory road wound away among the dark volcanic slopes.

The Van settled into its parking space with an undignified bounce.

Albert was wiping the café counter.

“Well?” he asked. “How fast were you moving?”

John sat down heavily. “It depends.”

Albert smiled. “On what?”

“On what we measured ourselves against. Earth, the Sun, a passing spacecraft: different answers. None of them is the absolute speed.”

“And your speedometer?”

“Still broken,” said Cal.

“Even if it worked,” John added, “it would have to tell us what it was measuring our speed relative to.”

Albert nodded approvingly and brought them two coffees.

Then, inevitably, two lemon doughnuts.

Cal looked at his. John noticed him looking.

“Don’t,” said John.

“I still haven’t said anything.”

For a while they sat quietly, watching the plateau become golden in the late light.

Nothing appeared to move. The mountains were motionless. The café stood exactly where it always had. John could feel the solid chair beneath him, and the reassuring weight of his cup in his hands.

And yet the Earth was turning. It was travelling around the Sun. The Sun was moving through the galaxy. The galaxy itself was on a journey whose speed could only be specified relative to something else.

The astonishing thing was no longer how fast all this was happening.

It was that, in the right circumstances, there was no experiment they could perform inside The Van that would reveal its constant velocity without looking outside.

John watched a shaft of sunlight reach across the table.

“There’s something else,” he said.

Cal sighed. “Of course there is.”

“If I throw a ball from a moving train, someone standing beside the track says the ball has the train’s forward speed as well as the speed I gave it.”

“Yes.”

“So if we switch on The Van’s headlights while we’re travelling very fast…”

Albert stopped polishing the counter.

“Ah,” he said softly. “Now you have a problem.”
