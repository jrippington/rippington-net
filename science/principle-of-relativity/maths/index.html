The maths behind The Day the Mountain Moved

The working behind the article, for anyone who wants to see it. The article itself needs none of it.

Two answers to one speed

Suppose that, relative to the Earth, The Van moves at 30 kilometres per second, and the probe travels in the same direction at 20 kilometres per second.

Using the familiar rule for ordinary, everyday speeds:

vVan relative to probe=30−20=10 km/s.v_{\text{Van relative to probe}} = 30 - 20 = 10\ \text{km/s}.vVan relative to probe​=30−20=10 km/s.

An observer on Earth describes The Van as travelling at 30 km/s. Someone on the probe describes it as travelling at 10 km/s in the same direction.

Nobody has made a mistake. Each answer has a clearly stated frame of reference.

The same everyday rule explains the numbers under the first figure. Relative to the probe, the ball moves along at The Van’s speed, say 2.0 metres a second, and across at the speed of the throw, 1.6 metres a second. Those two motions are at right angles, so they combine like the sides of a right-angled triangle, by Pythagoras:

v=2.02+1.62≈2.6 m/s.v = \sqrt{2.0^2 + 1.6^2} \approx 2.6\ \text{m/s}.v=2.02+1.62​≈2.6 m/s.

Relative to The Van, the along part is zero, and the ball’s speed is just the throw’s 1.6 metres a second.

At speeds close to the speed of light, however, this simple rule of adding and subtracting speeds is not exact. That is where our next adventure begins.
