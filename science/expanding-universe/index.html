Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 14 of 14

The wrong galaxy

The day began, inevitably, with Andromeda.

“I’ve found another problem with our one-week trip,” John said.

Cal looked up from his coffee. “Only another one?”

The one-week trip was the journey to the Andromeda Galaxy they had planned in the fifth journey: a week by The Van’s clocks, travelling so close to the speed of light that the two and a half million light-years shrank to almost nothing, and more than two and a half million years by everyone else’s. They had never actually gone. The simulator had been quite enough.

“If the universe is expanding,” said John, “won’t Andromeda be moving away from us?”

Albert nearly dropped his tray of lemon doughnuts.

“You have chosen,” he said, “possibly the worst nearby example.”

“Why?”

“Andromeda belongs to our own small family of galaxies, held together by their gravity against the expansion. It is coming towards us.”

Cal smiled. “So your easiest destination may have the courtesy to come towards you.”

“That isn’t the point,” John protested.

“No,” said Albert. “The question is still excellent. Choose a much more distant galaxy.”

John accepted the correction. Cal accepted the last doughnut, then examined its shape and declined to look at John.

Albert was right about Andromeda, and it was the first galaxy whose motion anyone ever measured. In 1912 the astronomer Vesto Slipher, at the Lowell Observatory in Arizona, spread Andromeda’s light into its colours and found its pattern of dark lines shifted towards the blue: the Doppler effect of the second journey, from a source coming towards us. It was approaching at about 300 kilometres a second (most of that is the Sun’s own motion round our galaxy). Then Slipher measured more of the faint spiral smudges, not yet known to be galaxies at all, and found something odd: Andromeda was the exception. By 1917, 21 of the 25 he had measured were moving away.

“So which ones are running away?” said John. “And how fast?”

Albert set down the doughnuts and went into the kitchen. He came back with a baking tray holding a flat, pale sheet of dough, into which somebody had pressed, in rough rows, a few dozen currants. Across the middle, for reasons he declined to explain, he had piped a wavy line of lemon icing.

“These are not for the doughnuts,” he said. “These are for the universe.”

He took a cocktail stick with a little paper flag on it and pushed it in beside one of the currants near the middle. Then he took out a ruler, measured from the flagged currant to a few of the others, and then, to Cal’s surprise, from a second currant near the edge as well. He wrote all the distances on a napkin and put the tray in the warm cupboard beside the oven.

“Come back in an hour,” he said.

What is actually expanding?

While the dough rises, consider the picture most people form when they hear that the galaxies are rushing apart and that it all began in a “Big Bang”: an explosion, a bomb going off at some point in empty space, with the galaxies flung outwards like shrapnel into the darkness around it. That picture is natural, because explosions are the only things in everyday life that send everything flying apart at once. But it’s wrong, and you can see why it’s wrong by thinking about what an explosion would look like from the inside.

An explosion has a centre. A fragment near the middle sees the other fragments moving away in every direction, but a fragment near the edge sees almost everything on one side of it, with empty darkness on the other. Fragments in different places see very different views, and from any of them you could work out where the centre was and point to it. In the universe, by contrast, wherever we look, out to the greatest distances, the galaxies are spread roughly evenly in every direction, and, as we’ll see, they are moving away from us in the same way in every direction. And there is good reason to think a galaxy anywhere else would see just the same. There is no direction in which the universe thins out, and nothing that looks like a middle.

The dough

An hour later, the dough had risen with the enthusiasm Albert’s dough always seemed to have, and spread across the tray, and every gap between the currants had doubled. Albert measured again from the flagged currant, and wrote the new distances beside the old ones.

The nearest currant had been 2 centimetres away, and was now 4: it had moved 2 centimetres in the hour. One that had been 5 centimetres away was now 10, having moved 5. One that had been 8 centimetres away was now 16, having moved 8. In the same hour, the farther a currant was, the farther it had gone, and so the faster it had been moving away from the flag. Twice as far meant twice as fast.

“Now move the flag,” said Albert.

Cal moved it to the second currant near the edge, and Albert measured from there, and compared the results with the second set of distances on the napkin. The pattern was the same. From the new currant, too, every other currant had moved away, and the farther ones faster, in proportion to their distance. Every currant could claim, with equal right, to be the one everything was rushing away from.

Each currant sits where it was put, and the dough between them grows. Every distance between currants is multiplied by the same factor, and that single fact produces the whole pattern: the farther apart two currants are, the more dough there is between them to grow, and the faster the distance between them increases.

The universe behaves in the same way. On the largest scales, each galaxy is more or less at rest among the galaxies around it, give or take a few hundred kilometres a second of its own wandering (Andromeda’s approach is wandering of this kind), and the distances between them grow. Every distance between distant galaxies grows by the same factor in the same time, and one number keeps track of that factor.

Scale factor

The universe’s scale: a single number that says how big the distances between distant galaxies are, compared with the same distances today. Today it is 1. When it was 0.5, every such distance was half what it is now; when it is 2, they will be twice as big. The universe’s history of expansion is the story of how its scale has changed with time.

Where the dough analogy fails

Like every analogy, the dough has limits. Albert’s dough has an edge, and a centre, and it rises into the kitchen around it. The universe, as far as anyone can tell, has no edge and no centre, and it isn’t expanding into anything. The question “what is it expanding into?” pictures the universe as a lump inside some larger room. The expansion is a change in the distances within the universe, and it doesn’t need anything outside. (Whether the universe goes on for ever, or is finite but curls back on itself, nobody yet knows. Either way, it has no edge.) For the same reason, the Big Bang happened everywhere: the hot, dense early universe filled all of space, and it has been thinning and cooling everywhere at once ever since.

“So am I expanding?” said Cal.

John looked at the empty doughnut plate. “I might be.”

The real answer is no. Albert had pressed three currants together into one hard little lump near the corner of the tray, and when the dough rose, the lump stayed a lump: the dough grew around it, and the three currants stayed stuck together. Things held together by their own forces don’t expand. Atoms are held together by electric forces, the Earth by its own gravity, the Solar System by the Sun’s. Our galaxy is held together by its gravity, and so is the small group of galaxies to which the Milky Way and Andromeda both belong. The expansion is simply the way the universe’s matter, spread almost evenly on the very largest scales, has been moving apart since the beginning. Wherever gravity has gathered matter into something that holds together, that thing keeps its size, while the gaps between such things go on growing.

The figure below shows a patch of an evenly expanding universe, with galaxies scattered fairly evenly through it. It is always drawn from the point of view of one galaxy, marked “you are here”, and the arrows show how fast every other galaxy is moving away from it. Beside it is what that galaxy measures: each galaxy’s speed away, against its distance. In this simple model the expansion rate stays fixed, at 70 kilometres a second for every megaparsec of distance. (A megaparsec is an astronomer’s unit of distance, about 3.26 million light-years.)

Play the expansion and watch the arrows: the farther a galaxy, the longer its arrow, and in the graph every galaxy falls on one straight line. Then choose another galaxy to live in, with the button or by clicking on one. The view moves, but the pattern doesn’t: the new galaxy sees everything moving away from it, by the same rule, and its graph is the same straight line. There is no galaxy at the centre. Finally, look at home’s small group: two neighbours, drawn as amber rings, inside a faint dashed circle. Held together by gravity, it keeps its size while everything else spreads out, and from home its two neighbours sit on the graph at zero speed. Switch gravity off, and the group spreads out with everything else.

The farther, the faster

What the dough did, the galaxies do. In 1927 the Belgian physicist and priest Georges Lemaître showed that Einstein’s general relativity allowed a universe that expands, and that in such a universe galaxies should be moving away from us at speeds in proportion to their distances; he used Slipher’s speeds and Edwin Hubble’s distances to estimate the rate. In 1929 Hubble, at the Mount Wilson Observatory in California, published distances to 24 galaxies which, set against their speeds (most of them Slipher’s), showed the same straight-line relation. It has been called Hubble’s law ever since, and in 2018 the International Astronomical Union recommended calling it the Hubble-Lemaître law.

Hubble’s law

The farther away a distant galaxy is, the faster it is moving away from us, in proportion to its distance: twice as far, twice as fast. The speed here is the rate at which the galaxy’s distance is growing, and the number that turns a distance into that speed is the Hubble constant, today’s expansion rate: about 70 kilometres a second for every megaparsec (about 3.26 million light-years) of distance. It holds for galaxies far enough away that their own small motions don’t matter. The rate is the same everywhere at any one time, but it changes over the universe’s history, so its general name is the Hubble parameter.

By Hubble’s law, then, a galaxy one megaparsec away, 3.26 million light-years, is moving away at about 70 kilometres a second, one twice as far away at about 140, and one a hundred times as far away at about 7,000.

The number 70 is a round one, chosen to make the arithmetic easy, and it hides a genuine puzzle. Measured from the faint microwave glow left over from the early universe, the one you met in the first journey, the Hubble constant comes out at about 67; measured from nearby exploding stars, at about 73. The two disagree by more than their uncertainties allow, and nobody yet knows why. Astronomers call it the Hubble tension.

Faster than light?

Cal had been doing sums on the back of Albert’s napkin.

“Twice as far, twice as fast,” he said slowly. “So far enough away…” He stopped, and did the sum again.

Follow the rule outwards, as Cal did. At 4,300 megaparsecs, about 14 billion light-years, Hubble’s law gives a speed of about 300,000 kilometres a second, which is the speed of light. Beyond that distance, galaxies are moving away from us faster than light.

That seems to contradict the whole first half of this series. The second journey and the journeys after it established that nothing can travel faster than light. How can a galaxy be receding faster than light?

“Can I still say the galaxy is running away faster than light?” John asked.

“You can,” said Albert, “if you are prepared to explain exactly which recession speed you mean. Tell me how you measure the speed of a car on the observatory road.”

“By how fast it passes things at the side of the road.”

“Exactly. A car’s speed is a speed relative to the road under its wheels, and the cars and the road and you are all in the same place. Now, what is under a distant galaxy’s wheels?”

John thought about it. “Nothing. Just… more universe.”

“Then please,” said Albert, “stop treating a galaxy as though it were a car on the observatory road.”

The speed limit of special relativity is about things that are in the same place: nothing can overtake a beam of light passing right beside it, measured with the rulers and clocks of anybody there. Every galaxy obeys that limit. Each one is moving slowly relative to the galaxies near it, and no galaxy anywhere overtakes a beam of light.

The “speed” in Hubble’s law is the rate at which the distance between two very distant galaxies grows, and that distance is made of all the distances in between. Imagine a long chain of galaxies, each one megaparsec from the next, from us out to a galaxy 5,000 megaparsecs away. Each galaxy is moving away from its neighbour at only 70 kilometres a second, a perfectly ordinary speed for a galaxy. But there are 5,000 links in the chain, and every link is growing. Add up all those growths, and the distance to the far end grows by 350,000 kilometres every second, faster than light, though nothing in the chain is moving fast relative to anything near it.

Underneath this is the lesson of the first journey once more: a speed is always a speed relative to something, measured from some frame. In the eighth journey we found that a falling room can hide gravity only over a small region, and in the tenth we found out why: spacetime is curved, so a frame of rulers and clocks can only be laid out over a small patch of it. Across an expanding universe, there is no single frame that covers both us and a galaxy billions of light-years away, so there is no ruler-and-clock answer to “how fast is that galaxy moving relative to us?” The recession rate is a perfectly good number, how fast the distance grows, but it is a different kind of quantity, and the speed limit was never about it.

To make that distance precise, cosmologists measure it at one moment of cosmic time. Because the universe is so evenly filled, the clocks of all the galaxies riding along with the expansion agree about how long it has been since the beginning; that shared time is cosmic time, and “the distance to a galaxy now” is as though a chain of rulers had been laid end to end between us and it and read at the same instant.

“That’s rather a long label for the speedometer,” Cal said.

The Van’s display, visible through the café window, lit up as though offended, and read 17 mph.

Hubble radius

The distance at which Hubble’s law gives a recession rate equal to the speed of light, so it changes as the expansion rate does. Today it is about 14 billion light-years. Galaxies beyond it are moving away from us faster than light, in the sense of Hubble’s law. It marks no edge, and, surprisingly, light from galaxies beyond it can still reach us, as we’ll find.

Light stretching with the universe

Back at the counter, Albert pointed at the line of icing. It had gone across the dough in neat, tight waves, about a centimetre from crest to crest. Now the waves were long and lazy, two centimetres from crest to crest, because every distance across the dough had doubled. Every crest had been carried away from the next one, just as every currant had.

Light crossing the universe is stretched in the same way. Light is a wave, and its colour is set by its wavelength, the distance from one crest to the next; blue light has shorter waves than red. As light from a distant galaxy crosses the expanding universe, the distances its crests travel through keep growing, and so does the gap between one crest and the next. By the time the light arrives, its waves are longer than when it set out, stretched by the same factor by which all the distances in the universe have grown during its journey. Blue light arrives redder, and red light arrives as infrared.

Cosmological redshift

The stretching of light’s wavelength by the expansion of the universe while the light is on its way. It is measured by a number called the redshift: how much the wavelength has grown, as a fraction of what it was when the light set out. A redshift of 1 means the wavelength has doubled; a redshift of 3, that it has grown to four times its length. And that stretch is exactly the factor by which the universe’s scale has grown during the journey: how much larger distances are now than they were when the light set out. Light from a galaxy with a redshift of 1 set out when all the distances between galaxies were half as big as they are today.

Is it a Doppler shift?

For nearby galaxies, this stretching agrees closely with the Doppler effect of the second journey. That is how Slipher and Hubble could treat their redshifts as Doppler shifts and turn them into speeds. But the two are different things, and the difference grows with distance.

A Doppler shift happens at the source. An ambulance moving away from you sends out each crest of its siren from a little farther away than the last, so the crests are spread out from the moment they leave. After that, the sound wave travels to you unchanged. A cosmological redshift happens along the way. The light leaves its galaxy with its ordinary wavelength, and is stretched a little more with every stretch of its journey, all the way to us. What sets the redshift is how much the universe grew between the light’s setting out and its arrival.

Treat a distant galaxy’s redshift as a Doppler shift and you get the wrong answer. Consider a galaxy with a redshift of 1, whose light set out about 7.7 billion years ago, in a universe that matches the measured one closely (we’ll describe the model in a moment). A Doppler shift that big would need a source moving away at 60 per cent of the speed of light. But the galaxy’s recession rate was about 68 per cent of the speed of light when the light set out, and is about 77 per cent now. Neither matches, because the redshift was never a Doppler shift. In 2004 the astronomers Tamara Davis and Charles Lineweaver showed that if you insist on treating cosmological redshifts as Doppler shifts, the brightness of distant exploding stars comes out wrong, and the idea is ruled out overwhelmingly.

The oldest light of all is that faint microwave glow from the first journey. It set out about 380,000 years after the beginning, when the whole universe was a glowing gas about 3,000 degrees above absolute zero, and its light was the orange glow of something that hot. Since then the universe has grown about 1,100 times, and so has the light’s wavelength: it arrives as microwaves, the glow of something less than three degrees above absolute zero. And the most distant galaxies yet seen are so redshifted that their light, which set out as ultraviolet and visible light, arrives as infrared. That is one reason the James Webb Space Telescope was built to see infrared light; one of the most distant galaxies it has found, MoM-z14, sent out its light about 280 million years after the beginning, when every distance in the universe was about 15 times smaller than it is today.

Can light from beyond the Hubble radius reach us?

John had been thinking about the galaxies beyond the Hubble radius.

“If some galaxies recede faster than light,” he said, “does that mean their light can never reach us?”

“Not necessarily,” said Albert.

Suppose the observatory road were made of elastic, and somebody was stretching it, so that every kilometre of it grew by a little every minute. Cal sets off from far up the road, walking steadily towards the café. If he were close to the café, the stretch of road between him and it would be short and would grow only slowly, and he would easily gain ground. But far up the road there is a great deal of road between Cal and the café, all of it growing, and if it grows faster than he walks, then for all his walking he gets farther from the café. That is a ray of light beyond the Hubble radius: travelling towards us at the speed of light, through space that is growing between it and us faster than that.

Whether Cal ever arrives depends on what the stretching does next. If the stretching slows down, so that each kilometre grows by less and less each minute, then the distance at which the road grows as fast as he walks moves outwards. Sooner or later it overtakes him, and from then on he gains, and eventually he arrives. In a universe whose expansion is slowing down, the Hubble radius grows like that, and light that set out beyond it can still reach us. That is how we can see galaxies that are, today, moving away faster than light: light from any galaxy with a redshift above about 1.5 left a galaxy that is now receding faster than light, and it reached us anyway.

But if the stretching keeps up, or speeds up, there are places on the road from which Cal will never arrive, however long he walks.

Three horizons and a radius

Since 1998, we have known which kind of universe we live in. Two teams of astronomers, measuring the brightness of distant exploding stars, found that the universe’s expansion is speeding up, where everyone had expected it to be slowing down. (In models like the ones in these figures, it switched from slowing down to speeding up about six billion years ago.) Whatever drives that speeding up is called dark energy, and nobody yet knows what it is; in the simplest description it is a constant energy of empty space itself, the “cosmological constant” that Einstein once added to his equations. Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery in 2011.

That changes what we can ever see, and it means that the universe has several horizons, which are easy to confuse.

The first is the past light cone, the cone of light from the fourth journey, turned to face the past. When we look out into the universe we look back in time, because light takes time to reach us: we see Andromeda as it was two and a half million years ago, and the most distant galaxies as they were more than 13 billion years ago. Everything we can see right now lies on our past light cone, the set of events whose light is arriving here, today.

Particle horizon

The present distance of the farthest matter whose light has had time to reach us since the beginning of the universe: the edge of the observable universe. Light has had about 13.8 billion years to travel, but the universe has expanded while it travelled, so the farthest matter whose light could have reached us by now is about 46 billion light-years away. The particle horizon grows with time, as light from farther away has longer to arrive.

Cosmological event horizon

The distance beyond which nothing happening now can ever be seen by us, however long we wait, because the space between will grow too fast for its light ever to arrive. It exists only if the expansion goes on speeding up, as it seems to be doing; in our universe it is about 16 billion light-years away. Like a black hole’s event horizon, it is a boundary of which events can ever send us news, but turned inside out: we are inside it, and it surrounds us at the same distance in every direction.

And then there is the Hubble radius, about 14 billion light-years, which isn’t a horizon at all. We can see galaxies beyond it, and we always will be able to see some of them as they once were. What the event horizon forbids is seeing them as they are now. A galaxy beyond the event horizon today is still visible, as it was billions of years ago, but its light from today will never reach us: we are watching its past, and its present is already out of reach.

Choose a galaxy, by its distance today, and a moment for it to send a flash of light towards us. Then play the flash and see whether, and when, it arrives.

To begin with, the galaxy is 20 billion light-years away today, and its flash is the one we are seeing now: it follows the edge of our past light cone exactly. It set out 2.3 billion years after the beginning, when the galaxy was 5.3 billion light-years away and moving away at about one and a half times the speed of light, and it reached us anyway. Now send the flash later, at 12 billion years. Today the galaxy is beyond the event horizon, and this flash never arrives. Send it at 8 billion years, and it does arrive, at 42.6 billion years. Somewhere between the two is the last moment of that galaxy’s history that we will ever see: in this model, about 9.9 billion years after the beginning, more than three and a half billion years ago. Then switch to the universe of matter only. Its event horizon disappears, and every flash from every galaxy arrives in the end, if you are prepared to wait.

What relativity leaves open

That evening, Albert ran The Van’s simulator one last time, and filled its windows with galaxies.

It was a picture of a patch of sky no bigger than a grain of sand held at arm’s length, and it was crowded. Each point of light was a galaxy, and most were unimaginably distant: red ones, white ones, spirals tilted at every angle, and smudges so faint and so red that the light from them had been travelling for nearly the whole age of the universe. Their light carried the evidence of cosmic expansion, of the universe’s early conditions, and of a history neither John nor Cal could begin to picture.

They had started, many journeys ago, with an apparently simple question over coffee: how fast is the mountain moving? They had discovered that there was no universal rest, and that motion only means anything relative to something. That light refused to add its speed to the lamp’s, whoever measured it. That clocks in motion disagree, that now in one place is not now in another, and that lengths shrink, so that a journey to Andromeda could be short for the traveller and catastrophic for the idea of returning home. That mass is a kind of energy. That a sealed room can’t tell gravity from being pushed along, that clocks run slower lower down, and that falling is the straightest path through curved spacetime, so that standing still on a planet means being pushed off that path by the floor. That gravity bends light, that it can make places time cannot bring you back from, and that spacetime itself rings with distant collisions. And now, that the universe is growing, and that some of it is already out of reach.

Standing before that field of galaxies, John found himself wondering what the universe actually contained. What were those galaxies made of? Why had they formed at all, and why in that pattern? What had happened before the first stars? What was the dark energy? How did anyone know the age of a universe whose space was expanding?

Those aren’t questions about relativity alone. They belong to astrophysics and cosmology, and answering them takes more than relativity: it takes the physics of the very small as well.

Back to Teide, and the beginning of another journey

The sky above Teide was astonishingly clear. From the plateau, John and Cal could see the deep, familiar blackness broken by thousands of stars, and the pale band of the Milky Way rising over the mountain. The café lights were dim. The mountain’s outline cut across the heavens. Somewhere up the observatory road, telescopes were at work.

Somewhere among the stars, if you knew where to look, was the faint smudge of Andromeda, coming towards them at its own unhurried pace. The light from it had set out when our distant ancestors were making some of the first stone tools. Beyond it, in every direction, were galaxies whose light had been travelling since before the Earth existed, and beyond those, galaxies whose present they would never see, already over the horizon, their news still on its way, or never to arrive.

John stood at the terrace wall and looked up, and for the first time the sky looked different to him. Under his feet, the terrace was pushing him up, out of his fall towards the centre of the Earth, and the top of his head, a little farther from that centre than his feet, was ageing a little faster. The light from Andromeda had crossed two and a half million light-years to reach him, bent a little by everything it had passed on the way. The galaxies beyond it were farther away now than when their light set out, and some of them had already slipped out of reach for ever. None of it showed. The night looked as it always had, still and close and simple. Everything they had found had been there all along, hidden behind the most ordinary experience there is: standing on the ground, and looking up.

Albert set down the final two lemon doughnuts.

Cal looked at his. He turned his plate through ninety degrees. It didn’t help.

“Don’t,” said John.

“I still haven’t said anything.”

“Fourteen journeys,” said John, “and you never have.”

They sat for a while without speaking. The air smelled of cooling rock and coffee.

“So,” said John at last, “we’ve spent fourteen adventures discovering that time, space and gravity are not remotely what we thought.”

“That’s a fair summary,” said Cal.

“And all we’ve done,” John continued, “is work out the rules.”

Albert smiled. “Precisely.”

“Where did you learn all this, Albert?” Cal asked.

Albert considered the question. “I once worked in an office,” he said, “where nobody minded if I looked out of the window.”

John looked at the dark sky. “Then what are we looking at?”

For once Albert had no prepared instrument, no folded diagram, no apple and no equation waiting on a napkin.

He simply pulled up a chair beside them, looked towards the stars, and said, “Now that is a very good question.”

Across the car park, through The Van’s windscreen, the speedometer flickered one final time.

17 mph.

Cal saw it first. He looked at the speedometer, and then at John, and raised an eyebrow.

“Relative to what?” said John.

Cal laughed until he had to put his doughnut down.

When he had recovered, he licked the last of the sugar from his finger, and looked at a single grain of it, glittering in the light from the café window.

“We’ve been out to the edge of everything we can ever see,” he said. “What about the other way? What is this actually made of?”

Albert took the grain from him, very carefully, as though it might go off.

“That,” he said, “is a much stranger journey.”
