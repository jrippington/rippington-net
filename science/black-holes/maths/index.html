The size of the event horizon, and its tidal effects

For an ideal black hole, one that doesn’t spin and has no electric charge, the radius of the event horizon, the Schwarzschild radius, is

rs=2GMc2.r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}.rs​=c22GM​.

Here MMM is the mass, GGG is Newton’s gravitational constant and ccc the speed of light.

For the Sun, M=1.99×1030M = 1.99 \times 10^{30}M=1.99×1030 kilograms, so

rs=2×6.67×10−11×1.99×1030(3.00×108)2≈2,950 metres.r_s = \frac{2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.99 \times 10^{30}}{(3.00 \times 10^{8})^2} \approx 2{,}950\ \text{metres}.rs​=(3.00×108)22×6.67×10−11×1.99×1030​≈2,950 metres.

For six and a half billion Suns, multiply by six and a half billion: about 1.9×10131.9 \times 10^{13}1.9×1013 metres, or 19 billion kilometres.

The same sum for the Earth, M=5.97×1024M = 5.97 \times 10^{24}M=5.97×1024 kilograms, gives about 8.9 millimetres. For the black hole at the centre of the Milky Way, about 4.3 million Suns, it gives 4.3×106×2,9504.3 \times 10^{6} \times 2{,}9504.3×106×2,950 metres, about 13 million kilometres.

The tidal effects. Two small things a distance LLL apart, one directly below the other at a distance rrr from the centre, are pulled differently, because Newton’s pull, GM/r2GM/r^2GM/r2, is a little stronger on the lower one. The difference is about

Δa≈2GMr3 L,\Delta a \approx \frac{2GM}{r^3}\,L,Δa≈r32GM​L,

which is the rate at which GM/r2GM/r^2GM/r2 changes with distance, times LLL. For something falling straight into a black hole, general relativity gives exactly the same stretch along the direction of the fall. At the event horizon, r=rs=2GM/c2r = r_s = 2GM/c^2r=rs​=2GM/c2, so

Δa=2GMrs3 L=c64G2M2 L.\Delta a = \frac{2GM}{r_s^3}\,L = \frac{c^6}{4G^2M^2}\,L.Δa=rs3​2GM​L=4G2M2c6​L.

The mass is on the bottom, squared: double the mass, and the stretch at the event horizon falls to a quarter. With g=9.81g = 9.81g=9.81 metres per second per second:

  • ten Suns (rs≈29.5r_s \approx 29.5rs​≈29.5 kilometres), across a person 2 metres tall: about 2×1082 \times 10^{8}2×108 metres per second per second, or twenty million g;
  • the Milky Way’s black hole, 4.3 million Suns, across 2 metres: about 1.1×10−31.1 \times 10^{-3}1.1×10−3 metres per second per second, a ten-thousandth of a g;
  • six and a half billion Suns, across 2 metres: about 4.9×10−104.9 \times 10^{-10}4.9×10−10 metres per second per second, or 5×10−115 \times 10^{-11}5×10−11 g.

The Van doesn’t fall straight in: it swings around the black hole on its way down (see below), and moving sideways adds to the stretch (Marck, 1983). At the event horizon, on The Van’s path, it makes the stretch about 6.5 times larger, so across John, 2 metres tall, it’s about 3×10−103 \times 10^{-10}3×10−10 g: still less than a billionth of a g.

The tidal effects by the time left. Fall straight in from rest far away, and the time left on your own clock until the centre, from a distance rrr, is (exactly, in general relativity, for this fall)

τ=23r32GM.\tau = \frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{2GM}}.τ=32​2GMr3​​.

Squared, that says r3/2GM=9τ2/4r^3/2GM = 9\tau^2/4r3/2GM=9τ2/4, so the stretch across LLL is

Δa=2GMr3 L=4L9τ2.\Delta a = \frac{2GM}{r^3}\,L = \frac{4L}{9\tau^2}.Δa=r32GM​L=9τ24L​.

The mass has gone: measured by the time left, the tidal effects are the same for every black hole. Across 2 metres they reach ten times ggg, 98 metres per second per second, when τ=232/98≈0.095\tau = \tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{2/98} \approx 0.095τ=32​2/98​≈0.095 seconds; something a hundred times stronger lasts until τ\tauτ is ten times smaller. The event horizon, r=rsr = r_sr=rs​, is crossed when τ=23 rs/c\tau = \tfrac{2}{3}\,r_s/cτ=32​rs​/c: about 12 hours before the end for six and a half billion Suns, and about 66 millionths of a second for ten. (The Van, swinging in, crosses about six hours before the end, and its stretch reaches ten times ggg about 0.07 seconds before it.) With engines, the longest anyone can last inside is πGM/c3\pi GM/c^3πGM/c3 (Lewis and Kwan): about 28 hours, and about 0.00015 seconds.

Holding still: the push and the clock

A clock held still at a distance rrr from the centre (outside the event horizon) runs slower than a clock far away by the factor

1−rsr.\sqrt{1 - \frac{r_s}{r}}.1−rrs​​​.

At twenty-six horizon radii, that’s 25/26≈0.981\sqrt{25/26} \approx 0.98125/26​≈0.981: The Van’s clock lost about 2 per cent, counting 0.981×60≈58.80.981 \times 60 \approx 58.80.981×60≈58.8 minutes in each hour of the distant universe’s. At the event horizon, r=rsr = r_sr=rs​, the factor is zero. A signal sent outwards by something held still at rrr arrives far away stretched by the reciprocal of the same factor; for something falling, like The Van, its speed away adds a Doppler stretch on top.

And the push needed to hold still at rrr is

a=GMr2 11−rs/r.a = \frac{GM}{r^2} \, \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - r_s/r}}.a=r2GM​1−rs​/r​1​.

The first part is Newton’s gravity; the second part is general relativity’s addition, which grows without limit at the event horizon. For the simulated black hole at twenty-six horizon radii, it comes to about 3.5 metres per second per second, about a third of a g.

The fall, by The Van’s clock

Falling straight in. Something dropped from rest at a distance RRR from the centre falls, by its own clock, along a path that general relativity gives in exactly the form Newton’s gravity gives for a fall straight down: a cycloid. With an angle η\etaη that runs from 0 at the release to π\piπ at the centre,

r=R2 (1+cos⁡η),τ=R38GM (η+sin⁡η),r = \frac{R}{2}\,(1 + \cos\eta), \qquad \tau = \sqrt{\frac{R^3}{8GM}}\,(\eta + \sin\eta),r=2R​(1+cosη),τ=8GMR3​​(η+sinη),

where τ\tauτ is the time on the falling clock (Misner, Thorne and Wheeler, Gravitation, 1973, §25.5).

In those units, since 8GM=4rsc28GM = 4 r_s c^28GM=4rs​c2, the factor in front is 12R3/2\tfrac{1}{2}R^{3/2}21​R3/2, and for a drop from R=26R = 26R=26 it is 66.2966.2966.29.

The Van’s fall. The Van didn’t drop straight in. As it let go, its engines gave it a push sideways of 7.33 per cent of the speed of light, as measured by someone holding still there. That fixes two numbers for the rest of its fall: its energy and its angular momentum, each per unit of its mass, which in these units are

E=γ1−1R=0.9832,L=γ vR=1.911,E = \gamma\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{R}} = 0.9832, \qquad L = \gamma\,v R = 1.911,E=γ1−R1​​=0.9832,L=γvR=1.911,

with v=0.0733v = 0.0733v=0.0733 and γ=1/1−v2=1.0027\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2} = 1.0027γ=1/1−v2​=1.0027. An orbit at that energy would need LLL about one per cent larger, so The Van falls, but swings around the black hole on the way. Its distance rrr and its angle φ\varphiφ around the black hole then follow

(drdτ)2=E2−(1−1r)(1+L2r2),dφdτ=Lr2,\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = E^2 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{r}\right)\left(1 + \frac{L^2}{r^2}\right), \qquad \frac{d\varphi}{d\tau} = \frac{L}{r^2},(dτdr​)2=E2−(1−r1​)(1+r2L2​),dτdφ​=r2L​,

which hold inside the event horizon as well as out. There’s no neat formula for the answer, so the simulator, and the figures in the article, step through these equations a little at a time. The Van’s clock reads 197 days and 15 hours at the event horizon, and 197 days and about 20½ hours at the end: about 5.9 hours inside. By the event horizon it has gone around the black hole 1.45 times, and by the end 1.84. Compared with falling straight in from the same place, the swing adds about six weeks outside the event horizon, and takes about six hours off the time inside.

The messages home. Label each event on The Van’s path with Eddington and Finkelstein’s time t~\tilde tt~ (the time of the article’s map of the region around the event horizon). Light sent straight outwards from distance rrr, outside the event horizon, at time t~\tilde tt~, reaches someone far away at

u=t~−r−2ln⁡(r−1),u = \tilde t - r - 2\ln(r - 1),u=t~−r−2ln(r−1),

plus a travel time that’s the same for every message. Light sent from r≤1r \le 1r≤1 never arrives. So the gap between two messages as they arrive, divided by the gap between them as sent, is du/dτdu/d\taudu/dτ, and that’s also how much each message’s light is stretched. Holding still at 26 horizon radii it would be 1/1−1/26=1.0201/\sqrt{1 - 1/26} = 1.0201/1−1/26​=1.020; with the sideways push, 1.0231.0231.023. For messages sent on The Van’s day 100 it’s 1.12; on day 190, 1.80; on day 197, 4.25; and on day 197½, 15.7. Green light, 555 nanometres, arrives at 555 times that: about 568, 622, 1,000, 2,360 and 8,700 nanometres, the last four red or infrared. The last message, sent from 1.12 horizon radii, arrives 239.75 days after the first.

Earth’s clock, as The Van sees it. Light falling in from far away keeps Eddington and Finkelstein’s v=t~+rv = \tilde t + rv=t~+r fixed along its path, so the time on a distant clock that The Van sees, when its own clock reads τ\tauτ, is vvv along The Van’s path, counted from its value when The Van let go. After one day by The Van’s clock, it’s 1.022 days. At the event horizon, it’s 195.25 days, while The Van’s own clock reads 197.61; at the end, 195.92 days.

The longest time inside. Something that falls from rest at the event horizon itself (the limit of being dropped from closer and closer to it), R=1R = 1R=1, reaches the centre after τ=12π\tau = \tfrac{1}{2}\piτ=21​π in these units, which is πGM/c3\pi GM/c^3πGM/c3. In 2007 Geraint Lewis and Juliana Kwan showed that nothing inside can last longer than that, however it fires its engines. For six and a half billion Suns, it’s 1.571×17.79≈281.571 \times 17.79 \approx 281.571×17.79≈28 hours. For ten Suns, whose rs/cr_s/crs​/c is about 98 microseconds, it’s about 155 microseconds: a little over a ten-thousandth of a second.
