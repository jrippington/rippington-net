Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 12 of 14

Choosing a black hole

“I want to see a black hole,” John announced.

Cal didn’t even look up. “No.”

“I didn’t say go into one.”

“You weren’t specific.”

“We could orbit it, look at it, send down a probe.”

It was late afternoon at the café, the day after the eclipse, and the plateau outside was turning gold. Albert placed a lemon doughnut between them. Cal discreetly rotated it so that its less troubling aspect faced the window.

“The edge of a black hole,” Albert said, “is not a surface you can negotiate with. I suggest a simulation.”

“Agreed,” Cal said immediately.

John sighed. “Fine. But make it a realistic one.”

Albert pointed through the window at The Van. “That would be a novelty.”

The Van’s simulator had been getting more use lately than in all the years before. It could do something The Van itself could never do: put them anywhere in the universe, while the real Van sat in the car park with its handbrake on. Albert, unusually, came out with them. He settled himself in the back of the cabin with his coffee, between the kettle and the cupboard where the probe lived, and said he wished to see what The Van would make of it.

John typed in what he wanted. A black hole, a big one: six and a half billion times the mass of the Sun, the size of the one at the heart of the galaxy M87, the first black hole ever photographed. Realistic, he added. As realistic as possible.

A line of text appeared on the dashboard.

SIMULATION REALISM: MAXIMUM. RETURN JOURNEY: NOT GUARANTEED.

“It always says that,” said John.

“It has never said that,” said Cal.

What a black hole looks like

The windows filled with stars, and in the middle of them, something that wasn’t there.

The black hole itself gave out no light at all. Where it sat, there was simply a disc of perfect darkness against the stars. Around it, though, was the brightest thing either of them had ever seen in the simulator: a broad, flattened whirl of gas, heated to millions of degrees as it spiralled inwards, glowing so fiercely that the picture looked almost impossibly bright against the black. Astronomers call it an accretion disc: matter that has strayed too near, on its way in.

Accretion disc

A flat, whirling disc of gas around a black hole (or around a young star, or anything very compact), creeping slowly inwards. Gas almost never falls straight in. Nearly anything arriving is moving a little sideways, so it goes into orbit instead of falling, and orbits that cross collide until the gas settles into one flat disc, all turning the same way. Friction in the gas lets it spiral slowly inwards and heats it, hottest and brightest near the inner edge.

A black hole is what’s left when a great deal of mass is squeezed into a small enough space. The gravity close to it is so strong that, inside a certain distance, nothing can ever get out again: not the gas, not a spacecraft, not even light. That distance marks a boundary around the black hole, called its event horizon.

Black hole

A region of space from which nothing, not even light, can ever escape. It forms when enough mass is packed into a small enough space: for instance, when the core of a very massive star collapses at the end of its life. From the outside, any black hole is completely described by just three things: its mass, how fast it spins, and its electric charge (which in practice is close to zero). Everything else about whatever fell in is hidden behind its event horizon.

Event horizon

The boundary around a black hole: the point of no return. Anything outside it can still, with enough effort, get away; anything inside it, and any signal sent from inside it, even light, can never get back out. It isn’t a surface made of anything. Nothing marks it, and someone falling through it would notice nothing at the moment they crossed. It is a boundary in the pattern of which events can affect which: events inside can never affect anything outside.

The simulated black hole’s event horizon had a radius of about 19 billion kilometres. That’s more than four times the distance from the Sun to Neptune. Put this black hole where the Sun is, and the whole Solar System, every planet, would be well inside it.

The black disc they could see, though, was bigger than that: about two and a half times as wide as the event horizon. That’s because light passing near the black hole is bent, exactly as starlight passing the Sun is bent, but enormously more. Near the black hole there’s a distance, one and a half times the event horizon’s radius, at which light can even go around in a circle: the photon sphere. Light from behind it is bent around its edges towards them, and light aimed too close to it is swallowed. The dark disc they saw was the black hole’s shadow: the patch of sky from which no light could reach them, because tracing any path back from that patch of sky leads into the black hole. It was the lens of the eleventh journey, pushed to its limit.

Photon sphere

The distance from a black hole at which light can travel in a circle around it: for a black hole that doesn’t spin, one and a half times the event horizon’s radius. It isn’t a surface made of anything, any more than the event horizon is. Light there is like a ball balanced on the crest of a hill: the circle is possible, but the slightest nudge sends it spiralling inwards to the black hole, or outwards and away. Light that passes just outside it can loop around the black hole once, or several times, before it escapes, and that light draws the thin, bright ring at the edge of a black hole’s shadow, its photon ring.

Cal enlarged the picture, and they worked out why the disc looked so strange. Seen almost edge-on, a flat disc of gas ought to look like a thin band across the black hole. This one had grown an arch over the top of the dark centre, and a thinner one beneath it. The arches were the back of the disc, the part hidden behind the black hole. Its light, setting off towards them, had been bent around the black hole, over the top and under the bottom, just as starlight passing the Sun was bent in the eclipse of the eleventh journey, but enormously more. From one spot, they could see the near side of the disc crossing in front of the shadow, and its far side wrapped around it.

Right at the edge of the shadow, sharper and thinner than anything else in the picture, ran a fine ring of light. It had passed just outside the photon sphere, about 29 billion kilometres from the centre, and gone around the black hole, partly or more than once, before escaping towards them: light from all around the sky, the disc included, squeezed into one bright thread.

The two ends of the band across the front didn’t match, either. On one side the gas was coming towards them, and on the other it was going away, near the inner edge at half the speed of light. Light from gas coming towards you arrives squeezed, bluer and brighter; light from gas going away arrives stretched, redder and dimmer. It’s the Doppler effect of the second journey, and at these speeds it made one side of the disc blaze blue-white while the other glowed a deep amber.

“Even the shape of the image is telling us about the curved paths,” he said.

“It’s the lens again,” said John, delighted. “A very demanding lens.”

The real thing has been photographed. In April 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope, a network of radio telescopes spread across the Earth and working as one, published the first picture of a black hole: the one at the centre of the galaxy M87, about 53 million light-years away, a ring of glowing gas around a dark centre, just as the simulator showed. In May 2022 the same team published a picture of the black hole at the centre of our own galaxy, the Milky Way. The dark centres in both pictures are the size general relativity predicts for their shadows. Their rings are the glowing gas, seen through the black hole’s lens; the photon ring itself is far too thin for the telescopes to pick out yet.

Holding still

John brought the simulated Van to a stop twenty-six times the event horizon’s radius from the centre: about 500 billion kilometres out, which for a black hole this size counts as keeping a respectful distance.

“We’re hovering,” he said. “Just sitting here.”

They felt it as the engines took hold: a small jolt, then a steady, gentle pressure into their seats that didn’t go away. To sit still at that distance, The Van’s engines had to push away from the black hole, without stopping, at about a third of a g: a third of the push of gravity on Teide. For as long as they wanted to stay, John and Cal would each feel about a third of their own weight, pressing them into their seats.

This is something the eighth journey and the tenth prepared us for. Something falling freely feels nothing; what’s felt is whatever stops you falling, like the floor of the café pushing up on the soles of your feet, which, as the tenth journey found, really is accelerating up to meet you. Near a black hole, staying put means being pushed, and the closer you try to stay, the harder you must be pushed. At twenty-six horizon radii it takes a third of a g. At ten, two and a half g; at five, about eleven g; at three, more than thirty; at twice the event horizon’s radius, more than eighty. At the event horizon itself, no push, however large, would be enough: no engine could hold anything still there.

And because The Van was sitting deep in the black hole’s gravity, its clock ran slow. As the ninth journey found on the mountain, a clock lower down runs slower than one higher up. Here, the effect was no longer measured in nanoseconds. The Van’s clock was running about two per cent slower than a clock far away from the black hole would: for every hour that passed in the distant universe, The Van counted a little under 59 minutes.

HOLDING POSITION. THIS IS HARDER THAN IT LOOKS.

“Everything is,” said Cal.

Letting go

“We could send the probe down,” said John.

He looked over at the cupboard behind the kettle. The probe was the battered observation box from the first journey, about the size of a microwave oven, with a camera on every face, a blinking green light and a ruler painted down one side. It had watched The Van slide past it, had fallen beside an apple in Albert’s café, and had floated outside while clocks were compared. In all that time it had never once broken down. The simulator had a copy of it, of course, accurate down to the scratches.

Cal was quiet for a moment. “It’s only a simulation,” he said.

“It is.”

“Then we’ll go ourselves.”

John stared at him. “You said no.”

“I said no to the real one.”

So they would let go, and fall. John set it up carefully. The Van’s clock, like every clock, would simply measure its own time, wherever it went. And every twelve hours by that clock, The Van would send a message home: a short pulse of green light, saying what its clock read and how far it had fallen.

“Home is fifty-three million light-years away,” said Cal. “The first one won’t get there for fifty-three million years.”

“There’s no hurry,” said John.

Albert leaned forward from the back. “Not straight down,” he said. “Give it a little push sideways, and it will take the long way around.”

“How little?” said Cal.

“About seven per cent of the speed of light.”

That’s little only by the standards of a black hole. To stay in orbit twenty-six horizon radii out, The Van would have needed about twice that speed, so the push couldn’t keep it up. But it was enough to make it miss, for a while: instead of dropping straight in, The Van would fall in a long curve, and swing around the black hole on its way down.

The dashboard counted down from three. There was a brief shove sideways as the engines gave The Van its push, and then the engines stopped, and the steady pressure in their seats was gone, and with it, all their weight. Albert, who had seen this coming, had put a lid on his coffee.

ENGINES OFF. FALLING FREELY.

For a while, nothing much seemed to happen. Then Cal pointed at the dashboard. It had two clocks on it now, both started at zero as The Van let go: The Van’s own, and Earth’s, as The Van saw it in the light arriving from home. After a day, Earth’s was already half an hour ahead. Theirs was the slow one. The Van was still almost as deep in the black hole’s gravity as it had been while it held still, and its clock was still running slow.

Albert set the simulator to run fast, so that days went by in seconds.

For months, The Van fell slowly, then less slowly, as anything falls, and the black disc ahead of it grew. Its messages went out every twelve hours, regular as a heartbeat. Day 50. Day 100. The dashboard showed them leaving, and below, the same messages reaching Earth, a little further apart than they had left. And though they left green, as the days went by they were reaching Earth yellow, then orange, and then red.

“Our light’s going red,” said John. “Is something wrong with the lamp?”

“Nothing,” said Cal quietly. “It’s leaving green.”

By day 180 on its own clock, The Van had fallen four fifths of the way to the event horizon, and it was moving fast now, at about four tenths of the speed of light. Its messages were reaching Earth infrared, where no eye could follow them, and further apart each time. Then the stars began to move. In six months, The Van had swung barely a third of the way around the black hole; in the next fortnight, it went all the way around and more, and the whole sky wheeled past the windscreen.

The messages went on leaving as regularly as ever. Day 196. Day 196 and a half. Day 197. Day 197 and a half. On the dashboard, that last one reached Earth three and a half days after the one before, very faint, and stretched almost beyond recognition.

Two and a half hours later, by The Van’s clock, nothing happened. There was no jolt, and nothing to feel, and nothing on the windscreen changed. The dashboard displayed:

EVENT HORIZON CROSSED. COMMUNICATIONS UNAVAILABLE.

Nobody laughed.

Albert paused the simulator. “About six hours left, by our clock,” he said. “I suggest we work out where we are before we use them.”

Two accounts of one fall

What did The Van actually go through? And what would anyone far away have seen? These are two quite different questions, and much of the confusion about black holes comes from mixing them up.

The Van’s account

Ride along with The Van, and the story is long, and almost dull until the end.

By its own clock, The Van fell for 197 days and about 15 hours, and then it crossed the event horizon. Nothing happened. There was no wall there to hit, and nothing to see or feel. The Van’s clock went on measuring The Van’s own time, one second per second, as every clock always does. Looking back, John and Cal could still see the stars they had come from.

That’s the equivalence principle again. A small freely falling box can’t tell, from anything inside it, that it is falling; and the event horizon isn’t a thing, so there is nothing at the event horizon for a falling box to bump into. Inside a small enough box, over a short enough time, the neighbourhood of the event horizon is just like empty space.

There is one thing a falling box can feel: the tidal effects of the eighth journey, the slight difference in gravity from one end of the box to the other, which stretches things along the direction of the fall and squeezes them from the sides. Near a small black hole, those differences are ferocious. At the event horizon of a black hole the mass of ten Suns, the pull on your feet would be about twenty million g stronger than the pull on your head, and you would have been pulled apart long before you got there: astronomers’ grimly cheerful word for it is “spaghettification”. But the bigger the black hole, the gentler the tidal effects at its event horizon. For a black hole the size of the simulated one, the difference in pull between John’s head and his feet, at the event horizon, is less than a billionth of a g. Nothing could possibly notice it.

So The Van crossed the event horizon intact, and fell on. About six hours later, by its own clock, it reached the centre, and there its story, as far as the theory can tell it, ended. (What “reaching the centre” means is a question we’ll come back to.) Near the very end, the tidal effects grew without limit, and nothing at all could have survived them.

From beginning to end, by its own clock, the fall took a finite time: just under 198 days.

Earth’s account

Earth, in this account, stands for anywhere far away from the black hole. Each of The Van’s messages takes about 53 million years to get there, but each takes the same time as the one before, so it’s the gaps between them that tell the story. Count Earth’s clock from the moment Earth sees The Van let go.

From Earth, the same fall looks quite different, and the reasons are things we have met before.

First, The Van was moving away, faster and faster. Light from something moving away from you arrives stretched out: its waves, and its pulses, arrive further apart than they were sent. That’s the Doppler effect of the second journey, the same effect that lowers the pitch of a passing ambulance’s siren.

Second, The Van was sinking deeper into the black hole’s gravity, and light climbing out of a gravitational field arrives stretched out too: the gravitational redshift of the ninth journey. On Teide, the stretch over the height of the mountain was about two parts in ten million million. Even while The Van held still, its messages would have reached Earth stretched by two parts in a hundred. Close to a black hole’s event horizon, the stretch is enormous, and right at the event horizon it becomes infinite.

Both effects stretch the messages, and both grow without limit as The Van nears the event horizon. So each message, though sent exactly twelve hours after the one before by The Van’s clock, reached Earth more than twelve hours after the one before, and the gap kept growing. At first the messages arrived about twelve and a quarter hours apart; those sent around The Van’s hundredth day, about thirteen and a half; those sent around its hundred and ninetieth, nearly a day apart. The last three from outside the event horizon, sent on The Van’s days 196 and a half, 197, and 197 and a half, arrived nearly two days apart, and then three and a half. The messages’ green light, its waves stretched, reached Earth yellow from about The Van’s thirtieth day, orange from its sixtieth, red from its hundredth, and from its hundred and eightieth, infrared. The light still arriving after the last message would have stretched on, into microwaves and radio waves, if there had been anything left to see.

There’s a third effect. Light sent outwards from closer and closer to the event horizon takes longer and longer to climb out. Light sent outwards from just outside the event horizon struggles out over days, or weeks, or years, the closer to the event horizon the longer. Light sent outwards from exactly at the event horizon stays there, held at the event horizon for ever. And light sent from inside the event horizon, even straight outwards, never gets out.

Put those together, and you have what Earth saw. The Van’s last message before the event horizon, sent on its day 197 and a half, about two and a half hours before it crossed, arrived about 240 days after Earth had seen The Van let go. The next was due at the start of The Van’s day 198, but The Van’s clock never got there: it ran out a few hours earlier, inside the event horizon. And even a message sent from inside would never have arrived, however long Earth waited.

The Van’s image, meanwhile, for anyone on Earth with a telescope good enough to see it, faded. The light still arriving at Earth, weeks later, had set out from closer and closer to the event horizon, so the image seemed to creep nearer and nearer to the event horizon without ever quite reaching it. But each day it was dimmer, and redder, until it was far too faint and far too stretched for any instrument to detect.

Does The Van freeze at the event horizon?

You may well have read that someone falling into a black hole would seem, to a distant observer, to freeze at the event horizon and hang there for ever. That’s what Earth saw, described carelessly. The image does creep towards the event horizon without ever crossing it, but it doesn’t hang there for anyone to see: it reddens and dims and, for a black hole like the simulated one, vanishes from sight within days. And nothing has happened to the falling thing itself. The Van crossed long ago, by its own clock, and John and Cal noticed nothing.

The “freezing” story also comes from the way the black hole’s spacetime was first written down. Karl Schwarzschild found the solution to Einstein’s equations for the gravity around a single, still mass within weeks of Einstein publishing them, in the winter of 1915, while serving in the German army on the Russian front; he died a few months later. His way of labelling times uses the clocks of observers far away, and in those labels The Van reaches the event horizon at a time of infinity, which for decades many physicists took to mean that nothing ever really gets there. The infinity is a fault in the labels, like the way the lines of longitude on a globe all crowd together at the North Pole, though nothing special happens to anyone standing there. Relabel the times sensibly, as we’ll do in a moment, and The Van sails through.

Why can’t light get out?

Once The Van is inside the event horizon, not even its light can climb back out to Earth. The most natural explanation of that, and the oldest, is wrong in an instructive way.

Dark stars

Throw a ball upwards and it slows, stops and falls back; throw it faster and it goes higher first. Throw it fast enough, about 11 kilometres a second from the surface of the Earth, and it slows for ever but never quite stops, and escapes. That speed is the Earth’s escape velocity, and a more massive or more compact world has a higher one.

In 1783, the English clergyman and natural philosopher John Michell wondered what would happen if a star were so massive and compact that its escape velocity was greater than the speed of light. Treating light as Newton did, as a stream of particles, he concluded that its light could never escape, and the star would be invisible: a “dark star”. He worked out that a star with the Sun’s density would need to be about 500 times the Sun’s diameter.

It is a useful first thought, and it even gives the right size for a black hole, by a happy coincidence. But it’s wrong in a way that matters. A ball thrown up from Michell’s dark star still rises before it falls back. Light sent upwards from its surface would climb some way before turning round, so anyone hovering a little way above the star could see it. Inside a real black hole’s event horizon, light sent outwards never gains any height at all: from the instant it sets off, it is closer to the centre than where it started.

A map of the whole region

To see why, we need a map. In the fourth journey, we drew spacetime diagrams, with distance across the page and time up it, so that every event, every happening at one place at one moment, is a point, and every object’s history is a line. At each event, the light cone shows the futures available: light leaves along its edges, and everything slower than light, which is everything else, must go somewhere inside it. So your light cone holds every future you could possibly have from that moment on: everywhere you could ever get to, and when. Fire your engines however you like, steer wherever you like, and your path still stays inside it, because to reach any event outside it you would have to outrun light. Whatever lies outside your cone, you can never reach, and nothing you do can ever affect it. In empty space, every light cone is the same: light can go left or right, at the speed of light, and the cones all point straight up.

Now draw the same kind of map around the black hole: distance from the centre across the page, time up it. There’s a choice to make about how to label the time, and Schwarzschild’s choice, as we saw, goes wrong at the event horizon. A better one was stumbled on by Arthur Eddington in 1924, and put to use by David Finkelstein in 1958 to show what the event horizon really is. On it, light falling straight in always runs at 45 degrees, as in empty space, and nothing goes wrong at the event horizon.

But light going straight out behaves very differently in different places. Far from the black hole, outgoing light runs up and out, at nearly 45 degrees, and the light cones look much as they do in empty space. Closer in, outgoing light climbs more and more steeply: it’s getting out, but slowly. At the event horizon, outgoing light goes straight up the page: it stays at the event horizon for ever. Inside the event horizon, the outgoing edge of the light cone leans over towards the centre. Both edges of the cone now point inwards. Light sent outwards and light sent inwards both end up closer to the centre, and so does everything else, because everything else has to stay inside the cone.

That’s what makes a black hole black: inside the event horizon, every direction into the future leads inwards. The centre is in your future, as surely as tomorrow is, and no steering can take you around it.

What The Van measures inside

It is very easy to look at that map and imagine that, inside the event horizon, John and Cal would find light behaving strangely: tilting over in the cabin, or leaving a torch more slowly one way than the other. It wouldn’t. Inside The Van’s cabin, as inside any small freely falling room, light leaves a torch at the speed of light in every direction, outward as well as inward, exactly as it does in empty space. John and Cal measure nothing odd at all. The tilted cones belong to the map: they show how the whole region around the black hole fits together, which is more than any small laboratory can measure.

One picture that helps reconcile the two is what the physicists Andrew Hamilton and Jason Lisle have called the river model. Imagine space flowing inwards like a river towards a waterfall, reaching the speed of light at the event horizon and going faster still inside, and everything in it, light included, swimming through the water in the ordinary way: far out, light swimming upstream gains ground; at the event horizon it only holds its place; inside, it is swept down, however it swims. It’s only a picture, since space isn’t a substance and nothing really flows, but it shows how light can behave quite normally in the cabin while the whole region carries it in.

Crossing the event horizon

Imagine you’re in The Van yourself, falling in beside John and Cal, and keep an eye on your light cone: the whole of your possible future.

Outside the event horizon, part of your cone still reaches outwards. However close you have come, and however hard it would be, somewhere among all your possible futures is a way back: fire the engines hard enough, for long enough, and you climb back up, and home. As you fall closer, that part of the cone narrows. At the event horizon, its outer edge stands straight up, and the last way out is gone. Inside, every future you have left, whatever you do, ends at the centre.

And you wouldn’t know when it happened. Nothing marks the event horizon: there’s no wall, no jolt, and nothing you could feel changes. Your clock ticks on as before. The stars, and the sky you came from, look just as they did a moment earlier. A pen let go in the cabin floats beside you, as it did outside. With a map and a clock, you could work out afterwards when you must have crossed. But nothing you could see, or feel, or measure around you would mark the moment when every future you had turned towards the centre.

So crossing the event horizon is a trade, made without your noticing. You give up every future with a way out in it, and every one you have left points the same way in space: inwards, which from then on is the direction in which time itself runs for you. You can no more head back out than you can walk back into yesterday.

Where spacetime ends

What happens at the centre is stranger than being crushed. As The Van nears it, the tidal effects grow without limit, and then, in general relativity, spacetime itself comes to an end. The Van doesn’t arrive somewhere and stay there, squeezed very small. Its history simply stops, after a finite time on its own clock, and the theory has no next moment to give it: beyond that edge there is no space and no time for anything to happen in. Everything inside the event horizon meets the same edge, sooner or later. For all of it, the centre is where time runs out.

That is what the theory says. Whether nature really does it is another question, and not one anybody can settle by looking: the centre is inside the event horizon, so nothing from it can ever reach the outside, and nothing that reaches it can ever report back. At the centre the theory’s own numbers become infinite, and most physicists read that as general relativity reaching the limit of what it can describe, rather than as a true picture of what happens there. Something else probably takes over, which needs a theory joining gravity to quantum physics, and nobody yet knows what it is.

Singularity

Where, in general relativity, spacetime comes to an end: the tidal stretching grows without limit as it’s approached, and the history of anything that reaches it simply stops, with no next moment and nowhere further to go. In the simplest black hole it lies at the centre, or more properly, it is a moment, in the future of everything inside the event horizon. Most physicists take it as a sign that the theory has reached its limits, and doubt that anything in nature is really infinite: a full description of what happens there probably needs a theory joining gravity to quantum physics, which no one yet has.

No way back

Could The Van’s engines save it, once it was inside? No. Engines can change which path The Van takes through spacetime, but every path open to it lies inside its light cones, and inside the event horizon every one of those leads to the centre. Firing the engines outwards doesn’t turn The Van round. In 2007 the astronomers Geraint Lewis and Juliana Kwan worked out how long anyone could possibly last between the event horizon and the centre, however they fired their engines. The longest possible time is limited by the black hole’s size: for one of six and a half billion Suns, it comes to about 28 hours. For a black hole the mass of ten Suns, it’s a little over a ten-thousandth of a second.

The difference is size. A bigger black hole is a bigger place, and the fall from its event horizon to its centre is a journey across it: even at nearly the speed of light, crossing 19 billion kilometres takes most of a day, while crossing the 30 kilometres of a black hole of ten Suns takes about a ten-thousandth of a second.

What tidal effects do

Size also decides what tidal effects do to you on the way. Tidal effects, as the eighth journey found, come from the difference in gravity from one end of something to the other, stretching it along the direction of its fall and squeezing it from the sides; and, as the tenth found, they are what curved spacetime feels like. Near a black hole they grow steeply as you fall, and at the centre, without limit.

What matters is when they become too strong to bear, and if you measure that by the time left on your own clock, the answer is the same for every black hole. The stretch across a person reaches ten times their weight, enough to pull anyone apart, about a tenth of a second before the end. Building something stronger hardly helps: make it a hundred times stronger, and it lasts until a hundredth of a second before the end, instead of a tenth.

What changes with size is where the event horizon comes in that countdown. A black hole of ten Suns is crossed in the last ten-thousandth of a second, long after tidal effects have done their work: anything falling in is pulled apart thousands of kilometres before it gets there. The simulator’s black hole is crossed hours before the end: about six, on The Van’s swinging path in, and about twelve for something falling straight in from far away, as in the figure below. Either way, tidal effects there are far too small to feel. The Van, or you, would cross it whole, and fall on unharmed for hours, until that last tenth of a second.

How big is a black hole?

For an ideal black hole, one that doesn’t spin and has no electric charge, the size of the event horizon depends on just one thing, its mass, by a short formula Schwarzschild found. Apart from the mass, it needs only two of nature’s fixed numbers: the speed of light, and Newton’s gravitational constant, the number that sets how strong gravity is.

Schwarzschild radius

The radius of the event horizon of an ideal black hole that doesn’t spin and has no electric charge: about 3 kilometres for every Sun’s worth of mass. It is directly proportional to the mass: a black hole twice as massive has an event horizon twice as wide. Any mass squeezed inside its own Schwarzschild radius becomes a black hole.

Put in the mass of the Sun, and the radius comes out at about 3 kilometres. That doesn’t mean the Sun is a black hole, or ever will be: it’s far too light to collapse into one. But to make a black hole of the Sun’s mass, you would have to squeeze the whole Sun, 1.4 million kilometres across, into a ball 6 kilometres across. For the Earth, the Schwarzschild radius is about 9 millimetres: the Earth squeezed to the size of a large marble.

Because the radius is proportional to the mass, big black holes are big. The one at the centre of the Milky Way, about 4.3 million times the mass of the Sun, has an event horizon about 13 million kilometres in radius, less than a quarter of the radius of Mercury’s orbit. The one in M87, six and a half billion Suns, is 19 billion kilometres in radius, as the simulator showed.

That’s also why the tidal effects at a big black hole’s event horizon are so gentle. The tidal stretching depends on how quickly gravity changes from one place to the next, and at a larger horizon that change is spread over a much greater distance: double a black hole’s mass, and the stretching at its event horizon falls to a quarter. At the event horizon of the one at the centre of our galaxy, the difference in pull between a person’s head and feet would be about a ten-thousandth of a g. Danger certainly remains, deeper in. But there’s no universal rule that a falling astronaut must be destroyed at the event horizon itself.

What we know and don’t know

Nobody has been to a black hole. How can we be confident they exist?

Because they have been caught, again and again, by what they do to their surroundings. Cygnus X-1, a powerful source of X-rays found in 1964, turned out by the early 1970s to be a bright, hot star orbiting an unseen companion far too massive to be anything but a black hole: about 21 Suns, by today’s measurements. At the centre of our own galaxy, astronomers have followed stars for more than thirty years as they orbit something invisible, one of them every 16 years, and the orbits show about 4.3 million times the mass of the Sun packed into a space far too small for anything but a black hole; Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez, who led the two teams, shared half of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics. There are the Event Horizon Telescope’s pictures of M87’s black hole and our own galaxy’s. And since 2015, there has been an entirely new kind of evidence, which is the subject of the next journey.

The other half of that 2020 Nobel Prize went to Roger Penrose, for showing, in 1965, that once enough mass has collapsed far enough, general relativity says a singularity must follow, however lopsided the collapse: somewhere inside, paths through spacetime must come to an end. That is also where our confidence runs out. Classical general relativity predicts the singularity, and most physicists take that as a sign that the theory is incomplete there. What really happens at the centre of a black hole is one of the great open problems of physics, and no school-level argument, or any other argument yet made, can say for certain.

Nor does a black hole become a portal to somewhere else simply because we don’t know everything about its interior. The event horizon, and the trapping inside it, rest on general relativity, which has passed every test so far, including tests made close to black holes themselves, and has no tunnel in it.

What you’d see falling in

Albert didn’t let the simulator run on at once. He took it back to the moment they had let go.

“Last time,” he said, “you watched the clocks.”

So this time, they watched the windows.

For months, by The Van’s clock, the view hardly seemed to change. The black disc sat ahead of them, small, with its fine bright ring around it and the glowing gas across it, as it had while they held still. It took them a while to see that it was growing. When they let go, it covered about a five-hundredth of the sky. By ten horizon radii out, it covered a hundredth; by five, a thirtieth; by three, a twelfth. All the while, its rim stayed sharp, because the rim isn’t the edge of anything. It’s light from all over the sky, the stars behind The Van included, bent around the black hole and crowded into one thin ring at the edge of the dark.

Three times before the event horizon, The Van passed through the plane of the disc, but never through the gas: once beyond its outer edge, and twice inside its inner edge, where the gas no longer circles but plunges in. Each time, the disc closed to a bright thread across the dark, and opened out again on the other side.

Then, a little over six months in, the stars began to move. The Van had come down to three times the event horizon’s radius, and the small sideways push it had been given at the start now mattered. No orbit can last this close to a black hole, and The Van skimmed around it, lower on every turn, while the whole sky wheeled past the windscreen: the disc, the stars, the long bright band of the Milky Way, sweeping from one side to the other. Lower still, it passed the photon sphere, where light itself can travel in circles. Some of the starlight reaching them had already been once around the black hole, and some twice, and so every star showed up more than once, each copy squeezed closer to the edge of the dark than the one before.

And the sky was changing shape. By now The Van was moving at more than four fifths of the speed of light, and the light of the sky was crowding forwards, towards the way The Van was going, and changing colour as it came: ahead, bluer and brighter; behind, redder and dimmer.

You’ve met the first of those effects, if you’ve ever run through rain. Stand still in rain falling straight down, and it lands on top of you. Start running, and the rain seems to slant towards you, coming at your face from ahead; the faster you run, the more of it comes from in front. Light does the same. Move fast enough through the light arriving from all around, and it all seems to come from ahead of you: the whole sky’s light crowds forwards, and the view behind empties out. Astronomers call it aberration. The second effect is the Doppler effect of the second journey: light coming at you from ahead arrives squeezed, bluer and brighter, and light catching you up from behind arrives stretched, redder and dimmer.

Aberration of light

The shift in the direction light seems to come from, for someone moving: like rain, which seems to slant towards you from ahead when you run through it. The faster you go, the more the light from all around crowds towards the direction you’re moving. The Earth’s own movement around the Sun shifts every star a tiny amount back and forth over a year, first measured by the English astronomer James Bradley in the 1720s. Near the speed of light, the shift is enormous.

Then The Van crossed the event horizon, and there was nothing there to see. The shadow covered about a third of the sky, as it had a moment before. John and Cal felt nothing, as they had felt nothing all the way down.

You might expect the dark to close behind them like a door. It never does. The light of the universe outside was still falling in after them, through the event horizon, as it always has and always will, and because they were falling ahead of it, it went on catching them up. Right to the end, the shadow never covered more than about half their sky.

There’s a famous story that someone falling into a black hole would look back and see the whole future of the universe flash past: stars burning out, galaxies colliding, everything there will ever be, in a moment. It’s a wonderful idea, and it isn’t mad: someone holding still just outside the event horizon, with impossibly strong engines, would see the universe outside speed up enormously, as the clocks of the ninth journey would lead you to expect. But falling in is the opposite of holding still, and for The Van the story is wrong. On The Van’s dashboard, Earth’s clock, as John and Cal saw it in the light falling in after them, never raced ahead. It fell behind their own. When The Van crossed the event horizon, Earth’s clock read a little over 195 days, and theirs nearly 198; at the very end, Earth’s had reached about 196. The light from home was chasing them in, and they were falling away from it so fast that it reached them stretched and slowed, as their own messages had been on their way home. The universe will go on for ever, but they would see almost none of its future: only about six and a half months more of it than they had seen when they let go.

And none of it was theirs any more. They could see all of it, but they could never go back to it, or send anything to it, or change anything in it. It lay in no future they had. It was the light of a universe that was no longer theirs.

In The Van’s last hour, the view changed faster than in all the months before. The sideways push from the start of the fall, long forgotten, came back. A skater spinning with arms outstretched spins faster as they pull their arms in. In the same way, as The Van was drawn in towards the centre, it was carried sideways, against everything else falling in, faster and faster, closer and closer to the speed of light. And the faster it went, the further the light of the whole sky crowded towards the way it was going, and the bluer it became.

In its last minutes, the dashboard displayed:

SPACE HAS TURNED INTO TIME. IT NOW FLOWS INWARDS, AND SO DO YOU.

The centre was no longer a place ahead of them. It was a moment in their future, as surely as tomorrow is, and it was coming towards them at one second per second.

At the same time, the tidal effects were stretching everything along the fall and squeezing it from the sides, and that included the paths of the light. Whichever way the light was travelling, it arrived more and more from the sides, until the universe was a ring of light around them, and the ring was narrowing. About a tenth of a second before the end, the tidal effects passed anything a body could bear. The simulator carried on regardless, as if The Van were a point.

The light of the whole universe crowded in ahead of them, a deeper and deeper blue, into a band, and the band narrowed to a line. Then the light flooded the windscreen, white, and faded, and the simulator showed nothing at all, because the theory has nothing to show. Space and time simply cease to exist. There is no later. There is nowhere else.

Back to Teide

Albert shut down the simulator.

The windows cleared, and there was the car park, and the café, and the plateau, nearly dark now, and the night outside was mercifully ordinary. Cal found himself relieved, out of all proportion, by the café’s little light in the growing darkness.

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Cal leaned across and looked at The Van’s dashboard clock. It said it was a quarter past seven on a Thursday, which it wasn’t, as usual. But it was still counting, one second after another, as it always had, and he watched it for some time.

He said nothing about it, and John said nothing about having watched him do it.

They walked out to the edge of the terrace. Beyond the ridge, the last light was going. Beyond that horizon there were villages, coastlines, people and roads. John could walk over the ridge tonight and come back in the morning.

“A black hole’s horizon isn’t like this one,” he said.

“No,” said Cal. “Walk over this one, and you can always walk back. Time brings you home.” He was quiet for a while. “Cross a black hole’s, and time can’t. Every tomorrow you have left is further in.”

“And you wouldn’t even feel yourself cross,” said John. “Nothing would change. The stars would look just as they had a moment before.”

Albert joined them with coffee. Even his hair looked subdued.

Somewhere above them, invisible, black holes were bending the light of everything behind them, and directing the futures of things passing too close. The Milky Way alone may hold a hundred million. Into some of them, even as the three stood there, gas was crossing the event horizon as quietly as The Van had, in the simulator, and nothing it did after that would ever be seen from outside.

“I prefer this horizon,” John admitted.

They stood there until the cups were empty. Cal had been looking up for some time.

“Everything we know about them,” he said, “we know from what’s around them. A star going around one. Gas glowing on its way in.”

“Yes,” said Albert.

“So a black hole with nothing around it. No star, no gas, nothing falling in. Just sitting out there in the dark.” He waved a hand at the sky. “It could be anywhere. We’d never know.”

“It would still bend the light of whatever was behind it,” said John. “Like the eclipse.”

“If a star happened to pass exactly behind it,” said Albert, “and someone happened to be watching.”

“And if nothing ever does?”

Albert pointed upwards.

“Sometimes,” he said, “spacetime sends the news itself.”
