Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 8 of 14

Something you can feel

On the Teide plateau, the ground is not subtle. It is immense and dark and evidently capable of persuading human beings to stay where they are.

John stamped his foot experimentally. Cal looked up from his coffee.

“Is that necessary?”

“I’m thinking about gravity.”

“Try thinking more quietly.”

“It’s pulling me down,” said John. “I can feel it. That’s what weight is: I can feel my weight.”

Albert put two fresh coffees on the table. “What do you feel?” he asked.

“Gravity.”

“Or the chair pushing you upwards?”

John looked down at the chair. “Those aren’t the same thing.”

“No,” said Albert. “They are not.”

Cal looked at the plate of lemon doughnuts. “If this lesson involves one of those becoming airborne,” he said, “I’m leaving.”

John was already on his feet. He went out to The Van and came back with the accelerometer from the cupboard: the little tube with a weight held between two springs, whose needle had stayed at zero all the time The Van coasted through empty space, and swung over whenever its thruster fired. He set it on the table and stood it on its end, so that the tube pointed straight up.

The weight sank a little in the tube. The lower spring squashed, the upper one stretched, and the needle swung over and settled.

1.00 g.

“It’s broken,” said John.

“It read one g yesterday,” said Cal, “when the engine was pushing us along at one g. And zero when we were coasting.”

“Yes. So it says we’re accelerating at one g. And we’re sitting at a table.”

Cal said nothing. He was remembering the note he had made the day before, during the long burn: it feels exactly like sitting at the café table. He had meant to think about it later. It seemed that later had arrived.

“Perhaps,” said Albert, “it is the only honest instrument you own.”

What you actually feel

The everyday answer to Albert’s question is so natural that it hardly seems like an answer at all. Sitting in a chair, you feel heavy. Standing, you feel the weight in your legs and the press of the ground on the soles of your feet. Lying in bed, you feel the mattress all along your back. The everyday explanation is that gravity is pulling you down, and that what you feel is the pull.

But look again at what you actually feel, in each case. It is always a push, and it always comes from whatever is holding you up: the chair, the ground, the mattress. Your feet feel the floor, and your back feels the bed. And inside you, the same thing happens on a smaller scale: each part of you is held up by the part beneath it, and in your inner ears tiny crystals press on the sensors that tell you which way is down. Weight, as you experience it, is the feeling of being held up.

The lift

The clearest everyday evidence is the lift. When a lift starts to go up, you feel heavier for a moment: your knees bend a little, and your stomach seems to sink. When it starts to go down, you feel lighter, and your stomach seems to rise. Then, once it is moving steadily, you feel normal again, whichever way it’s going, which is just what the principle of relativity says you should: steady motion can’t be felt.

If you stood on bathroom scales in the lift, they would show it. Suppose the scales read 70 kilograms when the lift is standing still. As the lift starts up, accelerating at one metre a second every second, they would read about 77; as it starts down, about 63. The Earth hasn’t changed, and neither have you. What has changed is how hard the floor is pushing on your feet. To start you moving upwards, the floor has to push harder than usual; to let you start moving downwards, it pushes less.

You have felt the extreme case too, though perhaps only for a moment. Jump off a low wall, and for the moment before you land you feel nothing pushing on you at all: no floor, no chair, no weight in your legs. The same thing happens at the top of a roller coaster, or a humpback bridge taken a little too fast, where the stomach seems to float. At those moments nothing is holding you up: you are simply falling.

Weight

A word with two meanings, which is the source of much confusion. In school physics, your weight is the force of gravity on you: your mass times the strength of gravity, about 9.8 newtons for every kilogram near the Earth’s surface. But what you feel as weight, and what bathroom scales measure, is something else: the push of whatever is holding you up. Physicists call that your apparent weight. Standing still on the ground, the two are equal. In a lift that’s starting to move, they differ. And in free fall, gravity is still pulling on you, but nothing is pushing, so you feel weightless and the scales read zero.

No gravity in space?

You have also seen pictures of astronauts floating in the International Space Station, and you may have been told that this is because there is no gravity in space. It’s a natural thing to think, and it is wrong by a long way.

The Space Station orbits about 400 kilometres above the ground. The Earth is about 6,400 kilometres in radius, so the Station is only about six per cent further from the Earth’s centre than you are, and the Earth’s gravity there is still nearly nine tenths of its strength at the surface. If you could build a tower 400 kilometres tall and stand at the top, you would feel only about an eighth lighter than usual. Something else is making the astronauts float. To find out what, John and Cal needed a room with no windows.

Three rooms with the blinds down

“Close the blinds,” said Albert, “and tell me where you are.”

That afternoon, The Van became a laboratory. It had blinds on every window, which, once pulled down, turned its cabin into a small, windowless room. Inside were John and Cal, the accelerometer, fixed upright to the wall, and the loose screw from The Van’s dashboard, which by now had a long history of escaping into the ventilation grille. The plan was simple. In each place they went, they would pull the blinds down, read the accelerometer, and drop the screw.

The first room: the car park

They started where they were, with The Van parked in the café car park and the blinds down.

The accelerometer read 1.00 g. John held the screw out at shoulder height and let go of it. It fell, a little faster every moment, and hit the floor about half a second later.

“Well,” said Cal, “that’s gravity.”

“That’s the control,” said John, writing it down.

The second room: deep space, at one g

Then they flew The Van out to the empty stretch of space where they had first thrown a doughnut across the cabin, so far from anything that its gravity hardly mattered. John turned The Van so that its roof pointed out into space and its floor was underneath them, as it would be in a lift, and set the engine to push at exactly one g.

The dashboard said:

ONE G, AS REQUESTED

And then, after a moment’s thought:

ARE YOU SURE?

They pulled the blinds down again. The accelerometer read 1.00 g. John held the screw out at shoulder height, and let go. It fell, a little faster every moment, and hit the floor about half a second later.

Cal looked at the screw on the floor for some time.

“If you hadn’t told me,” he said, “I’d say we were still in the car park.”

This time, though, they had a witness outside. At the moment John let go of the screw, Cal let the probe out through the airlock. The probe, as usual, had nothing pushing it once it was clear of The Van. Neither did the screw, once John had let go of it. And at that moment the two of them were moving at the same speed as The Van, so they carried on side by side.

In the probe’s pictures, the screw hung where John had let go of it, perfectly still, and it was The Van that moved. Pushed on by its engine, it surged upwards around the screw, faster every moment, until its floor came up underneath the screw and collected it.

“So the screw didn’t fall,” said John slowly. “The floor came up to meet it.”

“And to meet you,” said Cal, “for as long as the engine’s running. That’s why you feel heavy in here: the floor is pushing you.”

The third room: falling

For the last room they went home, nearly. The Van climbed to 400 kilometres above Tenerife, the height of the Space Station, above almost all of the air, and stopped there. Then it hovered, on whatever it was that let it take off from a car park and hang in the sky: a device John called the lift unit, since the handbook, being for a dishwasher, didn’t say.

They pulled the blinds down. The accelerometer read just under nine tenths of a g, since gravity is that much weaker up there. John held out the screw.

Then the dashboard said:

LIFT FAULT

And The Van’s lift unit stopped working.

Everything floated. The screw, still between John’s fingers, suddenly weighed nothing at all. The kettle, which was not a structural component, drifted gently off its shelf. John’s feet left the floor, and so did Cal’s. The accelerometer’s weight rose back to the middle of its tube, and the needle went to zero.

“We’re falling!” said Cal.

“We’re weightless!” said John.

They were both right. Anyone watching from the ground below would have seen The Van, and everything in it, falling straight down towards Tenerife, a little faster every second. But inside, nothing seemed to fall at all. John let go of the screw, and it hung in mid-air beside his hand, just as it had in deep space with the engine off. Six seconds after the fault, when they had fallen about 150 metres and were going down at over 50 metres a second, the lift unit came back on with a bang, and everyone, and everything, landed on the floor at once. The accelerometer’s needle leapt to three g, and back.

The screw rolled into the ventilation grille.

What the three rooms show

Can you tell the car park from deep space?

John and Cal couldn’t tell the first two rooms apart. Parked in the car park, the accelerometer read one g and the screw fell to the floor in half a second. In deep space, with the engine pushing at one g, the accelerometer read one g and the screw fell to the floor in half a second. Any experiment they could have done with the blinds down would have given the same result in both.

From outside, though, the two rooms looked completely different. Seen from the café, the screw fell, and the usual explanation is that gravity pulled it down to the floor. Seen from the probe in deep space, where there was no gravity to speak of, the screw stayed put and the floor came up to meet it. Inside the room, those two stories give identical results.

Why can’t falling be felt?

The third room was a surprise of a different kind. Falling towards the Earth, with gravity pulling on everything nearly as hard as it does on the ground, the inside of The Van was just like the inside of The Van coasting through deep space with its engine off: the screw floated, the accelerometer read zero, and if they hadn’t known, they couldn’t have told.

That is because gravity does something so familiar that we barely notice it: it makes everything fall in exactly the same way. A heavy thing doesn’t fall faster than a light one. The air can confuse this, slowing a feather much more than a hammer, but take the air away and the difference disappears. On 2 August 1971, at the end of his last moonwalk, the Apollo 15 commander David Scott held up a geologist’s hammer and a falcon feather, and dropped them together on the airless Moon. They hit the ground at the same moment. Galileo had argued that they would, more than three hundred years earlier.

The Moon’s surface is a crude laboratory, and physicists have tested the same idea far more precisely since. Between 1906 and 1908 the Hungarian physicist Loránd Eötvös and his colleagues showed that different materials fell alike to better than one part in a hundred million. In 2022 the team behind MICROSCOPE, a French satellite that carried two test masses of titanium and platinum round the Earth, reported that the two fell alike to within about one part in a thousand million million.

So when The Van fell, everything in it fell together: The Van, the screw, the kettle, John, Cal, and every part of John and Cal, down to the tiny crystals in their inner ears. Nothing had to push anything else to keep up, because everything was already keeping up. And with nothing pushing, there was nothing to feel.

Free fall

Motion with nothing but gravity acting: no floor, no rope, no rocket, and no air to push against. A dropped stone in a vacuum is in free fall; so is the Moon, and so is the Space Station, and so are its astronauts. Because gravity makes everything fall alike, everything in a freely falling room falls together, and nothing in it seems to fall at all. Gravity hasn’t gone away. It just can’t be felt, because nothing is pushing.

This is very different from other forces. A magnet pulls on a steel screw, but not on a doughnut. Rub a balloon on your jumper and it will pick up scraps of paper, but not the table they are lying on. If you could fall freely under one of those forces, you would notice: the screw would race off towards the magnet and leave everything else behind. Gravity is the only force we know that pulls on every kind of thing in exact proportion to its inertia, its resistance to being pushed, so that everything responds to it identically. In Isaac Newton’s theory, that is a remarkable coincidence. Newton noticed it, and tested it, with pendulums made of different materials, and found no difference. But he had no explanation for it.

What the accelerometer measures

The accelerometer, it turns out, was the honest instrument after all. Its little weight hangs between two springs, and the springs can only tell it one thing: how hard the tube is being pushed. In the falling Van, nothing pushed the tube, so the weight floated in the middle and the needle read zero, even though The Van was speeding up towards the Earth at nearly nine metres a second every second. On the café table, the table pushed the tube upwards, the springs passed the push on to the weight, and the needle read one g, even though, as far as anyone at the table could see, nothing was moving at all. And in deep space, with the engine pushing at one g, the floor pushed the tube, and the needle read one g again.

The needle, then, measures one thing only: how hard the instrument is being pushed, by the table, by the floor of an accelerating Van or, falling freely, by nothing at all. Whether it is speeding up relative to the ground makes no difference to it.

Proper acceleration

The acceleration you can feel, and an accelerometer measures: how hard you are being pushed by whatever is holding you up or driving you along, such as a floor, a chair or a rocket’s engine. Standing on the ground, it is one g, though you aren’t going anywhere. Falling freely, it is zero, though your speed is increasing. In a rocket in deep space, it is whatever the engine provides. Why physicists call the reading on the ground an acceleration at all is one of the surprises of the tenth journey.

Your phone has an accelerometer in it, a tiny one, and it works in the same way. Lying on a table, it reads about 9.8 metres per second per second, pointing upwards, which is how the phone knows which way up it is. Some laptops with spinning hard disks used the same trick in reverse. In 2005 Apple began fitting its laptops with what it called a Sudden Motion Sensor: an accelerometer that watched for its reading suddenly falling to zero. A reading of zero meant the laptop was falling, and the sensor parked the disk’s delicate reading heads before the laptop hit the floor.

“So the accelerometer is happy when it’s falling?” Cal asked, when John explained this to him.

“Yes,” said John. “It’s the landing that upsets it.”

Albert, who had overheard, nodded slowly. “I should like that sentence on a sign outside the café.”

The equivalence principle

In 1907, two years after his first paper on relativity, Einstein was writing a long review of it for a scientific yearbook, and trying to fit gravity into the new theory. He was sitting at his desk in the patent office in Bern, so he later told it, when it occurred to him that a person falling freely, from the roof of a house say, would not feel their own weight. Years later, he called it the happiest thought of his life. It is the thought behind John and Cal’s three rooms.

The equivalence principle

In a small enough room, over a short enough time, no experiment can tell the effects of gravity from the effects of acceleration. A room standing on the ground behaves exactly like a room accelerating at one g through empty space; a room falling freely behaves exactly like a room coasting through empty space, far from any gravity. Einstein made it the starting point of his theory of gravity, general relativity. In a bigger room, or over a longer time, the two can be told apart, as the stretched Van later in this journey shows.

Einstein took the coincidence that puzzled Newton and asked a question about it. If falling freely feels exactly like coasting through empty space, and no experiment in a small room can tell them apart, are they really two different things? Inside the falling Van, a screw left alone stayed where it was, as it had in The Van coasting far from anything: for those few seconds, the falling Van behaved in every way like an inertial reference frame, in the sense of the first journey. And the car park, by every test John and Cal could make inside it, behaved like a room being pushed along at one g.

That leaves two ways of describing the café, and the weight you feel there.

In the first, the everyday one and Isaac Newton’s, gravity is a pull. It pulls you down, the floor pushes you up just as hard, the two balance, and you stay where you are. The accelerometer on the table reads one g because the table is holding it up against gravity, and a dropped screw falls because gravity pulls it down to the floor.

In the second, the floor is pushing you because it is accelerating, as The Van’s floor pushed John and Cal through deep space, and there is no pull to balance. The accelerometer reads one g because the floor is pushing it along. A dropped screw isn’t pulled anywhere: the floor comes up to meet it.

The second picture sounds absurd. If the floor were pushing you along the way The Van’s engine did, you would expect to be going somewhere, and the café has stood in the same place for as long as anyone can remember. But inside a room with the blinds down, no experiment can choose between the two pictures. That is what the equivalence principle says, and it is as far as the three rooms can take us.

Newton’s picture describes everything John and Cal measured perfectly well. What it can’t do is say why gravity, alone among the forces, moves everything alike: in Newton’s theory, that is a coincidence. Einstein suspected it was a clue. The equivalence principle also makes predictions that Newton’s picture doesn’t, about light and about clocks, which the rest of this journey and the next begin to test. Which of the two pictures is right, and how the second could possibly be true of a café that never goes anywhere, is the subject of the tenth journey.

Falling round the Earth, and round the Sun

Now the astronauts make sense. The Space Station isn’t beyond the Earth’s gravity. It is falling, all the time, just as The Van fell, and so are its astronauts. The difference is that it is also moving sideways at about 28,000 kilometres an hour. As it falls, the curved surface of the Earth falls away beneath it, so it never gets any closer. It falls all the way round the Earth about every hour and a half, and has been doing so since 1998. Its astronauts float for the same reason that John and Cal floated: they and their spacecraft are falling together, and nothing is pushing them.

And there is an older question waiting here. In the first journey, sitting at the café, John and Cal wondered why they couldn’t feel the Earth’s motion. Its steady motion, by the principle of relativity, they couldn’t feel. But the Earth travels round the Sun on a curved path, changing direction all the time, and a change of direction is an acceleration. Why can’t we feel that?

Because the Earth is falling round the Sun, as the Space Station falls round the Earth, and everything on it is falling with it. Nothing has to push us to keep us on the Earth’s curved path, so we feel nothing. The only push we feel is the Earth’s own surface, holding us up against the Earth’s own gravity.

When the room gets too big

“So a falling box hides gravity completely,” said Cal, when they were back in the car park. “Get in one, and you can’t feel it at all.”

“In a small box,” said Albert. “Try a bigger one.”

The Van’s simulator could stretch The Van to any length they liked. John told it to make The Van as long as Teide is high, nearly four kilometres from end to end, and to put a screw floating at each end. Then he had it drop the whole enormous Van from 400 kilometres up, as the lift unit had dropped the real one.

At first, nothing happened, as before. Everything fell together, and the two screws hung in their places at either end of the cabin. But after a few seconds the screws began to drift. They were drifting towards each other. After six seconds each had moved about four centimetres; after a minute, more than four metres.

Then John turned the stretched Van on its end, so that one screw was nearly four kilometres above the other, and dropped it again. This time the screws drifted apart, and twice as fast.

Why the screws drift

The reason is that gravity isn’t quite the same everywhere. It always pulls towards the centre of the Earth. Two screws side by side, four kilometres apart, are pulled towards the same point, 6,800 kilometres below them, along lines that aren’t quite parallel. As they fall, the lines converge, and the screws close in on each other. Two screws one above the other are pulled in the same direction, but not equally hard: gravity weakens with height, so the lower screw is pulled a little more strongly than the upper one, falls a little faster, and the two draw apart.

A freely falling room can hide gravity only where gravity is the same throughout the room. In a small room, over a short time, it very nearly is, which is why the real Van’s six-second fall felt like deep space. In a big room, or over a long time, the differences add up, and no choice of falling room can remove them.

Tidal effects

The small differences in gravity from one place to another, which make nearby freely falling objects drift together or apart. Near the Earth, objects side by side drift together, because they fall towards the same centre, and objects one above the other drift apart, because the lower one is pulled harder. They grow with the size of the room and, roughly, with the square of the time. They are named after the tides: the Earth falls freely in the pull of the Moon and the Sun, and the oceans on its near and far sides are pulled slightly more and slightly less than its middle, so they bulge, and the sea rises and falls about twice a day.

That is why the equivalence principle needs its small print, “in a small enough room, over a short enough time”. The drift also shows that falling can hide gravity only in part: letting a room fall makes gravity vanish in one place, but not everywhere at once. Something remains, in the way that freely falling paths draw together and apart. What that something is, and why it suggests that spacetime itself is curved, is also a question for the tenth journey.

How the drift grows with size and time

Start with The Van, six metres long, play the fall, and watch how little the screws move: about 14 millimetres in a minute. Then make the room larger, Teide, a thousand kilometres, and watch the drift grow in proportion. Turn the room on its end, and see the screws separate instead, twice as fast. And notice the shape of the record: let the fall run twice as long, and the drift grows about four times. A small room hides gravity very well for a short time; a large room, or a long wait, gives it away.

The light in the lift

Albert had one more experiment, and he described it while Cal rescued the screw from the grille with a teaspoon.

“Put a torch on one wall of your Van,” he said, “in deep space, with the engine pushing at one g. Shine it straight across to the other wall.”

“It goes straight across,” said John.

“Does it? While the light is crossing, the engine is pushing the Van upwards, faster and faster. The light is not being pushed.”

Cal put the teaspoon down. “It’s the screw again.”

It is. Seen from the probe outside, the light travels in a perfectly straight line, just as the screw hung perfectly still. But while it crosses, The Van accelerates upwards around it. So, measured inside The Van, the beam arrives at the far wall a little lower than it set out, and its path, like the screw’s, curves down towards the floor.

The next figure runs Albert’s experiment with the light slowed down enormously, so that The Van has time to move while it crosses, and the drop drawn far larger than it really is. Start with the engine off. The Van coasts beside the probe, and the light goes straight across the cabin, as John said it would. Then switch the engine on. Seen from the probe, on the left, the light still goes straight across, but The Van rises around it, faster every moment, so that inside The Van, on the right, the light’s path curves down towards the floor and meets the far wall below the torch. Last, put The Van back in the car park. The accelerometer reads one g, as it did with the engine on, and inside, the light’s path curves down in just the same way.

The real drop is fantastically small. Light crosses the cabin from side to side, about two metres, in about seven billionths of a second, and in that time The Van, accelerating at one g, moves up by about two tenths of a millionth of a millionth of a millimetre: an eighth of the width of a single proton. Tiny as it is, if the equivalence principle is right, and a room standing on the ground behaves in every way like a room accelerating at one g, then light crossing a room on the ground must curve downwards in the same way. Gravity must bend light.

Near the Earth the bending is far too small to see. Near something far more massive, such as the Sun, it can be measured, and the eleventh journey goes looking for it.

John worked through the numbers on a napkin, and then sat back.

“So even light, which has no rest mass, is involved.”

“Yes,” said Albert. “And clocks too.”

“I thought we’d finished with clocks,” Cal complained.

“We have scarcely begun,” said Albert.

Back to Teide

They had a last coffee as the shadows lengthened across the plateau. Outside, The Van let out a long hiss that suggested a leak. Nobody wished to investigate it until after tea.

John got up, stood on the volcanic ground beside the table, and flexed his knees.

“I’ve stood on this mountain all these years,” he said, “and I thought I could feel the Earth pulling me down.”

“What you feel is the mountain refusing to let you fall,” said Cal.

Albert smiled. “A good way of putting it.”

For a moment John imagined the chair, the table, the café and the whole plateau, all holding him up, away from the path he would otherwise follow, down towards the centre of the Earth. Every one of them pushing.

“But which is it?” he said. “Is the Earth pulling me down, and the mountain holding me up against the pull? Or is the mountain pushing me, the way The Van’s floor pushed us in deep space?”

“In a room with the blinds down,” said Albert, “no experiment can tell you. To find out, you will need more than a screw.”

Then John remembered the light, curving down across The Van, and Albert’s remark about clocks.

“If gravity and acceleration are related,” he said, “does that mean clocks at different heights can disagree?”

Albert reached beneath the counter. He had, apparently, been keeping two atomic clocks beside the napkins.
