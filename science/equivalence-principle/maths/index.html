What does the scale read?

Bathroom scales measure the push of the floor, so their reading follows from Newton’s second law: the total force on you equals your mass times your acceleration. Take upwards as positive. Two forces act on someone standing on scales in a lift: gravity, pulling down with force mgmgmg, and the scales, pushing up with force NNN. If the lift, and the person, accelerate upwards at aaa,

N−mg=ma,soN=m(g+a).N - mg = ma, \qquad \text{so} \qquad N = m(g + a).N−mg=ma,soN=m(g+a).

For someone of mass 70 kilograms, with g≈9.8 m/s2g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2g≈9.8 m/s2:

  • Standing still, a=0a = 0a=0, and the scales push with N=70×9.8≈686N = 70 \times 9.8 \approx 686N=70×9.8≈686 newtons. Scales show this as 70 kilograms.
  • Starting upwards at a=1 m/s2a = 1\ \text{m/s}^2a=1 m/s2, N=70×10.8=756N = 70 \times 10.8 = 756N=70×10.8=756 newtons, shown as about 77 kilograms.
  • Starting downwards at a=−1 m/s2a = -1\ \text{m/s}^2a=−1 m/s2, N=70×8.8=616N = 70 \times 8.8 = 616N=70×8.8=616 newtons, shown as about 63 kilograms.
  • Falling freely, a=−ga = -ga=−g, and N=m(g−g)=0N = m(g - g) = 0N=m(g−g)=0. The scales read nothing, though the person’s mass is still 70 kilograms and gravity is still pulling on them with 686 newtons.

And in The Van in deep space, with no gravity to speak of and the engine accelerating it at a=ga = ga=g, the scales push with N=m(0+g)=mgN = m(0 + g) = mgN=m(0+g)=mg: the same 686 newtons as in the car park, and the same reading. That is the equivalence principle, in one line.

How far the screws drift

Gravity 400 kilometres up. Newton’s law of gravity gives its strength a distance rrr from the centre of the Earth as

g(r)=GMr2,g(r) = \frac{GM}{r^2},g(r)=r2GM​,

where GM≈3.986×1014GM \approx 3.986 \times 10^{14}GM≈3.986×1014 cubic metres per second squared for the Earth. Gravity falls off with the square of the distance from the centre, so 400 kilometres up, at r=6,771r = 6{,}771r=6,771 kilometres instead of the surface’s 6,3716{,}3716,371, it is weaker by the factor

(6,3716,771)2=0.885,\left(\frac{6{,}371}{6{,}771}\right)^2 = 0.885,(6,7716,371​)2=0.885,

nearly nine tenths of its strength at the surface, and about an eighth less: g≈8.69g \approx 8.69g≈8.69 metres per second per second. Falling from rest for six seconds, as The Van did when its lift unit failed, it gains 8.69×6≈528.69 \times 6 \approx 528.69×6≈52 metres a second and falls 12×8.69×62≈156\tfrac{1}{2} \times 8.69 \times 6^2 \approx 15621​×8.69×62≈156 metres.

Side by side. Two screws a distance ddd apart, at the same height, start from rest and fall straight towards the centre of the Earth, each along its own line. The two lines meet at the centre, and the screws are always at the same height as each other, so the gap between them shrinks in proportion to their distance from the centre. When each has fallen a distance sss from its starting distance r0r_0r0​, the gap is d (r0−s)/r0d\,(r_0 - s)/r_0d(r0​−s)/r0​: smaller by

For a short fall, s≈12gt2s \approx \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2s≈21​gt2, so

Δ≈g2r0 d t2.\Delta \approx \frac{g}{2 r_0}\, d\, t^2.Δ≈2r0​g​dt2.

So the drift grows in proportion to the size of the room and to the square of the time. At 400 kilometres up, g/r0=8.69/(6.771×106)≈1.28×10−6g / r_0 = 8.69 / (6.771 \times 10^6) \approx 1.28 \times 10^{-6}g/r0​=8.69/(6.771×106)≈1.28×10−6 per second squared. With the numbers:

  • The Van, 6 metres long, falling for a minute: s≈15.6s \approx 15.6s≈15.6 kilometres, and the gap closes by 6×15,600/6,771,000≈0.0146 \times 15{,}600 / 6{,}771{,}000 \approx 0.0146×15,600/6,771,000≈0.014 metres, about 14 millimetres.
  • Teide’s height, 3,715 metres, for six seconds: s≈156s \approx 156s≈156 metres, and the gap closes by about 8.6 centimetres, so each screw moves about 4.3 centimetres.
  • The same, for a minute: the gap closes by about 8.6 metres, 4.3 metres each.

One above the other. Now the screws fall along the same line, and the difference is in how hard they are pulled. Differentiating GM/r2GM/r^2GM/r2, a small height ddd further out, gravity is weaker by about

12×2gr0 d×t2=gr0 d t2.\tfrac{1}{2} \times \frac{2g}{r_0}\, d \times t^2 = \frac{g}{r_0}\, d\, t^2.21​×r0​2g​d×t2=r0​g​dt2.

Side by side, each screw’s pull, aimed at the centre, has a sideways part towards the other of g×(d/2)/rg \times (d/2)/rg×(d/2)/r, so together they close at gd/rgd/rgd/r. One above the other, they part at 2gd/r2gd/r2gd/r: twice as fast, as the stretched Van showed.

How far the torch beam drops
