Four and a half billion years

The simulator had been waiting four and a half billion years, and by the morning it had run out of patience.

When John and Cal came out to the car park with their coffee, The Van’s dashboard screen was already lit, and it spoke before anyone had touched it.

REWIND READY: 4,567 MILLION YEARS. PLEASE HOLD ON TO YOUR COFFEE

Albert followed them out with a plate of lemon doughnuts, set it on the dashboard as though he did so every day, and climbed into the back. Cal touched his shirt pocket, where the napkin with the grain of sugar in it was folded, the words on the outside where he could see them.

“Somewhere in what we’re about to see,” said John, “are the atoms in that.”

“All of them,” said Albert. “Its hydrogen from the first three minutes, its carbon and oxygen from stars long dead. Until this morning they were scattered across a great deal of the galaxy.”

Cal pressed the button.

The stars on the screen began to slide. The Moon came apart and was gone, then the Earth and the other planets, and the Sun swung backwards round the galaxy, twenty times in a couple of minutes. Then the Sun itself shrank and went out, and where it had been there was only a darkness among the stars.

It was a cloud: one dense knot in a great cold cloud of gas and dust, the kind the eighth journey had found every star being born in. And it was already rich. Nine billion years of stars had lived and died before it, and as the last journey found, they had left their carbon, oxygen, silicon and iron in the gas. It was still nearly all hydrogen and helium, but about one and a half per cent of it, by weight, was everything else, much of it in grains of dust and soot finer than smoke. Nor was the knot alone. Stars are born in crowds, and the Sun had brothers and sisters, a whole cluster of them, long since scattered across the galaxy.

After a moment, the simulator added a second line.

“It’s started keeping secrets,” said Cal.

Faster as it falls

The knot was falling in on itself under its own weight, as the sixth and eighth journeys had watched clouds do. And as it fell, Cal noticed something.

“It’s turning,” he said.

It hadn’t been, or not so anyone could see. Now the whole cloud was slowly going round, and the more it shrank, the faster it went.

That turning answers a question most people never think to ask, though the answer is written across the sky. From the café terrace at night, the planets are never just anywhere: Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn all keep to one line across the sky, the line the Sun follows by day. All eight planets go round the Sun in nearly the same flat plane, tilted to one another by a few degrees at most, so from the Earth, inside the plane, we see them along its edge. They all go round the same way, too, and the Sun turns on its axis that way as well.

Nothing obliges them to. Planets made separately, or captured one at a time from passing clouds, would go round in every plane and in both directions, like comets falling in from the far edge of the Solar System, which arrive from every direction in the sky. Something made the planets orderly, and it was the turning.

Try it

Sit on a swivel chair with a bag of sugar in each hand, your arms stretched out wide. Get someone to start you turning gently, then pull the bags in to your chest. You speed up, quite startlingly, without anyone pushing you. Stretch your arms out again and you slow down. Ice skaters spin up the same way, pulling their arms in tight.

Warning

Do this gently, on a chair that’s steady on its feet, with plenty of room round you, and stop if you feel dizzy.

A turning thing keeps its amount of turning unless something outside twists it. For something going round a centre, that amount is its speed round the centre multiplied by how far out it is. Pull it in to half the distance and, to keep the same amount, it must go twice as fast; and since its circle is now only half as long, it gets round four times as often. Physicists call the amount of turning angular momentum, and like energy, it’s never lost, only handed on.

Why can’t gravity change it? Because gravity pulls everything in the cloud straight towards the middle. A pull towards the middle can make a piece of gas fall inwards, but it can’t push it round faster or hold it back, any more than you could speed up a roundabout by pulling on it towards its centre. Nothing else pushes on the falling gas from outside. So as the cloud shrinks, its amount of turning stays exactly what it was, and it has to go round faster and faster. There’s no way for it not to.

The simulator’s cloud was about a third of a light-year across, turning about once in twenty million years, so slowly that the hour hand on the café’s clock goes round about fifteen billion times as fast. It fell in over about a hundred and eighty thousand years. By the time its outer gas had fallen four hundred times closer to the middle, it was going round a hundred and sixty thousand times as fast: once in about a century and a quarter.

A disc

But the turning does more than speed the cloud up. It stops it falling in.

Anything going round in a circle needs a pull towards the middle to keep it on the circle, or it flies off in a straight line, as a conker does when you let go of its string. The faster it goes round, and the tighter the circle, the harder the pull must be. As a piece of gas falls inwards and spins up, it needs more and more of the cloud’s pull just to stay on its ever-faster circle, until at last all the pull is used up holding it there and none is left to drag it further in. It stops falling, and goes into orbit.

That only works, though, for gas falling across the turning. Gas above or below the middle of the cloud, falling along the axis it turns round, gets no closer to the axis as it falls, so it doesn’t spin up at all, and nothing stops it. It falls straight towards the middle plane. There, gas falling from above meets gas falling from below, and their up-and-down motions cancel in the crash, the energy going into heat, which the dust glows away into space. The going round can’t cancel like that, because all of it goes the same way. So the cloud collapses along its axis but not across it. It ends as a thin, flat disc, going round in one direction, with nearly all of the turning in it and almost none of the up and down.

Protoplanetary disc

A flat disc of gas and dust going round a newly formed star, left over from the cloud it was born from. It’s flat because the cloud was turning: gas can fall freely along the cloud’s axis, but falling across it, the gas spins faster and faster until it goes into orbit. Discs like this are found round most young stars, typically tens to hundreds of times as wide as the Earth’s orbit, and they last a few million years. Planets are made in them.

On the screen, the cloud had become exactly that: a broad, thin disc, glowing a dull red where it was thickest, and in the middle of it a bright knot where the gas with too little turning to stay out had fallen all the way in.

Cal looked at the disc for some time. Then he looked at the plate of doughnuts on the dashboard, and then at the disc again.

“Albert,” he said. “That shape.”

Albert said nothing at all, and offered him a doughnut.

So the planets go round the same way, in nearly the same plane, because they were made in one disc, from one cloud’s turning. The only way out would be for something to have twisted their orbits round since, and nothing has. (The planets’ own spins have been knocked about: Uranus lies on its side, and Venus turns backwards. Their orbits still lie where the disc was.) Most of the cloud ended up in the Sun, poured onto it from the disc’s inner edge. The disc kept perhaps a few hundredths of it, and every planet, moon and comet was made from that.

The frost line

The bright knot in the middle was already shining, by the heat of its own squeezing, as the eighth journey found every young star does. Its core wouldn’t reach the ten million degrees it needed to fuse hydrogen, as the sixth journey found every star must, for a few tens of millions of years, and by then the giant planets would long since have formed. But it was bright.

THE YOUNG SUN: SHINING. FUSION: NOT YET. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

Now the disc had a lamp in the middle, and that changed what it could make. Close to the young Sun, the disc was hot; far out, it was bitterly cold. And what can be solid depends on how hot it is.

“So near the Sun everything’s melted,” said John, “and further out it freezes. Water freezes at nought degrees. So somewhere about here,” he pointed at the disc, about where the Earth would be, “the water turns to ice.”

“In a freezer,” said Cal. “There’s no air out there.”

It’s easy to forget, and it moves the line a long way. Liquid water needs air pressing on it. In the near-empty disc, ice doesn’t melt as it warms: it turns straight into vapour, as ice cubes do, very slowly, when they’re left in a freezer for months and shrink without ever getting wet. In space that happens fast unless the ice is very cold, below about minus a hundred degrees Celsius.

How cold was the disc? A grain of dust in it was warmed by the young Sun’s light and lost heat by glowing, as everything does, and it settled where the two balanced. Close to the Sun the light is fierce and the grain was hot; further out, the light is spread over a wider sphere and the grain was colder. At the Earth’s distance, once the disc had settled, it came to about seven degrees Celsius, a cool spring day. At Jupiter’s, about a hundred and fifty below. Ice could last only beyond about two and three-quarter times the Earth’s distance from the Sun, about where the asteroid belt is now.

Frost line

The distance from a young star beyond which its disc is cold enough for water to stay frozen. In the near-vacuum of a disc, ice doesn’t melt but turns straight to vapour, so it lasts only below about minus a hundred degrees Celsius. Inside the frost line, only rock and metal can be solid; beyond it, there is ice as well, which gives growing planets far more to build with. In the young Solar System it lay, once the disc had settled, about where the asteroid belt is now.

At first, while gas was still pouring through the disc onto the young Sun, the inner disc was far hotter, hot enough near the Sun to melt rock and even boil it, and the frost line lay further out. As the disc settled, the line moved in.

The asteroid belt still shows where the line was. The asteroids on its inner side, nearer the Sun, are mostly stony and dry. Those on its outer side are dark and rich in carbon, with water locked into their minerals, and Ceres, the biggest object in the belt, is a ball of rock and ice. The frost line ran through the middle of them.

Dust into planets

How do you make a planet out of dust? The simulator showed it in stages, and it wasn’t equally sure of all of them.

The first stage happens under every bed. Dust grains drifting gently in the gas touched and stuck, the way fluff gathers into balls in the corners of a room, and the fluff grew into pebbles. The next is harder. Things a metre or so across are a problem: the gas, held up a little by its own pressure, goes round slightly more slowly than they do, so they feel a headwind, which should drag them spiralling into the Sun within a century or two, and when they collide they tend to break rather than stick. Nobody is sure how the young Solar System got past that size. The best idea at present is that pebbles crowded together in the gas, as cars bunch up in traffic, until a cloud of them was dense enough to fall together under its own weight, all at once, into a body kilometres across.

PEBBLES INTO BOULDERS: RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: LOW. STILL ARGUED

There’s a witness. On 1 January 2019, NASA’s New Horizons spacecraft flew past Arrokoth, a small icy world about forty-four times as far from the Sun as the Earth is, where nothing has disturbed it since it was made. It’s two lumps, about twenty and fifteen kilometres across, stuck together with hardly a mark where they touched. They met at a few metres a second at most, perhaps slower than walking pace. Things smashing into one another at speed couldn’t have made it; a cloud of pebbles falling together could.

After that, gravity took over: bodies kilometres across pull on one another, and the bigger ones pulled in their neighbours and grew fastest. Inside the frost line, with only rock and metal to build with, they grew into a few dozen small worlds, from the size of the Moon to the size of Mars. Beyond it, with ice as well, there was two to four times as much to build with, and something grew far bigger. Meteorites come in two families whose atoms differ slightly, one made inside Jupiter’s orbit and one outside it, and their ages show the two were kept apart from about a million years after the first grains until three or four million. The obvious thing to have stood between them is Jupiter, whose core must have been about twenty times as heavy as the Earth within its first million years.

A core that heavy can pull in the disc’s gas, and Jupiter did, swelling into a giant of hydrogen and helium; Saturn, further out and a little later, did the same. They had to be quick: about half the stars in young clusters have lost their discs within about three million years, blown away by their own light and wind and their neighbours’. Uranus and Neptune, far out, where everything grew more slowly, caught only a little gas before it went, and stayed mostly ice and rock. Further out still, nothing grew big at all, and the leftovers are still there, in a broad ring of small icy worlds beyond Neptune called the Kuiper belt, Arrokoth among them.

Closer in, there was no gas left to catch by the time the rocky worlds had grown. They pulled one another into crossing orbits and crashed together, over tens of millions of years, until four were left: Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars.

“And the Moon?” said Cal. “Where does that come in?”

“Not yet,” said Albert. “Tomorrow.” He nodded at the sky, where the Moon, two days past new, was too close to the Sun to see. “The evidence is up there. Go and read it first.”

He climbed out of The Van.

“Aren’t you coming?”

“I have seen it,” said Albert, which nobody liked to ask about, and he took the doughnuts back to the café, leaving two in a paper bag.

The Moon, by earthlight

John had been to the Moon once before, alone, to catch Albert’s photons. Cal never had.

“So we’re going to the dark side,” said Cal, buckling himself in.

“Today the dark side is this one,” said John.

It’s a common muddle. The Moon’s far side never faces the Earth, but it isn’t dark: over a month it gets exactly as much sunlight as the side we see, and when the Moon is new, with the side facing us in its night, the far side is in full day. The Moon was two days past new, its face towards the Earth nearly all in darkness.

The Van rose from the car park with a noise like a lift deciding which floor it was on, out of the blue of the morning into the black. The speedometer, with nothing to measure against, said 17 mph, and nobody remarked on it.

The Moon is about 384,000 kilometres away. As The Van came in over its near side, the ground below was in deep night, and yet it wasn’t dark. It was lit, softly and evenly, a cold blue-grey, by a light from behind them. Cal turned round in his seat and saw why.

The Earth hung in the black sky, nearly full, blue and white and nearly four times as wide as the Moon looks from the plateau, lighting the ground forty or fifty times as brightly as the full Moon lights the plateau. Night on the Moon’s near side is never really dark, because the Earth is always up. It’s the faint glow on the dark part of a thin crescent Moon, seen from the Earth, and Leonardo da Vinci worked out five hundred years ago what it was: sunlight, bounced off the Earth onto the Moon, and back.

And the Earth didn’t move. Hour after hour it hung in the same place in the sky, because the Moon keeps one face to the Earth. The Moon once turned faster, but the Earth’s pull stretched it very slightly into an egg shape, and pulling on the stretch braked its turning until it matched its going round: tidal effects, working the other way.

By earthlight the Moon’s face showed its two kinds of ground: the seas, wide and smooth and dark, and the highlands, pale and rough and crowded. Out on one of the dark plains a single mountain stood up alone.

“That’s Pico,” said John, checking the map. “Mons Pico. It’s named after Teide.”

Cal laughed out loud.

It is. It was probably the German astronomer Johann Schröter who named it, after the volcano outside the café’s window, and a scatter of peaks nearby is called the Teneriffe Mountains.

Off to the west, something was shining far more brightly than anything round it, even in the earthlight: the crater Aristarchus. On 19 April 1787 William Herschel saw three glowing spots on the dark part of the Moon and wrote them up as “three volcanos”, and the brightest was probably Aristarchus, catching the earthlight as it was now.

“Volcanoes,” said John. “That’s what I’ve always thought they were.”

Holes, not mountains

They flew east, towards the line where the Sun was rising, and as the light changed, John explained.

“Look at them,” he said. “A ring of cliffs, a flat floor, and often a peak in the middle. It’s the plateau: the great crater the café sits in, with Teide in the middle. The Moon’s covered in volcanoes, and the seas are lava. I’ve stood on the stuff.”

It was a perfectly good argument, and for a long time many astronomers who thought about it agreed with him. He was half right.

At the dawn line the Sun was only just coming up, so low that every rise in the ground threw a shadow kilometres long, and the craters stood out, their rims lit gold on one side, their insides black: crater after crater, from the size of the café’s car park to the size of Tenerife and bigger, overlapping, inside one another, on top of one another, as far as they could see.

Seen like that, they were holes, and that was the first thing wrong with John’s volcanoes. A volcano is a mountain with a hole at the top: Teide stands more than three and a half kilometres above the sea, and the crater at its summit is about a hundred metres across. Even the plateau’s great crater, sixteen kilometres long, sits high on the island’s pile of lava. A crater on the Moon is a hole in flat ground, its floor well below the plain round it, with a low rim of thrown-out rock. The American geologist Grove Karl Gilbert made exactly this point in the 1890s, and argued that the Moon’s craters had been made by things falling on it.

One thing troubled Gilbert: the craters are round. Things falling on the Moon would come in at a slant, and a stone thrown at a slant into sand makes a long, low groove. Why weren’t the craters oval?

The answer is speed. Things hitting the Moon arrive at something like twenty kilometres a second, give or take, and at that speed every kilogram carries the energy of dozens of kilograms of explosive. The falling body doesn’t plough a groove. It’s stopped dead within a few times its own size, and all that energy comes out at once, as an explosion, which blasts out a round hole whatever angle it came in at. Only things skimming in almost flat make oval craters, and a few craters are oval. Every crater is the scar of an explosion, many times wider than whatever made it.

Volcanoes don’t make holes a thousand kilometres across, and the Moon has basins that size. Nor do they make pits the size of a pinhead, and the rocks the Apollo astronauts brought back are speckled with tiny craters, dug by grains of dust arriving at the same tremendous speeds. The Earth has craters too. In 1960 Eugene Shoemaker and his colleagues found coesite at Meteor Crater, in Arizona, a form of silica made only by a shock of enormous pressure, and nobody has doubted since that a falling body made it. To make what the Moon shows, a volcano would have to make holes instead of mountains, round ones, of every size from a pinhead to an ocean. There’s no such volcano.

So the craters are wounds. But the seas really are lava, and there John was right. The rocks brought back from them are basalt, a dark volcanic rock like a great deal of the island’s own old lava, from molten rock welling up from inside the Moon to flood the low ground. It filled the biggest basins, which is why so many of the seas are round.

John’s picture of the Moon’s mountains shows impact and lava at once. The Apennines aren’t a range in the ordinary sense at all. They’re part of the rim of the Imbrium basin, blasted out about 3.9 billion years ago by something enormous, and the smooth plain beside them is lava, poured in later: the Sea of Showers.

Reading the order

Once you know that, the Moon’s face can be read like a page of history, by three rules.

The first is that whatever is on top came later. Where one crater breaks into another, the one doing the breaking is younger. In John’s picture of the craters Theophilus, Cyrillus and Catharina, Theophilus’s rim cuts into Cyrillus beside it, so Theophilus came second, and the three get older, and more battered, from north to south.

The second is that a crater flooded with lava is older than the lava. The Bay of Rainbows, in another of John’s pictures, was a great crater about 250 kilometres across until the lavas of the Sea of Showers poured in and drowned its wall on the seaward side, leaving the rest as an arc of mountains round a bay.

The third turns order into age: count the craters. The longer a piece of ground has lain out under the falling stones, the more craters it has.

Albert put it best, over the radio, with the second and a quarter’s pause each way John remembered from his last visit.

“My cupboard,” he said. “The props from your earlier journeys. The currant dough has been in there since the last series but one, and it has more dust on it than the black box, which came later. You can tell which came first by the dust. But to say how long, you must know how fast the dust falls, and for that you need one thing in the cupboard whose date you already know.”

On the Moon, the seas have far fewer craters than the highlands, so they’re younger: the lava flooded in after most of the battering was over, burying the craters already there. The highlands are so crowded that a new crater can hardly land without wiping out an old one. And a few craters are much younger than anything round them. Aristarchus, the bright crater Herschel took for a volcano, is so bright partly because it’s young, perhaps 280 million years old by one count of the small craters on its floor. Rock left out on the Moon slowly darkens in the wind from the Sun and the rain of tiny grains, and Aristarchus’s hasn’t had time.

Then they flew on, over the Moon’s edge and into full sunshine, and saw the far side.

It hardly has any seas. On the near side the dark plains cover nearly a third of the ground; on the far side, about two per cent. The far side is highland, crater on crater on crater, with the biggest basin on the Moon, about 2,500 kilometres across, near its south pole. Its crust is much thicker than the near side’s, so lava seldom found a way up through it, though nobody is sure why the Moon grew lopsided. Nobody saw any of it until October 1959, when the Soviet spacecraft Luna 3 photographed it, and the first rock from it came home only in June 2024, collected by China’s Chang’e 6 inside that great basin. (The Moon’s two faces are side by side on this site’s own Moon, Ithil.)

Cal took one of Albert’s doughnuts out of the bag, held it up, and let go. It drifted down so slowly, in the Moon’s gentle pull, that he caught it again on the way to the floor.

“A sixth of the weight,” he said. “I could live here.”

“There’s no café,” said John.

What the Moon is made of

The pale highlands hold a clue to the Moon’s making. Their rock is mostly a light, glittering stone called anorthosite, built of crystals light enough to float, and it covers the whole Moon. So the Moon’s whole outer part was once molten, an ocean of molten rock hundreds of kilometres deep, on which those crystals floated up and gathered into a crust, as ice gathers on a freezing pond. Apollo 15 brought back a piece of it from the foot of the Apennines, the Genesis Rock, about four billion years old. Something gave the young Moon a great deal of heat, all at once.

Other clues agree. The Moon is about three-fifths as dense as the Earth, because it has hardly any iron: its iron core is about a fifth of its width, where the Earth’s is more than half of the Earth’s. Its rocks are dry, with almost none of the water and other easily boiled things in the Earth’s. And the oxygen in the Moon’s rocks comes in the same mixture of isotopes, the slightly heavier and lighter kinds of the third journey, as the oxygen in the Earth’s, while rocks from Mars, which reach us as meteorites, have a different mixture.

So the clues were these. Something had heated the young Moon through, all at once, until its whole outer part was molten. Something had left it short of iron, and dry. And it was made of what looks very like the Earth’s own stuff. A Moon made somewhere else and captured would have no reason to look like any of that. Whatever made it had happened to the Earth as well, and John and Cal looked at each other over the dashboard and didn’t say what they were both thinking: that it must have been very large.

The Moon is also moving. It is drifting away from the Earth, about 3.8 centimetres a year, measured by firing lasers from the Earth at mirrors on its surface and timing the echoes. One is a French reflector carried by Lunokhod 1, the first rover on another world, which came down in 1970 near the Bay of Rainbows, in John’s picture.

A clock in every rock

They were back by the middle of the afternoon. Albert had put the kettle on, and his cupboard of props was standing open.

“You can say which came first,” he said, when they had told him about the craters. “Now, how long ago?”

The answer is in the rocks, and John and Cal had already met the clock. In Through the Wall, they found that radioactive atoms decay by pure chance, each with the same chance every second however long it has existed, so that after a certain time, its half-life, half of any sample has gone, after twice that time three-quarters, and so on. Each kind has its own half-life, and nothing changes it: not heat, nor pressure, nor anything the inside of a planet can do. So radioactive atoms are clocks, and very good ones, if you can tell when they started.

Teleport to the Quantum Mechanics moonThrough the WallHalf-lives, and why radioactive atoms decay by pure chance

A rock starts the clock when it sets. As molten rock cools, its atoms lock into crystals, and some crystals take in radioactive atoms as they grow while shutting out the atoms those decay into. Uranium decays, through a chain of steps, into lead, and some crystals welcome uranium and have no room for lead. From the moment such a crystal sets, every atom of lead in it was once an atom of uranium. So the more lead there is for each uranium atom left, the longer ago the crystal formed. Count them, and you have the age.

Radiometric dating

Finding how long ago a rock or a crystal formed from the radioactive atoms in it. When it sets, it locks in radioactive atoms and, often, shuts out the atoms they decay into; from then on, the decayed atoms build up inside it at a rate set by the half-life, which nothing can change. Comparing how many of the decayed atoms there are with how many of the original ones are left gives the time since it set. Uranium, decaying into lead, dates the oldest rocks and grains in the Solar System.

There’s a check built in. Uranium comes in two isotopes, which decay into two different kinds of lead at very different rates: uranium-238 with a half-life of about four and a half billion years, and uranium-235 with one of about seven hundred million. In the same crystal, the two run side by side, like a slow clock and a fast one. If both give the same age, the crystal hasn’t lost or gained anything since it set, and the age can be trusted.

The Moon’s rocks have been dated by clocks like these, in laboratories all over the world. The pale crust of the highlands is about 4.4 billion years old, so the Moon itself is a little older still. The great basins were blasted out about 3.9 billion years ago, the Imbrium basin among them. The seas’ lavas brought back by Apollo set between about 3.9 and 3.1 billion years ago, and some came much later: the youngest yet dated, brought back by China’s Chang’e 5 in 2020 from the Ocean of Storms, set about two billion years ago.

Those dates are Albert’s thing in the cupboard whose date is known. Put the craters counted on each dated place against its age, and you learn how fast the craters came: very fast in the Moon’s first few hundred million years, and slowly ever since. (Whether the battering came in a late storm about 3.9 billion years ago, or was simply dying away, is still argued.) Then you can count the craters anywhere, on Mars or Mercury or the moons of Jupiter, and read off how old the ground is. The Moon is the Solar System’s calendar: close enough to have been sampled, and quiet enough to have kept its record, where the Earth’s oldest craters and rocks have nearly all been worn away or buried.

The oldest things there are

Albert reached into the cupboard, past the currant dough and the old leather gloves, and brought out a slice of stone about the size of his palm, polished on one face. It was dark grey, almost black, speckled all over with small round grains, and here and there with white flecks a few millimetres across.

“A meteorite,” he said, laying it on the table. “It fell in Mexico, in 1969, in a great many pieces, near a village called Allende. I have had this for some time.”

The Allende meteorite is a piece of an asteroid, one of the small bodies left over from the disc, and it has hardly changed since it was made. The white flecks are the oldest things in it: minerals rich in calcium and aluminium, the first to become solid as hot gas cools, at well over a thousand degrees. They condensed out of the hot gas close to the young Sun, before anything else in the Solar System was solid at all.

The two uranium clocks agree: such flecks formed 4,567 million years ago, within a few hundred thousand years of one another, and no solid made in the Solar System has been found older. That’s its birthday, as near as anyone can say. The Sun, already shining in the middle of the disc as the first grains formed, is the same age, give or take a million years or so, and that’s how anyone knows how long it has been shining: longer than the Moon by something like a hundred million years, and longer than any rock on the Earth has existed. The first to read the Solar System’s age from a meteorite was the American geochemist Clair Patterson, in 1956, with lead from Canyon Diablo, the iron meteorite whose fall made Meteor Crater. He got 4.55 billion years.

“So this,” said Cal, very carefully not touching the white flecks, “is older than the Earth.”

“A little,” said Albert. “Do not put it in your pocket.”

The flecks hold another clock, long since stopped. They took in a little aluminium-26, a radioactive kind with a half-life of only about 717,000 years, which decays into magnesium. Within a few million years the Solar System’s aluminium-26 was all gone, but its magnesium is still locked in the flecks, which is how anyone knows it was there. A half-life that short means it must have been made shortly before the flecks formed, within a million years or so, by a star nearby: perhaps a heavy star exploding, or perhaps one blowing off its outer layers in a wind before it exploded. That was the simulator’s second line, first thing in the morning. Whether a dying star’s blast actually pushed the Sun’s cloud into collapsing, or only seasoned it, nobody knows.

Other suns, other discs

Nobody can rewind the Sun’s own disc. But other stars are being born now, and their discs can be seen.

In 2014 the ALMA telescope, a great array of radio dishes on a high plateau in Chile, made a picture of the disc round HL Tauri, a young star about 450 light-years away, in Taurus, perhaps a million years old. Its disc of dust, a few times wider than Neptune’s orbit, is laid out in bright rings separated by dark gaps, far tidier than anyone had expected round a star so young. The likeliest explanation is that planets are forming in the gaps, sweeping up the dust in their paths. ALMA has since photographed dozens of such discs, many with rings and gaps of their own.

Back to Teide

The sun went down behind the western ridge, and a little later, low in the west, the Moon came out of the glow it left: a thin crescent, two days old, its bright edge turned towards where the Sun had set. Inside the crescent, the rest of the Moon showed faintly, grey and ghostly, its dark seas just visible.

“The old Moon in the new Moon’s arms,” said Albert. “Sailors used to say it meant a storm coming.”

“That’s earthlight,” said Cal. “We were there this morning.” He looked at it for a while. “It’s all still lying about up there, isn’t it. Four and a half billion years of it.”

“It is the only place it has been left alone,” said Albert.

John was looking at the crescent’s inner edge, where the Sun was rising on the Moon, the craters along it throwing their long shadows, far too small to see from the plateau.

The stars were coming out. Cal looked from the Moon to the ground under his feet, the plateau’s old black rock, still faintly warm from the day.

“It melted,” he said. “Something boiled its water off and left it short of iron. And it’s made of the same stuff as us.” He looked up at the crescent again, sinking towards the ridge. “What happened?”

“The worst day the Earth ever had,” said Albert. He stood and collected the cups. “The simulator can show it to you. In the morning.”
