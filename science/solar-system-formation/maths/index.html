How much faster a shrinking cloud turns

Why the turning is kept. A parcel of gas of mass mmm at position r\mathbf{r}r, moving with velocity v\mathbf{v}v, has angular momentum L=m r×v\mathbf{L} = m\,\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{v}L=mr×v. It changes only if something twists it:

dLdt=r×F.\frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}.dtdL​=r×F.

The pull of the mass at the middle points along r\mathbf{r}r, so r×F=0\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = 0r×F=0, and L\mathbf{L}L stays fixed. For gas going round the cloud’s axis, the part that matters is the component along the axis, which for each kilogram is

j=vφR=ΩR2,j = v_\varphi R = \Omega R^2,j=vφ​R=ΩR2,

its speed round the axis vφv_\varphivφ​ times its distance from the axis RRR, or its rate of turning Ω\OmegaΩ times R2R^2R2. Collisions between parcels pass angular momentum from one to another, but they can’t change the total. So if RRR halves, vφv_\varphivφ​ doubles and Ω\OmegaΩ quadruples.

How long it takes. Gas starting at rest at R0R_0R0​ and falling freely towards a mass MMM reaches the middle after the free-fall time

tff=π2R032GM=5.6×1012 s≈180,000 years.t_\text{ff} = \frac{\pi}{2} \sqrt{\frac{R_0^3}{2GM}} = 5.6 \times 10^{12}\ \text{s} \approx 180{,}000\ \text{years}.tff​=2π​2GMR03​​​=5.6×1012 s≈180,000 years.

Why a disc. Gas falling along the axis keeps its RRR, so its turning doesn’t speed up and nothing stops it reaching the middle plane. There, streams from above and below collide; their momenta along the axis are equal and opposite and cancel, and the energy goes into heat, which is radiated away. The total angular momentum about the axis, Lz=∑mRvφL_z = \sum m R v_\varphiLz​=∑mRvφ​, survives every collision. Of all the arrangements with a given LzL_zLz​, the one with the least energy is a thin disc of gas in circular orbits, all going round the same way, so that is where losing energy while keeping LzL_zLz​ must end.

How warm the disc was

Where ice lasts. In a near-vacuum there’s no liquid water: ice is solid as long as the water vapour round it is at a higher pressure than ice’s own vapour pressure, and turns to vapour otherwise. Ice’s vapour pressure falls very steeply as it cools, and at the tiny pressures of water vapour in the disc the crossing comes at about 150 to 170 K. Taking 170 K,

rfrost=(280 K170 K)2 AU=2.7 AU,r_\text{frost} = \left(\frac{280\ \text{K}}{170\ \text{K}}\right)^2\ \text{AU} = 2.7\ \text{AU},rfrost​=(170 K280 K​)2 AU=2.7 AU,

in the middle of today’s asteroid belt (about 2.2 to 3.3 AU).

More to build with. In Hayashi’s disc the solids inside the frost line, rock and metal, come to 7.1 grams on each square centimetre at the Earth’s distance, falling as r−3/2r^{-3/2}r−3/2; beyond it, with ice, 30 grams at the same scale, about 4 times as much. Reckonings from the Sun’s measured make-up (Lodders 2003) give a smaller jump, about 2. Hence “two to four times as much”.

A hotter start. While gas was still pouring through the disc onto the young Sun, the friction of that flow heated the inner disc far more than sunlight did, and the frost line lay further out. As the flow died away, it moved in; detailed models put it, at various times, anywhere from beyond 5 AU to as close as 1.

How hard things hit the Moon

The least it can be. Anything falling onto the Moon from far away arrives at least at the Moon’s escape speed,

vesc=2GMMoonRMoon=2×6.674×10−11×7.35×10221.737×106=2.4 km/s,v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M_\text{Moon}}{R_\text{Moon}}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 7.35 \times 10^{22}}{1.737 \times 10^{6}}} = 2.4\ \text{km/s},vesc​=RMoon​2GMMoon​​​=1.737×1062×6.674×10−11×7.35×1022​​=2.4 km/s,

and asteroids and comets, already moving round the Sun at tens of kilometres a second, usually arrive much faster: estimates of the average for asteroids range from about 13 to 22 km/s.

The energy in each kilogram. Its energy of motion for each kilogram is 12v2\tfrac{1}{2}v^221​v2:

12(20,000 m/s)2=2.0×108 J/kg,12(15,000 m/s)2=1.1×108 J/kg.\tfrac{1}{2}(20{,}000\ \text{m/s})^2 = 2.0 \times 10^8\ \text{J/kg}, \qquad \tfrac{1}{2}(15{,}000\ \text{m/s})^2 = 1.1 \times 10^8\ \text{J/kg}.21​(20,000 m/s)2=2.0×108 J/kg,21​(15,000 m/s)2=1.1×108 J/kg.

A kilogram of TNT releases 4.184×1064.184 \times 10^64.184×106 J, so each kilogram of the falling body carries the energy of about 27 to 48 kilograms of TNT.

For example. A stony asteroid a kilometre across, with a density of 2,600 kg m⁻³, has a mass of 43π(500 m)3×2600=1.4×1012\tfrac{4}{3}\pi (500\ \text{m})^3 \times 2600 = 1.4 \times 10^{12}34​π(500 m)3×2600=1.4×1012 kg; at 18 km/s it brings 12×1.4×1012×(1.8×104)2=2.2×1020\tfrac12 \times 1.4 \times 10^{12} \times (1.8 \times 10^4)^2 = 2.2 \times 10^{20}21​×1.4×1012×(1.8×104)2=2.2×1020 J, about 50,000 megatons. It makes a crater something like 15 to 20 kilometres across, many times its own size.

Why the craters are round. The body is stopped within a few times its own width, and its energy is released in a fraction of a second, very nearly at a point. The shock wave spreads from there evenly in every direction, much as from a buried explosion, so the crater is round. Experiments firing projectiles into targets at an angle (Gault and Wedekind, 1978) found craters elongated only for impacts within roughly 10 to 15 degrees of the ground, depending on the target, a small share of all impacts.

With so short a half-life, it must have been made within a million years or so of their forming.

The craters’ calendar. Counting craters at the places where rocks have been dated gives how the number of craters wider than a kilometre, on each square kilometre, grows with the age TTT of the surface (in billions of years). A fit widely used (Neukum, Ivanov and Hartmann, 2001) is

N(>1 km)=5.44×10−14(e6.93T−1)+8.38×10−4 T,N(>1\ \text{km}) = 5.44 \times 10^{-14} \left(e^{6.93 T} - 1\right) + 8.38 \times 10^{-4}\, T,N(>1 km)=5.44×10−14(e6.93T−1)+8.38×10−4T,

steep for surfaces older than about 3.5 billion years, when the battering was heavy, and nearly a straight line since. A surface 3.9 billion years old has about 0.033 such craters on each square kilometre, one a billion years old about 0.0008: forty times fewer, for a surface a quarter as old.
