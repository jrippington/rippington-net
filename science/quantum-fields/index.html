Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Field Guide to the Very Small, part 16 of 16

The grain, given back

In the morning, the plate of three doughnuts was still on the counter where Albert had left it, and so was the grain of sugar, on its saucer, under its glass. The napkin propped against the glass still said what it had said for fifteen journeys:

DO NOT EAT

Albert waited until John and Cal had their coffee. Then he lifted the glass, very carefully, as though the grain might go off, and slid the saucer along the counter to Cal.

“You asked what it was made of,” he said. “I think you are ready to have it back.”

Cal looked at the grain for a long time. It was the same grain, glittering and slightly crooked, that he had licked off his finger under the stars at the end of the last series, sixteen journeys ago if you counted this one.

“I know quite a lot about you now,” he told it.

He did. It was a crystal, its molecules stacked in rows, each molecule forty-five atoms of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen holding on to one another by sharing their outer electrons, as the eleventh journey found. Each atom was a tiny, heavy nucleus with electrons round it, nearly all empty space, as the first journey found. The electrons weren’t little planets. They were waves, held in by the nucleus’s pull, which could take only certain shapes and so could never fall in, as the eighth journey found, and no two of them could share a shape, which was why the grain was solid and his finger didn’t sink into it.

“Not quite all,” said Albert.

“What’s left?”

“What an electron is,” said Albert. “And a nucleus. You know what they do. You have never asked what they are.”

“Then let’s look,” said John.

Down through the grain

They took the grain out to The Van on its saucer, with the glass over it again for the journey across the car park, because a gust of wind on the terrace would have ended the series early. The dashboard was showing a kettle. Cal hit it in the place that usually worked, and the kettle went away.

John set the grain on the dashboard and asked the simulator to take them into it, as it had on the very first morning, and this time not to stop.

At a hundred times, the grain was a block of clear crystal, its faces flat and its edges sharp. At a million times, the simulator drew the sugar’s molecules, stacked in their rows like bricks in a wall, each one a knot of forty-five atoms. At ten million times it drew one molecule alone, the atoms joined where they shared their electrons. At a hundred million times, on the first morning, the screen had gone grey:

RESOLUTION NOT AVAILABLE. PLEASE CONSULT YOUR DEALER

This morning it didn’t. The simulator had evidently been consulting someone. It went on, into a single carbon atom, and drew what the eighth journey had found there: not orbits, but soft clouds, the shapes in which the atom’s six electrons could be found, two to a shape, filling the space round a centre too small to see.

Then it went into the centre. To get from the edge of the atom’s clouds to the nucleus, the simulator had to magnify ten thousand times more, and for a long time the screen showed nothing at all, the emptiness of the first journey, until at last a speck appeared in the middle and grew into a tight cluster of twelve small balls, six of one kind and six of another, packed together like a bunch of grapes.

NUCLEUS: CARBON. 6 PROTONS. 6 NEUTRONS

“Neutrons,” said Cal, who had met them in passing in the ninth journey, two to every alpha particle, and hadn’t given them a thought since.

A proton is the nucleus of hydrogen, the particle carrying the atom’s positive charge, which the first journey met. A neutron is almost exactly as heavy and carries no charge at all; James Chadwick found it in 1932. Every nucleus except the simplest hydrogen is a cluster of both.

And that was Cal’s puzzle from the evening of the eighth journey, which the ninth had only half answered, with a pull that acts when particles are almost touching. The protons in a nucleus are all positive, and two charges of the same kind push each other apart, harder the closer they are. In a nucleus they are packed closer than anything else in the universe, so close that the push between two of them is about the weight of a bag of shopping, on particles so light that a billion billion billion of them weigh about a kilogram and a half. Something must hold them together far more strongly than the electric push drives them apart.

The strong force

What holds the protons and neutrons of a nucleus together, against the electric push between the protons. It is by far the strongest force known: at the distances inside a nucleus it is about a hundred times stronger than the electric force. But it reaches only about as far as the width of a proton, so outside the nucleus it has no effect at all. Inside protons and neutrons it holds their quarks together, and it is so strong that a quark can never be pulled out on its own.

The simulator went on, into one of the protons, and showed three smaller things inside it, in a haze of something that never kept still.

PROTON: 3 QUARKS. AND A GREAT DEAL OF ACTIVITY

The idea of quarks came in 1964, from Murray Gell-Mann and, independently, George Zweig, to explain why there seemed to be so many kinds of heavy particle: hundreds of them could be sorted into neat families, if each were made of smaller pieces in different combinations. Gell-Mann took the name from James Joyce’s Finnegans Wake, in which a flock of seabirds cries “Three quarks for Muster Mark!” For four years nobody knew whether quarks were real things or a bookkeeping trick.

Then, in 1968, a team at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center, in California, did what Geiger and Marsden had done to the atom in the first journey. They threw things at the proton and watched what came back. Their projectiles were electrons, sped up to very nearly the speed of light along a straight tunnel two miles long, so that their waves were far shorter than a proton is wide, short enough to see inside it. If a proton’s charge were spread evenly through it, like Thomson’s pudding, the electrons should have passed through with only gentle nudges. Instead, far more of them than that came off at wide angles, kicked hard by something small and solid inside. It was Rutherford’s experiment again, one level down, and it found the quarks. Jerome Friedman, Henry Kendall and Richard Taylor shared the Nobel Prize for it in 1990.

Quark

One of the particles protons and neutrons are made of. A proton is two “up” quarks and one “down” quark; a neutron is one up and two downs. Up quarks carry two thirds of a proton’s charge and down quarks minus one third, so the proton’s add up to one and the neutron’s to none. Quarks are held together by the strong force so tightly that none has ever been seen on its own: pull one out, and the energy spent pulling makes new quarks, which pair up with it. There are six kinds of quark, but everything in the sugar is made of the two lightest.

No smaller pieces

“And inside the quarks?” said Cal.

John asked the simulator to go on. It thought for some time.

NO SMALLER PARTS FOUND

As far as anyone can tell, it’s right. Electrons and quarks have been probed at energies far beyond those of 1968, and if they have insides, nobody has seen them; an electron behaves as though it had no size at all. Cal’s question, asked under the stars sixteen journeys ago, had come to the end of its pieces. The grain is quarks and electrons, and that’s all.

“Then that’s the answer,” said John. “Quarks and electrons. Little balls, at the bottom.”

“Is it?” said Albert, who had come out with his coffee. “Then tell me something about your little balls. Every electron in that grain, and every electron in the universe, is exactly the same as every other. Not nearly the same. Exactly. You found that out in the eleventh journey.”

They had. No two of Albert’s doughnuts were alike, which was, on balance, a mercy. But every electron is exactly like every other: the same mass, the same charge, the same spin, to every decimal place anyone has ever measured, and so exactly alike that, as the eleventh journey found, swapping two of them changes nothing at all, and that is what keeps the atom’s electrons in their shells and the grain solid.

“Why?” said Albert. “Little balls made in different places, at different times, by different means. Why are they all perfect copies?”

Nobody had an answer.

“And something else,” said Albert. “In the third journey, you found that light arrives in lumps, photons. And on the day of the most expensive doughnut in the universe, you found that energy and mass are the same thing. Put those together, and particles ought to be able to appear out of energy, and vanish into it. Little balls don’t do that. A ball is a ball. You can’t make one out of a flash of light.”

“Can you?” said Cal.

“Physicists do it every day,” said Albert. “And hospitals do the opposite.”

That was three puzzles: every electron is identical; light comes in lumps; and particles can be made and unmade. If particles really were little separate balls, the three would be unrelated coincidences. There is a way of seeing them in which all three follow from one idea. It is the deepest answer physics has to Cal’s question.

What a field is

Start with something familiar. Out on the plateau, at every point, there is a temperature: warm by the café’s wall in the sun, cooler in the shadow of the rocks, colder still higher up the mountain. Take a thermometer anywhere and you get a number. A physicist would say there is a temperature field across the plateau: something with a value at every point in space. The wind is another, with a speed and a direction at every point. Neither is a thing in one place. Each is spread out over everywhere.

Fields can ripple. Drop a stone in a pond, and the height of the water’s surface, which is a field over the pond, ripples outwards. In Chasing a Beam of Light, John and Cal found that light is ripples in a field of exactly this kind, the electromagnetic field, which fills all of space: at every point there is an electric push and a magnetic one, waiting to act on any charge that comes by, and a ripple in them, a changing electric push making a changing magnetic one and back again, is light.

So far that is the physics of 1865, when James Clerk Maxwell worked it out. It says light is a smooth wave in a field. And it is half right, because in the third journey John and Cal found that light, when it arrives, always arrives in lumps.

Ripples that come in lumps

Put those two facts together, and you have to say something strange about the field: its ripples can’t take any size you like. They come in whole lumps.

This isn’t as odd as it sounds, because the eighth journey has already shown why it happens. A wave that’s held in, a guitar string fixed at both ends or an electron held in by a nucleus, can take only certain shapes, and so only certain energies. Each way the electromagnetic field can ripple, each wavelength in each direction, behaves like a little held-in wave of its own, and the quantum rules allow it to carry energy only in whole steps: one step, two steps, three, never two and a half. The size of each step was found in the second journey. It is Planck’s constant times the ripple’s frequency, bigger for blue light than red. One step of a ripple, travelling along, is a photon.

So a photon isn’t a little ball of light flying through the field. It is the smallest possible ripple in the field, one lump of it, and the lump moves along at the speed of light because that’s how fast the field’s ripples travel. Brightness is how many lumps; colour is how big each one is. The third journey’s discovery, light in lumps, is what you get when a field obeys the quantum rules.

Then physics took its boldest step. In the late 1920s and the 1930s, Pascual Jordan, Paul Dirac, Werner Heisenberg, Wolfgang Pauli and others asked whether the same could be true of everything. Perhaps an electron, too, is a lump of ripple, in a field of its own: an electron field, filling all of space just as the electromagnetic field does, invisible and silent wherever it isn’t rippling. Wherever it carries one lump of ripple, there is an electron. And the same for quarks, each kind with its field. The idea worked. Worked out fully, from the 1940s onwards, it is called quantum field theory, and it is the most precisely tested theory in all of science.

Quantum field

Something spread through all of space, with a value at every point, whose ripples, obeying the quantum rules, can only come in whole lumps. Each lump is a particle. The electromagnetic field’s lumps are photons; the electron field’s are electrons; each kind of quark has a field of its own. The fields are always there, everywhere, even in empty space; a particle is what a field is doing in one place, not a separate thing sitting in it.

The figure below draws a field in the simplest way there is: as a row of weights on springs, each joined to its neighbours, so that a push on one passes along the row as a ripple. (A real field isn’t made of springs, and it has a value at every point of space, not just along a row; the springs are only a picture of how its ripples travel.) In the old picture, you could give the row any amount of energy, as little as you liked. With the quantum rules, it takes energy only in whole lumps. Press the buttons to add a lump from the left or from the right, and watch each one travel along the row as a single particle; add two, and they pass straight through each other. Try to add half a lump, and the field refuses. The ledger counts the lumps and their energy. And look closely at the row when it carries no lumps at all.

Why every electron is the same

Now go back to Albert’s first puzzle, the eleventh journey’s: why is every electron exactly like every other?

Think about ripples on a pond. Drop two stones, and two rings of ripples spread out. Are they “the same”? In one sense, obviously: they’re both made of the pond’s water, rippling in the same way, and a ring made at one end of the pond can’t differ from a ring of the same size made at the other, because there is nothing to differ. A ripple doesn’t carry a label or a serial number. It has no material of its own. It is just the pond, doing something in one place.

That is why every electron is identical. They are not a crowd of separate little balls that happen to have been made alike. They are all ripples in one electron field, which fills the whole universe, and a lump of ripple in it here is exactly the same kind of thing as a lump of ripple there, in the way that one ring on the pond is the same kind of thing as another. The electrons in the grain of sugar, in Cal’s finger, and in a star on the far side of the universe are all the same field, doing the same thing in different places.

And it explains, too, why swapping two electrons changes nothing, which the eleventh journey needed. With two balls, you could always ask which one went where, even if you couldn’t tell them apart by looking. With two lumps of ripple in the same field, there is no “which one”. There are just two lumps. Swapping them isn’t a change that has been made too subtly to see; it isn’t a change at all.

“So the doughnuts are all different,” said Cal, “because they’re each made separately, by Albert.”

“With care,” said Albert.

“And the electrons are all the same because none of them is made separately. They’re all the same field.”

“That,” said Albert, “is very nearly the whole of it.”

A mirror for every particle

The third puzzle, that particles can be made from energy, has a history, and it begins with a man who almost never said anything he hadn’t thought through.

In 1928 Paul Dirac, in Cambridge, set out to write an equation for the electron obeying both the quantum rules and Einstein’s special relativity, the rules of the first seven journeys of the last series. He found one, and it was beautiful: it explained, without anyone putting it in by hand, why an electron has spin (the tenth journey) and the size of its magnetism. But it had a strange extra set of solutions, which seemed to describe electrons with less than no energy. Dirac worried at them for three years. In 1931 he proposed what they had to mean: there must be another particle, exactly like the electron, with the same mass and the same spin, but with the opposite charge. He called it an anti-electron. Nobody had ever seen one.

The next year, Carl Anderson, at the California Institute of Technology, found one without looking for it.

He was photographing cosmic rays, the fast particles that rain down on the Earth from space, in a cloud chamber. A cloud chamber is a glass box of air saturated with vapour, ready to condense, like the air behind a high aeroplane on a cold day. A charged particle flying through it knocks electrons off the molecules in its way, and droplets form along its path, so that it leaves a trail of mist, exactly as the aeroplane leaves its contrail. Anderson’s chamber sat between the poles of a powerful magnet, which curls the path of any charged particle passing through it: positive charges curl one way and negative charges the other, and faster particles curl less. To tell which way a particle was travelling, Anderson put a plate of lead six millimetres thick across the middle of the chamber. A particle loses some of its speed going through the lead, so it curls more tightly on the far side.

On 2 August 1932 he photographed a track that curled the wrong way. It came up from below, crossed the lead, and curled more tightly above it, so it was travelling upwards, and from the way it curled, it was positive. But it was far too light to be a proton: a proton curling like that would have been so slow that it stopped within a short distance, and this track went on more than ten times as far. It was a particle with the electron’s mass and the opposite charge: Dirac’s anti-electron. Anderson called it the positron, and won the Nobel Prize for it in 1936.

Antimatter

Matter made of antiparticles. Every kind of particle has an antiparticle with exactly the same mass and spin and the opposite charge: the electron’s is the positron, positive; the proton’s is the antiproton, negative. When a particle meets its antiparticle, the two can vanish together, and their whole energy, mass included, goes off as other particles or as light. Antimatter is made all the time, in cosmic rays, in the decay of some radioactive atoms and in particle accelerators, but it never lasts long here, because it soon meets ordinary matter.

Anderson’s photograph is one of the most famous in physics.

Energy into matter

A positron can also be made on purpose. Take a photon of gamma rays, very short light carrying a great deal of energy, and send it past an atom’s nucleus. If it has enough energy, it can vanish, and in its place an electron and a positron appear, flying apart. Energy has become matter.

How much is enough? The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe found that mass is energy, by E = mc²: an electron’s mass is a definite amount of energy, called its rest energy, about half a million electronvolts (the tiny unit of energy the eighth journey used for single atoms). To make an electron and a positron, the photon must supply both their rest energies, so it needs at least about a million electronvolts, 1.02 million to be exact. Below that, nothing can happen, however many photons you send. Above it, the photon’s spare energy goes into the pair’s motion, and the faster they fly apart. (The nucleus is needed only to take up a little of the photon’s push; a photon alone in empty space can’t turn into a pair and keep its momentum and energy accounts straight at once.)

And it runs backwards. When a positron meets an electron, the two vanish together, and their energy flies off as two gamma rays, each carrying one electron’s rest energy, in opposite directions. Albert said as much on the day of the most expensive doughnut, when he was explaining how little of a doughnut’s rest energy eating it sets free: “Only when matter meets antimatter does the whole of the rest energy come out.”

Hospitals use the reverse every day. In a PET scan, a patient is given a sugar, as it happens, labelled with a radioactive atom that gives out positrons. Wherever the body burns the most sugar, the most positrons are given out, and each one, within a millimetre or so, meets an electron and vanishes into two gamma rays flying off in opposite directions. A ring of detectors round the patient catches both, and the line between them points back to where the positron died. Thousands of such lines draw a map of where the body is busiest.

The figure below is a cloud chamber in a magnet, seen from above, with a single nucleus at its centre. A gamma ray comes in from the left; it leaves no trail, because it carries no charge, so it’s drawn dashed. Set its energy with the slider and fire it. Below about a million electronvolts, it passes the nucleus and goes on its way, and nothing happens. Above, it becomes an electron and a positron, which curl in opposite directions in the magnet, spiralling inwards as they slow down in the chamber’s gas. The more energy, the faster they go and the more gently they curl. The table keeps the energy accounts. Then bring a positron and an electron together and watch them go back into light.

That answers the third puzzle, and it closes the argument. Particles can be made from energy, and unmade into it. A little ball can’t be made out of light; it would have to come from somewhere. But a field, which is everywhere already, can start to ripple wherever it’s given the energy, and stop. A gamma ray passing a nucleus is the electromagnetic field handing one lump of its energy to the electron field, which takes it as two lumps, an electron and a positron, the only way it can take it. Three facts that would be coincidences if particles were little balls, that every electron is identical, that light comes in lumps, and that particles are made and unmade, are one fact if particles are lumps of ripple in fields.

Empty space isn’t empty

If every particle is a lump of ripple in a field, what is left when there are no lumps at all? Empty space: a vacuum, with no particles in it anywhere. It sounds like the one thing that ought to be simple. It isn’t.

The seventh journey found that nothing can be perfectly still, because a wave squeezed into a place must jiggle, and the eighth found that a held-in wave’s lowest level, though it can never be lost, isn’t zero. Each way a field can ripple is a held-in wave of exactly that kind. So even with no lumps in it at all, every field, everywhere, keeps its lowest jiggle, which can never be taken away. That is the restlessness you saw in the row of springs when it carried no lumps. Empty space isn’t nothing. It is every field there is, at its lowest, and still faintly trembling.

Can such a thing ever be detected? In 1948 the Dutch physicist Hendrik Casimir worked out that it could. Put two flat, uncharged metal plates very close together, facing each other in a vacuum. Between them, the electromagnetic field can only ripple in ways that fit the gap, as a guitar string can only play the notes that fit its length; outside, every way is allowed. With fewer ways to tremble between the plates than outside, the field’s jiggling outside pushes harder than the jiggling between, and the plates are pressed together, though nothing is there. The push is tiny unless the gap is very narrow, but it grows fast as the gap closes. With the plates a hundred-thousandth of a millimetre apart, it is about as strong as the pressure of the air.

In 1997 Steve Lamoreaux measured it, between a plate and a gently curved lens a few thousandths of a millimetre apart, and found the push to within about five per cent of Casimir’s prediction; others have since measured it far more precisely. (To be fair to the doubters, physicists still argue over how best to describe what the measurement shows: the same push can also be worked out as an attraction between the moving charges in the metal, without speaking of the vacuum at all. Both descriptions give the same number.)

Where the sugar’s weight comes from

Albert had been listening with his coffee on the dashboard. Now he reached for the grain’s saucer, very gently, and held it up.

“How much does it weigh?” he said.

“Almost nothing,” said Cal.

“About a fifth of a milligram,” said Albert. “And what is the weight made of?”

That is a question with a surprising answer. The obvious one is: its electrons, protons and neutrons, and inside those, its quarks, add up their masses and there you are. But the electrons are very light, almost two thousand times lighter than a proton, and between them they make up only about three hundredths of one per cent of the grain’s weight. So nearly all of it is in the nuclei, in the protons and neutrons, and so in their quarks.

And the quarks are light too. A proton’s three quarks, weighed by their own masses, come to about nine million electronvolts’ worth. A proton weighs about 938 million. The quarks’ own masses are about one per cent of the proton.

Where is the other ninety-nine per cent?

The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe found the answer to that, long before the quarks were found. Energy has mass. Albert’s box with a lamp at each end got lighter when it sent out light, and a box with light bouncing about inside it would be heavier than an empty one, by the light’s energy divided by the speed of light squared. A proton is a box of exactly that kind. Inside it, the three quarks are never still: squeezed into a space about a millionth of a billionth of a metre across, they move at nearly the speed of light, as the seventh journey’s squeezing says they must. And the field of the strong force between them, whose lumps are called gluons, because they glue the quarks together, holds an enormous energy of its own. That energy, the quarks’ motion and the gluon field’s, has mass. It is almost all of the proton’s mass.

So almost none of the sugar’s weight is the weight of anything in it. About one per cent is the quarks’ own masses, and a few hundredths of one per cent the electrons’. Nearly ninety-nine per cent is energy: the energy of quarks rushing about and of the field that holds them, weighing what E = mc² says it must. The same is true of the doughnut, the café, the mountain and John and Cal. Most of what you weigh is energy.

“So this doughnut,” said John, taking one from the plate Albert had brought out, “is ninety-nine per cent energy.”

“That would explain a lot,” said Cal, “about how you get up the observatory road.”

There is one more piece, and it’s often misunderstood. In 2012 the Large Hadron Collider at CERN found the Higgs boson, a lump of ripple in a field called the Higgs field, which fills all of space and gives the electrons and the quarks their own masses: without it, they would have none. It is sometimes said that the Higgs field gives everything its mass. For the sugar, it gives about one per cent: the quarks’ own masses and the electrons’. The other ninety-nine is the strong force’s energy, and the Higgs field has almost nothing to do with it.

What the fields don’t explain, yet

Quantum field theory is the most successful account of nature anyone has ever written down. One of its predictions, how strongly an electron behaves as a magnet, agrees with experiment to better than one part in a billion. Every particle anyone has made in any accelerator, and every one that has fallen from space, is a lump of one of its fields. But it doesn’t explain everything, and it stops in three places.

The first is gravity. Every other force is a field whose ripples come in lumps: light’s in photons, the strong force’s in gluons. Gravity, by the relativity series’ account, is the curving of spacetime itself, and nobody has yet managed to make spacetime obey the quantum rules. A theory that did would have to say what happens at the centre of a black hole, where One Way In found that general relativity runs out of answers, and what happened at the very beginning of the universe. Nobody has it yet, though a great many very clever people have spent their lives trying.

The second is matter nobody can see. Astronomers who weigh galaxies, by how fast their stars go round, find far more mass in them than all the stars and gas they can see. If that missing matter is made of particles, they are lumps of a field nobody has yet found. A later series comes back to it.

The third is why there is any sugar at all. Energy makes matter and antimatter in exact pairs: an electron with every positron, a quark with every antiquark. The early universe was a furnace of energy, so it should have made exactly as much antimatter as matter, and as it cooled the two should have met and vanished into light, leaving nothing to make stars, or planets, or sugar. Yet here is the sugar. Something, somewhere, favoured matter, very slightly, and nobody knows what. That too is for a later series.

Back to Teide

That night the sky over the plateau was as clear as it had been at the end of the last series, sixteen journeys before. The coaches had gone, the café was dark but for the light over the counter, and John and Cal sat on the terrace wall with Albert, looking up.

Cal had the grain of sugar on the saucer on his knee. He had tried to put it back under its glass, and Albert had told him it was his.

He looked from the grain to the stars, and for the first time they seemed to him to be made of the same thing.

The starlight falling on the terrace was lumps of the electromagnetic field, photons, each given out by an atom on a star dropping from one of its levels to a lower one, in the colours the eighth journey had found were every element’s fingerprint. The stars shone at all only because, in their cores, protons got close enough to fuse by leaking through a wall they couldn’t climb, the ninth journey’s tunnelling. And some of them, when they had burned out, would be held up for ever against their own weight by nothing but the exclusion principle of the eleventh journey, electrons that could not share. The grain in his hand was held together by the same rules. Its electrons were the same field as the electrons in the stars. Its weight was the same kind of energy as theirs.

“It’s all the same physics,” he said. “The sugar and the stars.”

“The same fields,” said Albert, “doing the same things, in different places.”

John was quiet for a long time, looking at the band of the Milky Way rising over the dark shape of the mountain.

“Then where did it all come from?” he said at last. “The protons in this grain, and the carbon, and the stars. Somebody had to make them.”

Albert smiled, the smile he kept for exactly the right question.

“That,” he said, “is not a question about the very small any more.”

Cal looked at the grain for a moment longer. Then he took the napkin with Albert’s three words on it, folded the grain into it, very carefully, and put it in his shirt pocket, with the words on the outside where he could see them.

“I’m keeping it,” he said.

“I would,” said Albert. “You will want it again.”

Out in the car park, The Van’s dashboard was showing a kettle.

They had spent sixteen journeys going down into a grain of sugar, through its crystal and its molecules and its atoms, into its nuclei and its quarks, to the fields at the bottom of everything. The next journeys go back: to the beginning of everything, and the long story of how this grain of sugar came to be.
