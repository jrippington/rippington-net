Cal wants to know what a grain of sugar is made of, and Albert has kept the grain under a glass. By the end of the day John and Cal have fired particles at gold, found that atoms are almost entirely empty, and worked out, to their alarm, that by every rule physics had, the sugar shouldn't be there at all.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Field Guide to the Very Small, part 1 of 16

The grain under the glass

Albert had kept the grain of sugar.

It sat on a saucer at the end of the counter, beside the till, under an upturned glass. Propped against the glass was a paper napkin with three words on it in Albert’s handwriting:

DO NOT EAT

It was early, and the plateau was still cold. The first coaches of the day hadn’t yet laboured up from the coast, and the sun was still low over the eastern ridge, so that long shadows lay across the old lava and the café’s windows looked out on a landscape of blue and grey. Inside, it was warm, and it smelled of coffee and of the first batch of lemon doughnuts, which Albert was lifting out of the fryer and rolling, one at a time, in a tray of lemon sugar.

John and Cal came in, sat at their usual table by the window, and looked at the glass.

“You kept it,” said Cal.

“You asked a question about it,” said Albert. “It seemed rude to throw it away.”

The night before, under a sky full of stars, Cal had licked the last of the lemon sugar from his finger and looked at a single grain of it, glittering in the light from the café window. They had spent fourteen journeys finding out what space and time really are, and how the universe has grown. “What about the other way?” Cal had asked. “What is this actually made of?” Albert had taken the grain from him, very carefully, and told them to come back in the morning.

“All right,” said John. “It’s sugar.”

“Made of?” said Albert.

“Smaller bits of sugar.”

“And those?”

“Even smaller bits of sugar.” John reached for a doughnut. “I could show you. Cut one of these in half, then in half again, and keep going until…”

Albert moved the plate out of his reach. “Not my doughnuts,” he said. “That is what the grain is for.”

Too small to see

John’s experiment is one of the oldest in thinking, even if nobody has ever been able to do it. Take a grain of sugar and cut it in half. Cut one of the halves in half, and keep going. Can you go on for ever, or do you come, eventually, to a piece too small to cut?

In the fifth century BC two Greek philosophers, Leucippus and his pupil Democritus, argued that you must come to an end: everything is made of tiny pieces that can’t be divided, which they called atomos, “uncuttable”. It was a guess, though a good one, and for two thousand years nobody could test it.

The evidence came from chemists. Around 1800 they noticed that when substances combine, they always do so in fixed proportions. Water is always eight parts oxygen to one part hydrogen by weight, never seven or nine, however you make it. In 1808 the English chemist John Dalton explained it: every substance is made of tiny atoms, each kind of substance of its own combination of them, joined in fixed numbers. Some substances can’t be broken down into anything simpler at all, and those are the elements.

Chemical element

A substance that can’t be broken down into anything simpler by heating it, dissolving it or any other chemical means, because it is made of only one kind of atom. Hydrogen, carbon, oxygen, iron and gold are elements. Everything else, from water to a lemon doughnut, is made of elements combined. About ninety occur naturally on Earth.

Albert, who has burnt more sugar than most people, can show you which elements are in it. Heat sugar too long in a pan and it goes brown, then black, and steam comes off it. The black crust left in the pan is carbon. The steam is water, which is hydrogen and oxygen. Every molecule of sugar is a cluster of 45 atoms: twelve of carbon, twenty-two of hydrogen and eleven of oxygen.

Atom

The smallest piece of an element that is still that element: the smallest piece of carbon that is still carbon. Atoms join together into molecules: a molecule of sugar is 45 atoms joined together. They are far too small to see. Despite the Greek name, atoms can be divided: as this journey finds, each is a tiny, heavy centre, its nucleus, with even lighter particles, electrons, somewhere round it.

And there are a great many of them. A grain of sugar is about half a millimetre across, and it contains about sixteen billion billion atoms. If you counted them, one every second, you would still be counting after thirty-five times the age of the universe.

“Then let’s look at one,” said John.

They went out to The Van, which sat in its usual corner of the car park looking, as ever, as though it had been assembled from several other vehicles in a hurry. Its speedometer read 17 mph. Nobody remarked on it any more.

The Van’s simulator lived on the dashboard screen, which was showing, for reasons nobody had ever discovered, a picture of a kettle. Cal hit the screen firmly in the place that usually worked, and the kettle went away. John set the simulator to show them the grain of sugar, then to magnify it.

At a hundred times, the grain was a clear glassy block, with flat faces and sharp edges: a crystal. At ten thousand times, its faces were stepped, like a hillside of terraces. At a million times, the simulator drew the sugar’s molecules as clusters of coloured balls joined by little sticks, stacked in neat rows.

“Is that what they look like?” said Cal.

“That is what the simulator thinks you would like them to look like,” said Albert, who had followed them out with his coffee.

At a hundred million times, the screen went to a soft grey blur.

RESOLUTION NOT AVAILABLE. PLEASE CONSULT YOUR DEALER

For once, the dealer could have done nothing. Light, as John and Cal found in Chasing a Beam of Light, is a wave, and you can’t see anything much smaller than its waves. The waves of visible light are about five thousand times longer than an atom is wide. Trying to see an atom with light is like trying to feel the shape of a single peppercorn while wearing Albert’s oven gloves. Nobody has ever seen an atom with light, and nobody ever will. (Today there are microscopes that feel their way across a surface and make pictures of single atoms. How they do it is a story for the ninth journey.)

“Then how does anyone know what’s inside one?” said John.

Albert finished his coffee. “You throw things at it,” he said, “and watch what comes back.”

Throwing things at atoms

To know what to throw, and what to expect, you need to know a little of what an atom was thought to be made of in 1909.

Twelve years earlier, in 1897, J. J. Thomson in Cambridge had found a particle far lighter than any atom: about one eighteen-hundredth of the lightest, hydrogen. It came out of every kind of matter he tried, and it carried a negative electric charge. It was the electron, and it meant that atoms weren’t uncuttable after all. They had parts.

If you remember televisions with a deep glass back, you have watched one of Thomson’s experiments for hours. Inside the glass was a tube with the air pumped out of it. At its narrow end, a hot wire boiled electrons out of its metal, and at the wide end, they struck the screen, whose coating glowed where they hit. Magnets round the neck of the tube steered the beam back and forth across the screen, line by line, painting the picture. Thomson’s tube was a cruder version, and what he measured was how much his beam bent in a magnet: enough to show that its particles were far lighter than any atom.

Electric charge

A property some particles have, which makes them push or pull on each other. It comes in two kinds, positive and negative: two charges of the same kind push each other apart, and two of opposite kinds pull together. The push or pull grows quickly as they get closer: twice as close, four times as strong. Electrons carry negative charge. Everyday matter has almost exactly as much positive charge as negative, so the two cancel, and you don’t notice; rub a balloon on your jumper and you move a few electrons, and the balloon lifts your hair.

Ordinary matter doesn’t push or pull on things electrically, so an atom must carry exactly as much positive charge as its electrons carry negative. Where was it? Thomson’s guess, in 1904, was that it was spread evenly through the whole atom, like a dough, with the electrons dotted through it like currants.

Albert, it turned out, still had the tray of currant dough that had once stood in for the expanding universe. He fetched it from the café and set it on The Van’s dashboard.

“Is that the universe?” said Cal.

“Today,” said Albert, “it is an atom.”

Thomson’s picture became known as the “plum pudding” atom, after the Christmas pudding, though Albert’s dough did just as well. Its positive charge was spread out, and its electrons were tiny and very light. That mattered for what happened next.

In 1909, at the University of Manchester, Ernest Rutherford had just the projectile to throw. Radium, the radioactive metal, constantly shoots out particles of its own, which Rutherford had named alpha particles ten years before. They are tiny, but more than seven thousand times heavier than an electron. They carry positive charge. (He had shown that same year that they turn into helium once they pick up some electrons.) And they leave the radium at about fifteen thousand kilometres a second, a twentieth of the speed of light.

Fire alpha particles at a thin sheet of metal, and what should happen, if atoms are plum puddings? An alpha particle passing through an atom feels the pudding’s positive charge pushing it away from every side at once, and the pushes from different sides largely cancel. The electrons hardly count: an alpha particle hitting an electron is like a bowling ball hitting a ping-pong ball, and carries on almost as if nothing had happened. So each alpha particle should go straight through the metal, nudged a little this way and that, and come out the far side very nearly on course.

John wanted to see it for himself, and the simulator could do better than look. It could rebuild an experiment. He asked for Rutherford’s laboratory, and the screen filled with a small brass apparatus. On the left, a speck of radium sat inside a lead box with a narrow slot in it, so that its alpha particles came out in a thin beam. In the middle stood a sheet of gold leaf, far thinner than any paper. All round it was a ring of screen coated with zinc sulphide, a chemical that gives a tiny flash of light whenever an alpha particle hits it: one particle, one flash. A microscope could be swung round the ring to watch for flashes at any angle.

The beam went on, and the flashes came, nearly all of them straight ahead, a little spread out, just as the pudding said they should. The Van counted them.

STRAIGHT THROUGH: 12,406. TURNED BACK: 0

John watched the numbers for a while.

“This is the most boring experiment in the history of physics,” he said.

“Geiger and Marsden counted every one of these by eye,” said Cal, “through a microscope, for hours.”

“Who?”

“Wait,” said Cal.

STRAIGHT THROUGH: 31,877. TURNED BACK: 1

Somewhere in the gold, one alpha particle had come back the way it came, as if it had hit a wall.

John put his coffee down.

What can turn a particle round?

That is what happened in Manchester in 1909. Hans Geiger, Rutherford’s assistant, and Ernest Marsden, an undergraduate of twenty, had been firing alpha particles at thin metal foils and counting where they went, flash by flash, through a microscope. Rutherford suggested Marsden look for any that came back towards the source. He found some. From platinum, about one alpha particle in eight thousand came back. Even a layer of gold less than a thousandth of a millimetre thick could turn them right round. Years later Rutherford remembered it as the most incredible thing that had ever happened to him: “as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.”

Why was it so incredible? Think about what it takes to turn something round.

To send a tennis ball back the way it came, you need two things. First, something it can’t simply push out of the way: a wall works, because a wall has the whole Earth behind it, but a balloon doesn’t. Second, a push strong enough to stop the ball and send it back in the short time it’s in contact. An alpha particle is far heavier than an electron and moving at a twentieth of the speed of light. To stop it and throw it back, the atom needs something much heavier than it, and an enormous push.

The push, here, is electric. The alpha particle is positive, and so is the atom’s positive charge, so the two push each other apart, and the push grows quickly as they get closer. That’s why a spread-out positive charge can never turn an alpha particle round. The particle can never get close to more than a small part of it, because the rest is spread all round. Once it’s inside the pudding, there’s positive charge on every side of it, pushing in different directions, and the pushes mostly cancel. At the very middle, they cancel completely. The most a pudding atom can do is give a passing alpha particle a nudge of less than a hundredth of a degree.

Could many nudges add up to a U-turn? A sheet of gold leaf is a few thousand atoms thick, so each alpha particle gets thousands of nudges on its way through. But the nudges come in random directions, this way, that way, up, down, and they mostly cancel too. Added together, they come to a degree or two at most, and the chance of thousands of random nudges all lining up to send a particle back is so small that you could fire alpha particles at gold for the age of the universe and never see it happen.

Now squeeze the same positive charge into a tiny point at the centre of the atom. Most alpha particles still pass nowhere near it and fly straight on: the atom is nearly all empty space, and the electrons are no obstacle. But an alpha particle aimed almost exactly at the point gets closer and closer to all of that charge at once, and the push grows and grows until it stops the particle dead and flings it back. It’s like a ball rolled at a hill with no top, whose slope gets steeper the higher it climbs: roll it straight at the middle and, however fast it’s going, it climbs, slows, stops and rolls back; aim it a little to one side and it’s turned off along the slope. Only the few alpha particles heading almost straight at the centre come back. That’s why so few did: the target is tiny.

And the point must be heavy. If it were light, the alpha particle would knock it out of the way, as the bowling ball knocks the ping-pong ball, and carry on.

So the bounce-backs leave no choice. Something in the atom can stop an alpha particle and send it back, so its positive charge must be packed into a tiny space, far smaller than the atom. It must be far heavier than the alpha particle, so nearly all the atom’s mass is there too. And since nearly everything goes straight through, the rest of the atom is almost entirely empty. In 1911 Rutherford worked out exactly how many alpha particles a tiny, heavy, positive centre should turn through each angle, and in 1913 Geiger and Marsden’s careful counts fitted his numbers. The centre became known as the nucleus.

An atom the size of the car park

How small is the nucleus? Rutherford’s counts said it was thousands of times smaller than the atom at least, and later measurements showed it is tens of thousands of times smaller.

Take the simplest atom, hydrogen, of which every molecule of sugar has twenty-two. Its nucleus is a single particle, called a proton, carrying one unit of positive charge, with one electron somewhere round it. Imagine the nucleus enlarged to the size of the grain of sugar under Albert’s glass. Then the atom’s edge would be about sixteen metres away: out through the café door, across the terrace, past The Van. Everything between the grain and the edge is empty, apart from one electron. And nearly all the atom’s mass, 99.95 per cent of it, is in the grain.

So the sugar is almost entirely empty space. So is the doughnut, and the table, and John.

“So this doughnut,” said John, “is mostly nothing.”

“That would explain a lot about its shape,” said Cal.

Albert gave him a look.

“Then why,” said John, pressing his hand down on the table, “can’t I put my hand straight through this?”

“An excellent question,” said Albert. “Keep it. You will need it later.” (He was right. It comes back in the eleventh journey.)

What does the electron do, out there in all that space? Rutherford’s picture was the obvious one, and it is still the one most people imagine when they think of an atom: the electron goes round the nucleus as a planet goes round the Sun. The Sun’s gravity holds the Earth in orbit; the nucleus’s electric pull holds the electron in orbit. Heavy centre, light things going round, mostly empty space: an atom is a tiny solar system.

It is a beautiful picture. And it can’t possibly be right.

An atom that can’t last

Cal noticed first. He had borrowed a napkin from Albert, who always seemed to have one, and drawn the planet-style atom on it: a dot for the nucleus, a circle for the electron’s orbit. Then he stopped, with the pen still on the circle.

“It’s going round in a circle,” he said.

“Yes,” said John.

“Going round in a circle is accelerating.”

He was right, and it is the whole problem. In The Day the Mountain Moved, John and Cal found that a physicist’s acceleration is any change in velocity, and velocity is speed in a particular direction. A car going round a roundabout at a steady speed is accelerating, because its direction keeps changing. So is an electron going round a nucleus, all the time.

Teleport to the Relativity moonThe Day the Mountain MovedAcceleration, velocity, and frames that move steadily

Why does that matter? Because of how light is made.

In Chasing a Beam of Light they found that light is a wave of electric and magnetic forces: a changing electric force makes a magnetic one, and a changing magnetic force makes an electric one, so that the two keep each other going and travel off through space. To start such a wave, you need something to make the electric force change. That is exactly what a radio mast does. A transmitter pushes electrons up and down the mast, millions of times a second. The electric push they exert on the space round them keeps changing, and the change spreads out from the mast as a radio wave. The wave carries energy away with it: that’s why a transmitter has to be fed with electricity, and why a radio, a long way off, can pick some of that energy up.

Teleport to the Relativity moonChasing a Beam of LightLight as a wave of electric and magnetic forces

Now ask what kind of motion makes such a wave. An electron sitting still makes none: its electric push on the space round it is steady, and nothing changes. What about an electron gliding steadily along in a straight line? Here the very first journey settles it. The principle of relativity says the laws of physics work in exactly the same way in every frame that moves steadily. Seen from a frame moving along with the electron, it is sitting still, and makes no wave; so it can’t be making one in any other steadily moving frame either. A steadily moving electron is just a still electron, seen by someone moving past it.

So the only thing that makes waves is a change in an electron’s motion: acceleration. Hold one end of a long rope and stand still, or walk steadily along with it, and nothing runs down the rope. Shake your hand from side to side, changing its motion, and waves run away from you. A radio mast shakes its electrons. And an electron going round a nucleus is changing direction every instant. It is accelerating all the time, so it must be sending out waves all the time.

Follow that through, one step at a time:

  • The waves carry energy away, and the energy can only come from the electron’s motion.
  • An electron with less energy can’t stay as far out, so it sinks closer to the nucleus.
  • Closer in, the pull is stronger, the electron goes round faster, and it accelerates harder, so it sends out waves faster still.
  • So it loses energy faster, and sinks faster: it spirals in.

How quickly? The rules of electricity and magnetism, the ones every radio station on Earth runs by, give a definite answer. A planet-style hydrogen atom would collapse in about sixteen trillionths of a second. In that time, light travels less than half a centimetre: across the grain of sugar about ten times. On its way in, the electron would go round about two hundred thousand times, sending out a rising whistle of light, climbing from ultraviolet into X-rays as it fell.

That is how long every atom in the universe should last. The sugar, the doughnut, the café, the mountain, John and Cal should all have collapsed in that flash, long ago.

“Show us,” said John.

Cal set the simulator to build a planet-style hydrogen atom, by the rules of 1911, and to slow everything down a million million times, so that its collapse would take fifteen seconds. The screen showed a single electron circling a single nucleus, round and round, then a little faster, then faster still, its circle shrinking, the light it gave off rising in pitch, until the circle closed up in a white flash, and nothing was left.

“Run it again,” said John.

Cal ran it again. The electron spiralled in again.

Then switch to a real hydrogen atom, which does something quite different: it stays exactly the size it is, and when it gives out light at all, it gives out only a few sharp colours.

The sugar is still there

Is there a way out? The conclusion is alarming enough to look hard for one.

Perhaps the electron doesn’t move at all? Then nothing would stop the nucleus’s pull from drawing it straight in, and the atom would collapse even faster. Any motion that keeps it from falling in, it turns out, means going round or swinging to and fro, and both are acceleration. Perhaps the electron gives out much less light than the rules say? But the rules are among the best tested in physics. They were worked out from experiments with jars of charge and coils of wire, and they predicted the speed of light from those alone; they are the rules every radio, every electric motor and every mobile phone is designed by. In 1911 nobody had seen them fail.

So by every rule physics had, there can be no such thing as a lasting atom. And yet there the sugar was, under Albert’s glass, made of atoms that had lasted, so far as anyone could tell, for billions of years.

There was a second clue. When atoms do give out light, they don’t give out a rising whistle of every colour. Heat hydrogen gas, or pass electricity through it, and it glows with just a few sharp colours: a deep red, a blue-green, and two violets, always the same ones, in the same places. Every element has its own set. Nothing in the planet-style atom explains why.

Two clues, then. Atoms last, when the rules say they can’t. And they give out only certain colours, when the rules say they should give out every one. Either something is very strange about atoms, or the rules themselves need to change for things as small as this. It took the best physicists of the next twenty years to find out which, and the answer was stranger than either. The rest of this series follows it, one experiment at a time.

Back to Teide

By evening the coaches had gone back down to the coast, and the plateau was quiet again. The sun was low, and the old lava had turned gold.

Albert lit the gas ring behind the counter to warm some milk. Then, with the air of a man doing a conjuring trick, he took a pinch of salt from the pot by the till and dropped it into the blue flame.

The flame flared yellow. Not orange, not white: a clear, pure yellow, and only yellow.

“Ask yourself,” said Albert, “why salt makes it that colour, and only that colour.”

“Sodium,” said John. “Salt has sodium in it.”

“That is a name,” said Albert. “I asked why.”

Nobody had an answer. Cal looked at the grain of sugar, still sitting under its glass, still not collapsed.

“It shouldn’t be there,” he said.

“By every rule we had,” said Albert.

“Then the rules are wrong,” said John.

Albert took the milk off the heat. “In 1900,” he said, “a very distinguished physicist, Lord Kelvin, gave a lecture in London. He said the beauty and clearness of physics was obscured by just two clouds. The first was the ether, the stuff light was supposed to travel through.”

“We know how that ended,” said Cal. They had spent fourteen journeys finding out.

“And the second?” said John.

“A rule for how heat shares itself out among things that jiggle about. It didn’t work, and nobody knew why.” Albert glanced at the fryer, cooling in the corner. “Come back in the morning. We will start with that one.”

Out in the car park, the simulator’s screen still glowed faintly through The Van’s windscreen.

ATOM NOT FOUND

It was wrong, of course. The atoms were all there. Nobody yet knew how.
