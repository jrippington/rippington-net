Swapping identical particles

Two particles, one at x1x_1x1​ and one at x2x_2x2​, are described by a single wave ψ(x1,x2)\psi(x_1, x_2)ψ(x1​,x2​) (each xxx standing for a place and a spin), and the chance of finding them at a pair of places is ∣ψ(x1,x2)∣2|\psi(x_1, x_2)|^2∣ψ(x1​,x2​)∣2. If the particles are identical, swapping them describes the same arrangement, so every chance must be unchanged:

∣ψ(x2,x1)∣2=∣ψ(x1,x2)∣2⇒ψ(x2,x1)=eiφ ψ(x1,x2).|\psi(x_2, x_1)|^2 = |\psi(x_1, x_2)|^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \psi(x_2, x_1) = e^{i\varphi}\,\psi(x_1, x_2).∣ψ(x2​,x1​)∣2=∣ψ(x1​,x2​)∣2⇒ψ(x2​,x1​)=eiφψ(x1​,x2​).

The swap can only turn the arrow, by some angle φ\varphiφ. Swapping twice is no change at all, so e2iφ=1e^{2i\varphi} = 1e2iφ=1, and

eiφ=+1oreiφ=−1:e^{i\varphi} = +1 \quad\text{or}\quad e^{i\varphi} = -1:eiφ=+1oreiφ=−1:

the arrow is left alone, or turned through half a turn. (In three dimensions of space these are the only two; in a flat, two-dimensional world there are others.)

Fermions take −1-1−1. With one particle in a pattern aaa and one in a pattern bbb,

ψ(x1,x2)=12[a(x1) b(x2)−b(x1) a(x2)],\psi(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\big[a(x_1)\,b(x_2) - b(x_1)\,a(x_2)\big],ψ(x1​,x2​)=2​1​[a(x1​)b(x2​)−b(x1​)a(x2​)],

which does turn round under a swap. If a=ba = ba=b, the two terms cancel and ψ=0\psi = 0ψ=0 everywhere: no two fermions in the same state. That’s the exclusion principle.

Bosons take +1+1+1:

ψ(x1,x2)=12[a(x1) b(x2)+b(x1) a(x2)].\psi(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\big[a(x_1)\,b(x_2) + b(x_1)\,a(x_2)\big].ψ(x1​,x2​)=2​1​[a(x1​)b(x2​)+b(x1​)a(x2​)].

At a single place, x1=x2=xx_1 = x_2 = xx1​=x2​=x, this gives ∣ψ∣2=2 ∣a(x)∣2∣b(x)∣2|\psi|^2 = 2\,|a(x)|^2|b(x)|^2∣ψ∣2=2∣a(x)∣2∣b(x)∣2, twice the chance for two particles that could be told apart, and for fermions, zero: bosons are more likely to be found together, fermions never.

Spin. Each xxx includes the particle’s spin. Two electrons in the same pattern can still make a wave that turns round under a swap if their spins do the turning: one up and one down, in the combination 12(↑↓−↓↑)\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\uparrow\downarrow - \downarrow\uparrow)2​1​(↑↓−↓↑). A third electron has no spin left to differ in. So each pattern holds two.

Which particles are which. Pauli proved in 1940, from relativity and quantum mechanics together, that particles of half-integer spin (the electron’s spin one half among them) are fermions, and particles of whole-number spin (the photon’s, spin one) are bosons.

Filling the shells

The hydrogen atom’s patterns at level nnn come in nnn families, with l=0,1,…,n−1l = 0, 1, \dots, n-1l=0,1,…,n−1, and family lll has 2l+12l + 12l+1 orientations, so level nnn has

∑l=0n−1(2l+1)=n2\sum_{l=0}^{n-1} (2l + 1) = n^2l=0∑n−1​(2l+1)=n2

patterns: 1, 4, 9, 16. With two electrons to each, the levels have room for 2n22n^22n2: 2, 8, 18, 32.

In an atom with many electrons, the inner ones screen the nucleus’s pull from the outer ones, and patterns of the same nnn but larger lll, which keep further from the nucleus, lie higher. The order of filling becomes, very nearly, by n+ln + ln+l and then by nnn:

1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, …1s,\ 2s,\ 2p,\ 3s,\ 3p,\ 4s,\ 3d,\ 4p,\ 5s,\ 4d,\ 5p,\ 6s,\ \dots1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, …

(s,p,ds, p, ds,p,d for l=0,1,2l = 0, 1, 2l=0,1,2), which gives the table’s rows their lengths: 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32. Within a family the electrons take a pattern each, with the same spin, before they pair (Hund’s rule). Chromium (3d5 4s13d^5\,4s^13d54s1) and copper (3d10 4s13d^{10}\,4s^13d104s1) are the two exceptions among the first 36, where an electron moves from 4s4s4s to 3d3d3d.

Why matter doesn’t collapse

In outline only: the proofs run to many pages.

Take NNN electrons and NNN nuclei, all held together by electric forces. Squeezing them into a smaller space lowers their electric energy but raises their energy of motion. For fermions, the energy of motion rises steeply with the number packed in, as N5/3/R2N^{5/3}/R^2N5/3/R2 for NNN electrons in a region of size RRR. Dyson and Lenard (1967, 1968) proved that this always wins in the end: the lowest energy the whole collection can have is no lower than a fixed amount times NNN,

Elowest≥−C N,E_\text{lowest} \ge -C\,N,Elowest​≥−CN,

so every extra atom adds a fixed amount of binding and a fixed amount of room, and matter has a size proportional to how much of it there is. (Lieb and Thirring, 1975, gave a short proof with a far better CCC.)

For bosons, Dyson showed in 1967 that the lowest energy falls at least as fast as

Elowest≤−C′ N7/5.E_\text{lowest} \le -C'\,N^{7/5}.Elowest​≤−C′N7/5.

Then the binding per particle grows without limit as the lump grows, and bringing two lumps of NNN together releases at least C′N7/5(27/5−2)≈0.64 C′N7/5C'N^{7/5}(2^{7/5} - 2) \approx 0.64\,C'N^{7/5}C′N7/5(27/5−2)≈0.64C′N7/5, more and more the bigger they are: bosonic matter would fall in on itself, the more of it the harder.

Stimulated emission

Einstein’s argument of 1916 and 1917. Atoms with two levels, N1N_1N1​ in the lower and N2N_2N2​ in the upper, energy hfhfhf apart, sit in light of frequency fff whose energy per unit volume and frequency is uuu. Three things can happen: an atom absorbs (rate B u N1B\,u\,N_1BuN1​), an excited atom gives out a photon by itself (rate A N2A\,N_2AN2​), or the light prompts it to (rate B′ u N2B'\,u\,N_2B′uN2​). In equilibrium at temperature TTT, N2/N1=e−hf/kTN_2/N_1 = e^{-hf/kT}N2​/N1​=e−hf/kT, and the rates balance:

B u N1=(A+B′u) N2⇒u=A/B′(B/B′) ehf/kT−1.B\,u\,N_1 = (A + B'u)\,N_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad u = \frac{A/B'}{(B/B')\,e^{hf/kT} - 1}.BuN1​=(A+B′u)N2​⇒u=(B/B′)ehf/kT−1A/B′​.

For this to be Planck’s law, u=8πhf3c31ehf/kT−1u = \dfrac{8\pi h f^3}{c^3}\dfrac{1}{e^{hf/kT} - 1}u=c38πhf3​ehf/kT−11​, at every temperature, Einstein needed

B′=B,AB=8πhf3c3.B' = B, \qquad \frac{A}{B} = \frac{8\pi h f^3}{c^3}.B′=B,BA​=c38πhf3​.

Without the prompted term B′B'B′, the formula comes out wrong: stimulated emission is required. In the later language of bosons, the rate at which an atom gives out a photon into a state already holding nnn photons goes as n+1n + 1n+1: the 1 is the photon it would give out anyway, and the nnn is the crowd pulling it in. A laser keeps more atoms in the upper level than the lower (in ruby, by flashing it with a lamp), so that prompted emission outruns absorption and the crowd grows: Maiman’s ruby laser of 1960 gave out deep red light at 694 nanometres.
