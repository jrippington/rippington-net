A question kept for later

John had been waiting ten journeys to ask it, and he asked it before the coffee arrived.

“On the first morning,” he said, “you told me to keep a question. Why can’t I put my hand through this table?”

He pressed his hand flat on the wooden top, as he had then. It was still there, on top of the table, where tables keep hands.

“You found out that day,” said Albert, “that the atoms of your hand and the atoms of the table are almost entirely empty.”

“If the nucleus were the grain of sugar,” said Cal, nodding at the glass on the counter, “the atom’s edge would be out past The Van.”

The grain was still there, under its upturned glass, with its napkin propped against it, still saying what it had said on the first morning. It had sat there for ten journeys, a crystal of sugar about half a millimetre across, flat-faced and sharp-edged, keeping its shape exactly. Its molecules hadn’t fallen in on one another. Its atoms hadn’t collapsed. It hadn’t gone soft, or spread out across the saucer, or shrunk.

“So that’s two questions,” said John. “Why doesn’t my hand go through the table, when both of them are nearly all empty space? And why does the sugar hold together, and keep its shape, instead of slumping into a puddle or squashing into a dot?”

“Last night,” said Albert, “I told you the answer was spin’s two answers.” He went to fetch the morning’s doughnuts. “Today you find out why.”

He put the plate down between them. There were six doughnuts on it, and, as always, no two were alike. One was a little taller than the others, one a little lopsided, one had more lemon sugar on one side than the other, and one had a shape that Cal looked at, and then didn’t. You could have told any of them from any other with your eyes shut, which was, on balance, a mercy.

“You could tell those apart in the dark,” said Cal.

“You could,” said Albert. “You could not tell two electrons apart if your life depended on it. That is the whole of today.”

Why electricity isn’t enough

The obvious answer to John’s question is electricity, and it’s half right.

The outside of every atom is its electrons, and electrons are all negative, so the electrons of John’s hand and the electrons of the table push each other apart. Press harder, and they push back harder. That’s what John feels in his palm.

But there’s a difficulty, and it shows the moment you look at a whole atom rather than its electrons alone. An atom is neutral: its nucleus carries exactly as much positive charge as its electrons carry negative. From outside, the two cancel. So why should two atoms push each other apart at all? As two atoms come together, each electron is pushed by the other atom’s electrons, but it is also pulled by the other atom’s nucleus. Let the electrons rearrange themselves a little, each sitting a little nearer to both nuclei, and the pulls can win. By electricity alone, it isn’t at all obvious why atoms shouldn’t simply merge, two clouds of electrons sinking into one around two nuclei, and then three, and then all of them, the whole table falling in on itself.

The eighth journey found what stops a single atom collapsing: its electron is a wave held in, and squeezing it closer to the nucleus makes it jiggle harder, until the squeeze and the jiggle balance. That gives a single atom its size. Does it also give the table its size? You might think so. In fact, it doesn’t, and the reason took until 1967 to prove.

In 1931, in a speech awarding Wolfgang Pauli a medal, the physicist Paul Ehrenfest asked his audience to think about a piece of metal, or a stone, and to be astonished that so little matter should take up so much room. His answer was one line: only the rule Pauli had found, that no two electrons can be in the same state. Thirty-six years later Freeman Dyson and Andrew Lenard proved him right, in a long and fearsomely difficult piece of mathematics. Without that rule, electric forces and the jiggle of the eighth journey are not enough. Matter would collapse: not just each atom into a smaller one, but every lump of matter into a dense knot, and the more matter there was, the more violently it would fall in, releasing more energy the bigger the lump. Dyson worked out that, in such a world, bringing two ordinary objects together would release energy on the scale of a bomb. (Elliott Lieb and Walter Thirring found a much shorter proof in 1975.)

So the table holds John’s hand up, and the sugar holds its shape, because of a rule about electrons that has nothing to do with electricity. It comes from electrons being all exactly alike.

Exactly alike

Albert’s doughnuts are all different, and so is everything else you have ever handled. Two coins from the same mint, two grains of sugar from the same bag, two snowflakes, two cars off the same production line: look closely enough and there’s always something, a scratch, a speck, a slightly different number of atoms, that tells one from the other. Even if there weren’t, you could keep track of them, watching one all the time, so that you always knew which was which.

Electrons aren’t like that. Every electron in the universe has exactly the same mass, exactly the same charge and exactly the same spin, measured to as many decimal places as anyone has ever managed. There’s no scratch, no label, no serial number, nothing at all to tell one from another. And you can’t keep track of them by watching, either: as the sixth journey found, watching an electron closely enough to follow its path changes what it does, and as the seventh found, it never had a sharp path to follow in the first place. Two electrons whose waves overlap can’t be told apart even in principle.

That sounds like a mere fact about labels. It turns out to decide almost everything.

Take two electrons, and two places for them to be, here and there. If they were doughnuts, there would be two different ways of having one in each place: this doughnut here and that one there, or that one here and this one there. You could tell which you had, by looking. For electrons, “this one here and that one there” and “that one here and this one there” are not two different arrangements. There’s nothing to tell them apart. They are the same arrangement, described twice.

So swap two electrons, and nothing can change. Every chance of finding anything anywhere must come out exactly as it was. Remember the fifth journey’s arrow, a little clock hand for every way a thing can happen, whose size sets the chance. A swap that changes nothing can’t change the arrow’s size. Could it turn the arrow round, though, without changing its size?

This is the step that settles it. Swap the two electrons twice, and you’re exactly back where you started, so the arrow must be exactly as it was, size and direction both. So whatever one swap does to the arrow, doing it twice must give no change at all. A swap could leave the arrow alone, and twice is still alone. Or a swap could turn the arrow through half a turn, and twice is a whole turn, back where it began. A quarter of a turn wouldn’t do, because twice would be half a turn, and so on: only those two are possible. Nature, it turns out, uses both.

For electrons, a swap turns the arrow through half a turn.

Now the consequence. Suppose two electrons were in exactly the same state: the same pattern, in the same place, with the same answer to spin. Then swapping them changes nothing at all, not even the description; it’s exactly the same arrangement. So the swap must leave the arrow exactly as it is. But a swap turns an electron pair’s arrow round. The only arrow that’s the same turned round as it was before is an arrow of no size at all. And an arrow of no size means no chance. Two electrons in the same state have no chance of being found. They can’t be.

Is there a way out? You’d have to say that electrons can, after all, be told apart, by some hidden mark; and then they’d behave like doughnuts, and atoms, as you’ll see, would be nothing like the atoms of the real world. Or you’d have to say that swapping can do something other than leave the arrow alone or turn it round, and twice would then not bring it back. Neither way out is open. No two electrons ever share a state.

Wolfgang Pauli found the rule in 1925, before anyone knew the reason. He was twenty-four, and puzzling over the way atoms’ sharp colours split into several when the atoms sit in a magnet. To make the patterns come out right, he had to give each electron one more label besides the ones that fix its pattern, a label with just two possible values, and then insist that no two electrons in an atom share all their labels. He had no idea what the two-valued label was. It was spin: Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit’s idea, later that year, was an attempt to explain Pauli’s two values. The reason for the rule itself, the turning arrow, came in 1926.

Pauli exclusion principle

No two electrons can ever be in exactly the same state: the same pattern, as the eighth journey’s held-in waves, and the same answer to spin. The same goes for protons and neutrons. It follows from their being exactly alike: swapping two of them turns their joint arrow round, so two in the same state would cancel to nothing. Since spin has two answers, each pattern can hold two electrons, one up and one down, and no more. Wolfgang Pauli found it in 1925.

Two kinds of particle

What about the other possibility, a swap leaving the arrow alone? Nature uses that too, for other particles, and every kind of particle there is belongs to one family or the other.

Electrons, protons and neutrons are of the turning-round kind. Photons, the lumps of light, are of the other: swap two photons and their arrow is left alone. For them, two in the same state don’t cancel. Their two arrows add, as the fifth journey’s arrows add along two ways that end in the same place, and the chance of finding two photons in the same state comes out larger than it would for two particles you could tell apart. Photons don’t merely tolerate company. They prefer it.

Fermions and bosons

The two families every kind of particle belongs to. Fermions, among them electrons, protons and neutrons, can never share a state: swapping two of them turns their joint arrow round. Bosons, among them photons, can share, and are more likely to crowd into the same state than particles you could tell apart. The names honour Enrico Fermi and Satyendra Nath Bose, who worked out how crowds of each kind behave. Which family a particle belongs to is tied to its spin: every particle whose spin has the halved angles of the last journey’s electron is a fermion.

Bose, a young physicist in Dhaka, worked out how to count photons as particles that can’t be told apart, in 1924. He sent his paper to Einstein in Berlin, who translated it into German himself and sent it off for publication. Fermi, in Florence, and Paul Dirac, in Cambridge, worked out the electrons’ kind in 1926. Why a particle’s spin decides its family took until 1940, when Pauli proved it, and the proof needs relativity as well as quantum mechanics: a rule from both halves of these journeys at once.

Two to a pattern

Now go back to the eighth journey’s atom. Its electron is a wave held in by the nucleus, and a held-in wave can take only certain patterns, each with its own energy. Hydrogen has one electron, and it sits in the lowest pattern, the round cloud closest in.

Give the nucleus a second electron, as helium has, and it can go into the same lowest pattern too, provided its spin gives the other answer. One up, one down: two different states, though they share a pattern. A third electron, though, has nowhere to go in that pattern. Both of spin’s answers are taken.

John tried it in the simulator, building a helium atom and then adding one more electron, aimed at the lowest pattern.

The third electron had to go into the next pattern up, further out and higher in energy. That’s lithium. And so it goes on, one electron after another, each taking the lowest place not yet full: two in each pattern, one up and one down, and then on up to the next.

The patterns come in families. At the lowest energy there’s only one pattern, the round cloud, with room for two electrons. At the next level up there are four, one round and three shaped like dumbbells pointing in three directions at right angles, with room for eight. At the next, nine, with room for eighteen. Electrons with about the same energy, and about the same distance from the nucleus, make what chemists call a shell.

Electron shell

A group of an atom’s electrons with about the same energy, at about the same distance from the nucleus. Because no two electrons can share a state, each shell has room for only so many: two in the first, eight in the second, and more further out. An atom fills its shells from the inside, and its outermost shell, the one partly filled, decides how it behaves with other atoms.

Since each pattern holds two, the shells fill up in a definite order, and an atom with a given number of electrons always ends up with the same number in its outer shell. That is the whole secret of the chemist’s table of the elements.

Mendeleev’s table

In 1869, in St Petersburg, the chemist Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the sixty-three elements then known in order of the weights of their atoms, and noticed that their behaviour came round in a cycle. Lithium, sodium and potassium are all soft, silvery metals that fizz and react violently in water. Fluorine, chlorine and bromine are all fierce, choking, eager to react with almost anything. So he laid the elements out in rows, so that each column held elements that behave alike. Where the pattern called for an element nobody had found, he left a gap, and predicted what it would be like: how heavy, how dense, what it would combine with. Within seventeen years, three of his gaps had been filled: gallium in 1875, scandium in 1879 and germanium in 1886, each very close to his predictions.

Mendeleev had no idea why the pattern was there. Nobody did, for more than half a century. The answer is the exclusion principle.

Every element in one column of the table has the same number of electrons in its outer shell. Neon, at the end of the second row, has its outer shell exactly full, eight electrons, nothing to give and no room to take, and it reacts with nothing at all. Argon and krypton, below it, are almost as aloof. Sodium, at the start of the third row, has one electron alone in a new shell, far out and loosely held, and gives it away to almost anything. Lithium and potassium, above and below it, are the same. Chlorine is one short of a full shell, and takes an electron from anything that will give one. Put sodium and chlorine together and sodium’s spare electron goes straight across, leaving two atoms each with a full shell, now charged, holding on to each other: common salt. The salt Albert dropped into the gas flame on the first evening is exactly this, and its yellow comes from that same lonely outer electron, lifted by the flame’s heat and dropping back.

Holding hands

Most atoms aren’t full, and they don’t stay alone. A hydrogen atom, with one electron in a shell that has room for two, and an oxygen atom, with six in a shell that has room for eight, can both do better by sharing.

Bring two hydrogen atoms close to an oxygen atom, and each hydrogen’s electron can pair up with one of oxygen’s unpaired ones, the two of them, one up and one down, spread across the gap between the two atoms, belonging to both. Each pair counts towards both atoms’ shells at once. The hydrogens now each have their two, and the oxygen, with two extra shared electrons, has its eight. The shared pair, sitting between the two nuclei, pulls both of them towards it and so holds them together. That’s water: two hydrogens and an oxygen, held by two shared pairs.

Chemical bond

What holds atoms together into molecules and solids. Most often it is a pair of electrons, one up and one down, shared between two atoms, filling both atoms’ outer shells and holding the two nuclei together. Sometimes one atom gives an electron to another outright, and the two, now oppositely charged, hold on to each other, as in common salt. The exclusion principle sets how many bonds each atom makes: hydrogen one, oxygen two, carbon four.

Here the exclusion principle is doing the bookkeeping twice over. It decides how many places are left in each atom’s outer shell, so how many bonds the atom makes. And it decides that a bond is a pair: two electrons can share the space between two atoms only if they give opposite answers to spin. A third can’t join them.

Carbon has four electrons in a shell with room for eight, so it shares four, and makes four bonds, to four neighbours at once. That’s why carbon can be the backbone of a long chain or a ring, with other atoms hanging off it, and why it’s the backbone of nearly every molecule in living things. Including the sugar.

The grain of sugar under Albert’s glass is a crystal of molecules of sucrose, each made of the 45 atoms the first journey counted: twelve carbon, twenty-two hydrogen and eleven oxygen. The drawing below is one molecule, every atom and every bond. Two rings, one of five carbons and an oxygen and one of four carbons and an oxygen, are joined by a single oxygen between them, and hydrogens and oxygen-hydrogen pairs hang off every carbon. Count the bonds at each atom: four at every carbon, two at every oxygen, one at every hydrogen, without exception. There are 46 bonds, each one a pair of electrons, one up and one down. In the crystal, the molecules stack in neat rows, held to their neighbours more weakly, mostly by the pull between one molecule’s oxygen-hydrogen pairs and the next one’s oxygens, which is why sugar dissolves in water, whose molecules can pull in the same way.

Why the floor holds you up

Now John’s hand.

Press two atoms together, and their electron clouds start to overlap. If electrons were doughnuts, that would be no trouble: the electrons of one atom could simply settle into the same low patterns as the other’s, and the two atoms would sink into one another, which is what Dyson and Lenard’s mathematics says would happen to everything. But the low patterns are full. Every place in them is taken, two to a pattern, one up and one down. An electron of John’s hand can’t move into a place where an electron of the table already is. To let the atoms overlap, some of the electrons would have to be pushed up into higher patterns, with more energy, and that energy has to come from somewhere: from the push of John’s hand. The harder he pushes, the more electrons would have to climb, and the harder they push back.

That’s the push John feels. It isn’t that his atoms are solid balls bumping into the table’s. It’s that the places his electrons would need are already occupied, and the only free ones are uphill.

The figure below is the simplest place to see it: a box of particles, whose walls you can squeeze. Held in, each particle’s wave can only take the shapes that fit the box, as the eighth journey’s string could only play certain notes, so the box has a ladder of levels, each a little higher in energy than the last, and squeezing the box pushes every rung up. Start with ten electrons. No two can share a state, so they stack two to a rung, one up and one down, five rungs high. Now switch to bosons, particles that can share. All ten of them sit on the bottom rung, and their energy is far smaller: ten electrons have eleven times the energy of ten bosons, and push on the walls eleven times as hard. Squeeze the walls and watch the levels climb: halve the width, and every rung’s energy goes up four times. Add more electrons, and they have to go higher still, so their push grows much faster than their number, while the bosons’ only keeps pace.

For the mathematically inclinedThe push of a squeezed box

That’s the push of every floor. In The Apple That Fell in a Straight Line, John and Cal found that the floor of the café is being pushed up all the time, off the path an apple would follow if it were let go, by the rock beneath it, and that what you feel as your weight is the floor accelerating up to meet you. But what is the rock doing, at bottom, to push? Its electrons are refusing to share their places with the electrons of the floor above, which are refusing to share with the soles of your shoes, which are refusing to share with your feet. All the way down into the Earth, the push that holds you up is, at bottom, the exclusion principle.

“So I never actually touch anything,” said John, looking at his hand on the table.

“You touch it as much as anything ever touches anything,” said Albert. “Which is to say, your electrons and its electrons get as close as they can without sharing, and then they stop.”

Matter’s solidity, then, is a matter of space being taken. The same rule decides how much room a lump of matter needs: the more electrons, the more places they must fill, and the more room they take up. That’s why a kilogram of anything ordinary, iron or sugar or rock, takes up roughly the space it does, and why it can be squeezed only a little, however hard you press.

Only a little: but not without limit. A star, at the end of its life, can be squeezed by its own enormous weight until its atoms are crushed together and its electrons are packed as tightly as the exclusion principle allows. Then nothing holds it up but electrons refusing to share their places, and some dying stars are held up by exactly that, for billions of years. A later series comes back to them.

Photons like company

Electrons keep their distance. Photons, the bosons, do the opposite, and there’s a machine in most homes that shows it.

An atom with an electron in a higher pattern than it needs, an excited atom, will sooner or later drop it to a lower one and give out a photon, as the eighth journey found: one sharp colour, the difference between the two patterns. Left alone, it does that whenever it happens to, in any direction. But if a photon of exactly that colour comes past while the atom is waiting, the atom can be prompted to give out its photon there and then, and the new photon is a perfect copy of the one that came past: the same colour, the same direction, its waves exactly in step. Now there are two identical photons going the same way, and they can prompt two more atoms, and those make four. Because photons are bosons, they don’t just tolerate being in the same state; the more of them there are in a state, the more likely another is to join them.

Einstein worked this out in 1916 and 1917, while thinking about how atoms and light share out heat, and called it stimulated emission.

“I wrote a short paper about that once,” said Albert, to nobody in particular, and went to see about the coffee machine.

For nearly forty years stimulated emission was a curiosity, until in 1954 Charles Townes and his students used it to amplify microwaves. Then on 16 May 1960, at the Hughes Research Laboratories in Malibu, California, Theodore Maiman put a rod of ruby inside a coiled flash lamp. The flash lifted the ruby’s atoms into a higher pattern; a few dropped back and gave out photons, which ran up and down the rod between mirrors at its ends, prompting more and more atoms to give out copies; and out of one end came a beam of deep red light, every photon of it the same. It was the first laser: the letters stand for light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation.

The laser pointer in the fourth journey, the one that threw a row of spots from a hair across Albert’s kitchen wall, is a crowd of photons all in step, every one in the same state, there because photons like company. So are the beams reading barcodes at supermarket tills, and those carrying most of the world’s telephone calls and internet traffic along glass fibres. A laser beam and the table under John’s hand are the two families of particle doing what each does: one crowding together, the other refusing to.

Back to Teide

By evening a wind had got up on the plateau, and the café was nearly empty. John had his hand on the table again, pressing, as if to check that it was still being refused.

“Two answers to spin,” he said. “And electrons that can’t be told apart. And from those two things, you get atoms that don’t fall in, and the periodic table, and chemistry, and salt, and sugar, and floors.”

“And a table that holds up your coffee,” said Cal.

“Like The Van,” said John. “Room for only two.”

“It has three seats,” said Cal.

“One of them,” said John, “is the kettle’s.”

Under its glass on the counter, the grain of sugar held its shape, as it had for ten journeys: three hundred and fifty million billion molecules, each one forty-five atoms holding hands in pairs of electrons, each pair one up and one down, none of them able to move into a place another already held.

Albert had gone into the back of the café. They heard him rummaging in the cupboard where he kept his props, and he came out with something small and complicated: a crystal in a brass holder, a little lamp, and two short tubes pointing away from each other.

“Electrons,” he said, “insist on keeping apart. Photons like to share. Tomorrow, out on the terrace, I should like to show you a pair of photons that share rather more than a colour.”
