When the glow went out of sight

The oldest light’s temperature grows with redshift, zzz, in step with the stretching of its waves:

T(z)=T0 (1+z),T0=2.7255 K.T(z) = T_0\,(1 + z), \qquad T_0 = 2.7255\ \text{K}.T(z)=T0​(1+z),T0​=2.7255 K.

A hot body’s glow fades from sight, even to an eye used to the dark, at about 525 °C, about 800 K (Draper’s point, from Red Hot, White Hot). The glow reached it when

1+z=800 K2.7255 K≈293.1 + z = \frac{800\ \text{K}}{2.7255\ \text{K}} \approx 293.1+z=2.7255 K800 K​≈293.

How old was the universe then? While matter rules the expansion, the age at redshift zzz is close to

t(z)≈23H0Ωm (1+z)−3/2,t(z) \approx \frac{2}{3 H_0 \sqrt{\Omega_\text{m}}}\,(1 + z)^{-3/2},t(z)≈3H0​Ωm​​2​(1+z)−3/2,

and with Planck’s values, 1/H0=14.51/H_0 = 14.51/H0​=14.5 billion years and Ωm=0.315\Omega_\text{m} = 0.315Ωm​=0.315, the factor in front is 17.2 billion years, so

t≈17.2×109 years2933/2≈3.4 million years.t \approx \frac{17.2 \times 10^{9}\ \text{years}}{293^{3/2}} \approx 3.4\ \text{million years}.t≈2933/217.2×109 years​≈3.4 million years.

A star’s balance

At every depth, the push of the gas must hold up the weight of everything above it. For a thin layer of thickness drdrdr at distance rrr from the centre, where the gas has density ρ\rhoρ and the mass inside is m(r)m(r)m(r):

dPdr=−G m(r) ρ(r)r2.\frac{dP}{dr} = -\frac{G\, m(r)\, \rho(r)}{r^2}.drdP​=−r2Gm(r)ρ(r)​.

The air. On the Earth, m(r)/r2m(r)/r^2m(r)/r2 is just g/Gg/Gg/G, and the pressure at sea level is the weight of the whole column of air above each square metre:

P0=101,325 Pa⇒P0g≈10,300 kg on every square metre,P_0 = 101{,}325\ \text{Pa} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{P_0}{g} \approx 10{,}300\ \text{kg on every square metre},P0​=101,325 Pa⇒gP0​​≈10,300 kg on every square metre,

about ten tonnes. If the air were all at one temperature, the balance would make its pressure fall off as P=P0 e−h/HP = P_0\, e^{-h/H}P=P0​e−h/H, with H=kT/(mˉg)≈8.4H = kT/(\bar m g) \approx 8.4H=kT/(mˉg)≈8.4 km. At 2,100 metres, e−0.25≈0.78e^{-0.25} \approx 0.78e−0.25≈0.78: about four fifths of the push at the coast, so a bag sealed at the coast swells.

A star. A rough version of the balance for a whole star of mass MMM and radius RRR takes dP/dr≈−Pc/RdP/dr \approx -P_\text{c}/RdP/dr≈−Pc​/R, m≈Mm \approx Mm≈M and ρ≈M/R3\rho \approx M/R^3ρ≈M/R3:

Pc∼GM2R4.P_\text{c} \sim \frac{G M^2}{R^4}.Pc​∼R4GM2​.

The gas in the middle pushes as an ideal gas does, P=ρkT/(μmH)P = \rho k T / (\mu m_\text{H})P=ρkT/(μmH​), where μ\muμ is the average weight of its particles in hydrogen atoms (about 0.6 for hot, stripped hydrogen and helium). Putting the two together,

kTc∼GMμmHR.k T_\text{c} \sim \frac{G M \mu m_\text{H}}{R}.kTc​∼RGMμmH​​.

For the Sun this gives

Tc∼6.67×10−11×1.99×1030×0.6×1.67×10−271.38×10−23×6.96×108≈1.4×107 K,T_\text{c} \sim \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.99 \times 10^{30} \times 0.6 \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27}}{1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 6.96 \times 10^{8}} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{7}\ \text{K},Tc​∼1.38×10−23×6.96×1086.67×10−11×1.99×1030×0.6×1.67×10−27​≈1.4×107 K,

close to the 15.7 million degrees of detailed models. The weight decides how hot the middle has to be.

How big a clump must be to fall in

A clump of gas falls in on itself only if its weight beats its push, which it does above the Jeans mass (James Jeans, 1902):

MJ=π5/26 cs3G3/2ρ1/2,cs=kTμmH,M_\text{J} = \frac{\pi^{5/2}}{6}\, \frac{c_\text{s}^3}{G^{3/2} \rho^{1/2}}, \qquad c_\text{s} = \sqrt{\frac{kT}{\mu m_\text{H}}},MJ​=6π5/2​G3/2ρ1/2cs3​​,cs​=μmH​kT​​,

where csc_\text{s}cs​ is the speed of sound in the gas. The first gas, cooled by its few hydrogen molecules, stalled at about T=200T = 200T=200 K at about 10410^4104 hydrogen nuclei in a cubic centimetre (Bromm, Coppi and Larson 2002; Abel, Bryan and Norman 2002). With hydrogen 76 per cent of it by weight and μ≈1.22\mu \approx 1.22μ≈1.22 (mostly atoms, with helium),

That is “about a thousand times the Sun’s mass” (definitions of the Jeans mass differ by factors of a few). Such a clump is about 2.8 light-years in radius, and falls in, if nothing stops it, in a free-fall time

tff=3π32Gρ≈1.4×1013 s≈450,000 years.t_\text{ff} = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32 G \rho}} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{13}\ \text{s} \approx 450{,}000\ \text{years}.tff​=32Gρ3π​​≈1.4×1013 s≈450,000 years.

Since MJ∝T3/2ρ−1/2M_\text{J} \propto T^{3/2} \rho^{-1/2}MJ​∝T3/2ρ−1/2, gas twenty times colder needs a clump 203/2≈9020^{3/2} \approx 90203/2≈90 times smaller at the same density, which is why dusty clouds today can make small stars (the eighth journey’s maths page has their numbers).

Why the first gas couldn’t cool. Lifting a hydrogen atom’s electron to its first step up costs ΔE=10.2\Delta E = 10.2ΔE=10.2 eV, so ΔE/k≈118,000\Delta E / k \approx 118{,}000ΔE/k≈118,000 K. In gas at 3,000 K the share of bumps with that much energy goes roughly as e−118,000/3,000=e−39≈10−17e^{-118{,}000/3{,}000} = e^{-39} \approx 10^{-17}e−118,000/3,000=e−39≈10−17, and the atom can’t glow. A hydrogen molecule’s lowest step of spin, from its second spin level to its lowest, is 0.0440.0440.044 eV (ΔE/k≈510\Delta E/k \approx 510ΔE/k≈510 K), given out as infrared light 28 micrometres long: about 230 times smaller than the atom’s step, and within reach of a gas at a few hundred degrees.

Hotter by losing heat

For a ball of ideal gas held up by its own heat, the balance leads to the virial theorem: the energy of the particles’ motion, KKK, and the energy of the ball’s gravity, Ω\OmegaΩ (negative, because work would be needed to pull the ball apart), are tied together:

2K+Ω=0.2K + \Omega = 0.2K+Ω=0.

So the ball’s whole energy is

E=K+Ω=−K.E = K + \Omega = -K.E=K+Ω=−K.

When it shines for a time Δt\Delta tΔt with brightness LLL, it loses energy L ΔtL\, \Delta tLΔt:

ΔE=−L Δt⇒ΔK=+L Δt,ΔΩ=−2L Δt.\Delta E = -L\, \Delta t \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta K = +L\, \Delta t, \qquad \Delta \Omega = -2 L\, \Delta t.ΔE=−LΔt⇒ΔK=+LΔt,ΔΩ=−2LΔt.

Its gravity’s energy falls by twice what it shines away (it shrinks), and its heat rises by the same amount it shone away: half the energy set free by the shrinking leaves as light and half heats the gas. Losing heat makes it hotter. Since Ω≈−GM2/R\Omega \approx -GM^2/RΩ≈−GM2/R, the time it takes to shrink a good way is about

tKH∼GM2RL,t_\text{KH} \sim \frac{G M^2}{R L},tKH​∼RLGM2​,

the Kelvin-Helmholtz time: for the Sun’s mass, radius and brightness, 3.1×1073.1 \times 10^{7}3.1×107 years, about thirty million years; for a first star of a hundred Suns, about five times the Sun’s radius and 1.3 million times as bright (Schaerer 2002), only about fifty thousand.

Lighting. The proton chain that powers the Sun runs at a rate that rises as about T4T^4T4 near fifteen million degrees, and it sets in, in earnest, at about ten million: then its heat replaces what the surface loses, and the shrinking stops. The heaviest first stars needed more. Light pushes too, and in a star of mass MMM the share of the push that is the gas’s, β\betaβ, falls as the mass rises (Eddington’s quartic, 1−β≈0.003 μ4M2β41 - \beta \approx 0.003\, \mu^4 M^2 \beta^41−β≈0.003μ4M2β4, with MMM in Suns: about 0.6 at a hundred Suns). Such a star is enormously bright, and the proton chain, rising only as T4T^4T4, couldn’t keep pace until the middle was about a hundred million degrees. There, three helium nuclei could at last make a carbon one, now and then, as the eleventh journey explains; a trace of carbon, about one part in ten billion by mass, is enough to let hydrogen burn by carbon’s faster route, whose rate rises as about T18T^{18}T18, and that stops the shrinking, at about 111 to 1.5×1081.5 \times 10^81.5×108 K (Marigo et al. 2001; Bromm, Kudritzki and Loeb 2001).

The smallest star

Squeezed close enough, electrons push back without needing heat, because no two can share a state (Room for Only Two). Their push grows with their number in a cubic metre, nen_\text{e}ne​, as

Pe=Ke ne5/3,Ke=(3π2)2/35 ℏ2me.P_\text{e} = K_\text{e}\, n_\text{e}^{5/3}, \qquad K_\text{e} = \frac{(3\pi^2)^{2/3}}{5}\, \frac{\hbar^2}{m_\text{e}}.Pe​=Ke​ne5/3​,Ke​=5(3π2)2/3​me​ℏ2​.

Add it to the gas’s push in the rough balance above, with ne≈ρ/(μemH)n_\text{e} \approx \rho/(\mu_\text{e} m_\text{H})ne​≈ρ/(μe​mH​) and μe≈1.14\mu_\text{e} \approx 1.14μe​≈1.14 for the first gas:

kTc∼GMμmHR−Ke μmH(μemH)5/3 M2/3R2.k T_\text{c} \sim \frac{G M \mu m_\text{H}}{R} - \frac{K_\text{e}\, \mu m_\text{H}}{(\mu_\text{e} m_\text{H})^{5/3}}\, \frac{M^{2/3}}{R^2}.kTc​∼RGMμmH​​−(μe​mH​)5/3Ke​μmH​​R2M2/3​.

The first term grows as the ball shrinks; the second, the electrons’ share, grows faster. So the middle’s temperature rises, reaches a peak, and falls again, as the electrons take more and more of the weight. The peak is at

Rpeak=2KeG(μemH)5/3 M−1/3,kTmax=G2μmH(μemH)5/34Ke M4/3.R_\text{peak} = \frac{2 K_\text{e}}{G (\mu_\text{e} m_\text{H})^{5/3}}\, M^{-1/3}, \qquad k T_\text{max} = \frac{G^2 \mu m_\text{H} (\mu_\text{e} m_\text{H})^{5/3}}{4 K_\text{e}}\, M^{4/3}.Rpeak​=G(μe​mH​)5/32Ke​​M−1/3,kTmax​=4Ke​G2μmH​(μe​mH​)5/3​M4/3.

A heavier ball peaks hotter, as M4/3M^{4/3}M4/3, so there is a mass below which the peak falls short of fusion. This rough sum puts the peak of a ball of 0.08 Suns at about 9×1069 \times 10^69×106 K, too high, since it treats the whole star as its middle. Careful models of the star’s layers put the dividing line at about 0.075 of the Sun’s mass for today’s gas, and about 0.09 for the first gas, which has no heavier atoms to hold its heat in (Saumon et al. 1994; Burrows et al. 1993): about 80 to 95 times Jupiter’s mass, or a twelfth of the Sun’s, give or take. Below it, the ball stalls at about the radius where the electrons hold it, about Jupiter’s, and cools for ever: a brown dwarf. Teide 1, of about 55 Jupiters, has never reached the 2.5 million degrees at which lithium burns, which is how its light shows it to be one.

The bubbles’ growth

A first star’s light. The 21-centimetre line, stretched

Hydrogen’s 21-centimetre note has a frequency of 1,420.4 MHz. Stretched by the expansion from redshift zzz, it arrives at

f=1420.4 MHz1+z,λ=21.1 cm×(1+z).f = \frac{1420.4\ \text{MHz}}{1 + z}, \qquad \lambda = 21.1\ \text{cm} \times (1 + z).f=1+z1420.4 MHz​,λ=21.1 cm×(1+z).

The old television channel three of The Van’s set (its picture at 55.25 MHz) listens at 1+z=25.71 + z = 25.71+z=25.7, waves 5.4 metres long, from about 130 million years after the beginning. EDGES’s dip, at 78 MHz, is from 1+z=18.21 + z = 18.21+z=18.2, about 220 million years in Planck’s universe (EDGES’s own paper, with slightly different numbers, says about 180).

Why the gas was colder than the light. Until a redshift of about 150, the few free electrons left over from the fog kept the gas at the oldest light’s temperature.
