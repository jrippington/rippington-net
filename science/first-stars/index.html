The simulator has run all night, and its screen is still black. John says the universe has had a power cut. By evening he and Cal have watched its glow fade like the plateau's cooling lava, found out why a ball of gas gets hotter as it loses heat, lit the first star, and watched its light strip the hydrogen bare in bubbles that grow and join until the long night is over.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 6 of 18

Lights out

In the morning the simulator’s screen was still black.

John had left it running all night, from the moment the fog cleared, and The Van had sat in its corner of the car park with its dashboard faintly lit, showing nothing at all. When Cal came across with two coffees, the line on the screen hadn’t changed since midnight:

NOTHING TO SEE FOR SOME TIME. PLEASE WAIT

Only the counter in the corner had moved. It counted the years since the beginning, and it said 94 million.

Cal put the coffees on the dashboard, looked at the black screen, and hit it, firmly, in the place that usually worked. It stayed black.

SCREEN WORKING. UNIVERSE DARK

“It’s been like that for ninety million years,” said John, who had his chin in his hands. “Give or take. It’s a power cut. The whole universe has had a power cut.”

Cal wound the simulator back to watch the lights go out. The counter ran down to 380,000 years, and the screen filled with the glow of the fourth journey: the oldest light, just set free, the warm yellow-white glow of gas at about 3,000 degrees, the same in every direction. He let it run. For a few hundred thousand years it hardly seemed to change. Then it began to deepen, to orange by about three quarters of a million years, then to red, then to a dull, dark red, like the embers in a grate, and at about three million years it was gone. The screen was black again, and it stayed black.

“That’s what the lava does,” said Cal.

Beyond the car park the plateau’s old lava lay in black ridges and tongues all the way to the foot of the mountain. Every bit of it had come out of the ground glowing, at around a thousand degrees. As a flow cools, its glow deepens to red, and at about 500 °C the last of it fades from sight, though the rock would still burn your hand for days. John and Cal had watched it happen in the simulator in Red Hot, White Hot.

The universe went out the same way, at the same temperature. Its glow wasn’t losing heat to anything, because there was nowhere outside it for the heat to go. It was cooling because the universe was growing, stretching every wave of its light, as Further Apart Every Day found, and a glow with longer waves is the glow of something cooler. By about three million years after the beginning, the glow everywhere was cooler than about 500 °C, and an eye could no longer see it.

It hadn’t stopped glowing, though. A lava flow that has gone dark still glows, in infrared light, which no eye can see, and so did the universe. Cal fetched the probe from its cupboard behind the kettle, and set the simulator to show what the probe’s warm camera, which sees in the infrared, would see. The screen lit up again, dimly, with an even glow from every direction, and as the counter ran on, the glow crept to longer and longer waves. At about fifteen million years, The Van’s outside thermometer, which had been listening, gave its opinion.

OUTSIDE TEMPERATURE: 20 DEGREES. RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

“A nice day for it,” said Cal.

It was. About fifteen million years after the beginning, the light filling the whole universe was at room temperature. It didn’t stay there. It went on cooling, and today, as the fourth journey found, it’s at 2.7 degrees above absolute zero. But from about three million years after the beginning until the first stars lit, there was nothing anywhere in the universe for an eye to see: no stars, no galaxies, no light of any colour a person could see. Astronomers call it the cosmic dark ages, a name usually credited to the Astronomer Royal, Martin Rees. It lasted something like a hundred million years, and it’s dark in a second sense too: nobody has ever seen anything from it.

A power cut

“So that’s a hundred million years of nothing,” said John.

Albert had come out to the car park behind Cal with flour on his hands. He had been setting the next morning’s dough to rise in the warm cupboard by the oven, with a cloth over it, in the dark.

“When the lights go out in the café,” he said, “does everything stop?”

“The fryer does.”

“And the dough?”

John thought about it. “It keeps rising,” he said. “It doesn’t need the lights.”

“Neither did this,” said Albert, and nodded at the screen.

John’s mistake is a natural one. To the eye, dark and empty look exactly the same, and when there’s nothing to see, it’s easy to assume there’s nothing happening. In a power cut the café goes dark and quiet, but the dough goes on rising, the fridge goes on keeping things cold for a while, and everything in the building goes on being pulled towards the ground. The dark ages were the same. Nothing shone, but in every part of the universe something was going on, and in the end it would bring the light back.

The fifth journey found it. Before the fog cleared, ordinary matter couldn’t gather into clumps, because the light trapped with it pushed back every time gravity pulled it together. Dark matter never felt the light. It had been gathering into clumps since long before the fog cleared, in the dark. And when the fog cleared, and the light flew free, nothing held the ordinary matter back any more.

The simulator could draw what nobody can see, if asked, and Cal asked it to draw the dark matter, in any colour it liked.

DARK MATTER: RECONSTRUCTED. COLOUR: INVENTED

The screen filled with a faint grey web, threads and knots very slightly denser than the space round them, the dark matter’s head start. Through it, drawn in pale blue, ran the ordinary gas: hydrogen and helium, nothing else, the mixture that came out of the third journey’s oven. As the counter ran on, the blue gas slid down into the grey knots, as rain on the plateau runs off the slopes into the hollows, and gathered in the middle of each.

By about a hundred million years, the first of the knots were ready. They were small, as these things go: about a million times the Sun’s mass, five sixths of it dark matter and a sixth ordinary gas, with a dense knot of gas at the middle of each, slowly getting denser. (Why the small knots were ready first is a story for the next journey.)

“So something is happening,” said John.

“It is rising,” said Albert, and went back to his dough.

How to light a star

“So the gas falls into the middle,” said John, “and that’s a star.”

“Is it?” said Cal. “Why should falling make a star? A stone falls and lands. Why doesn’t the gas just fall into a heap?”

He went round to the back of The Van, where the tyre was soft, as it usually was, and pumped it up while he thought about it. When he’d finished, he held the end of the pump out to John, as he had in the second journey, and it was warm. Squeezing a gas heats it.

The tyre is half of the answer. It holds up a corner of a two-and-a-half-tonne van with nothing but air, because the air’s molecules are rushing about and battering the rubber from inside. Pack more of them in, or make them move faster, and they batter harder. Hotter air is faster molecules, so warm air pushes harder than cold, and squeezed air harder than loose. That push is what a physicist calls pressure.

The other half was on the dashboard. On the way up from the coast the day before, Cal had bought a bag of crisps at the garage in the town, and it had sat on the dashboard ever since. It was as round and tight as a drum.

Air has weight, and at the coast it’s squeezed by the weight of all the air above it, the whole height of the sky: about ten tonnes on every square metre. The air at the coast pushes back exactly as hard, or it would be squashed flatter. On the plateau, a couple of kilometres up, about a fifth of the air is below you, so the air presses with only about four fifths of the coast’s push. The bag was sealed at the coast, and the air inside it still pushes as hard as the coast’s, against an outside that pushes less. So it swells. At every height in the sky the same balance holds: the push of the air there exactly holds up the weight of all the air above it. That’s why the sky neither falls to the ground nor floats away into space.

Hydrostatic equilibrium

The balance in which, at every depth, the push of a gas or a liquid holds up exactly the weight of everything above it. The air is in this balance: at the coast it is squeezed by the weight of the whole sky, and pushes back just as hard, which is why it is denser there than on a mountain. A star is in it too: its own weight presses in from every side, and the push of its hot gas holds the weight up. (Hydrostatic is from the Greek for water standing still.)

A star is a ball of gas in exactly that balance, only its weight is its own. Every part of it is pulled towards every other part, so the whole of it presses inwards on its middle, and the middle’s hot gas pushes back. How hard the middle has to push decides how hot it has to be.

Falling in

Now follow a knot of gas falling in on itself.

Gravity pulls every part of it towards every other part, so it shrinks, and shrinking squeezes it. Squeezing heats it, like the air in Cal’s pump, and the hotter gas pushes back harder. So a falling cloud puts the brakes on itself: the further it falls, the hotter it gets, and the harder it pushes. If it could keep all its heat, its push would grow until it held the whole cloud up, long before it was anywhere near as small or as hot as a star, and there it would hang, a vast, lukewarm ball of gas, for ever. Cal’s heap, and John’s star, would both have to wait.

So a cloud can only go on falling if it gets rid of its heat as fast as the squeezing makes it.

A gas gets rid of heat by glowing. Its atoms and molecules are always bumping into one another, and a hard enough bump knocks one into a higher energy level, as Why the Atom Survives found. It drops back, and gives out the difference as light. If the cloud is thin enough for that light to escape, the light carries the heat away, and the cloud stays cool enough to keep falling. A cloud that glows well falls easily. A cloud that can’t glow can’t fall far.

The first gas couldn’t glow

The first gas was very bad at glowing.

It was nothing but hydrogen and helium, and at the temperatures of a falling cloud, neither of them can glow at all. To lift a hydrogen atom’s electron to its first step up costs 10.2 electronvolts, all at once. Albert’s till, in Light Arrives in Pieces, takes the whole price in a single note and won’t let small coins club together, and this is a large price. In a gas at a few thousand degrees, a typical bump carries a fraction of an electronvolt, and bumps carrying the whole price are vanishingly rare. Below about ten thousand degrees, a hydrogen atom is almost never knocked up a step, so it hardly glows at all. Helium’s first step is higher still.

What saved the first clouds was a few hydrogen atoms joined together in pairs, as molecules: fewer than one atom in every thousand. A molecule can spin, end over end, and its spins come in steps too, but much smaller ones, more than two hundred times smaller than the atom’s first step. Bumps in a gas at a few hundred degrees can just afford them. The molecules are set spinning, they slow down again by giving out infrared light, and the light escapes. It’s a feeble way to cool, with so few molecules doing all the work, but it was enough to bring the falling gas down to about 200 degrees above absolute zero, about −70 °C, and no further.

(Clouds today are full of dust, and of molecules such as carbon monoxide, made from heavier atoms left behind by earlier stars, and they can get far colder, as the eighth journey finds. The first clouds had none.)

That matters, because warm gas pushes hard. To fall in anyway, a clump of gas has to be heavy enough for its own weight to beat its push, and the warmer it is, the heavier it has to be. At about 200 degrees above absolute zero, a clump of the first gas had to weigh about a thousand times as much as the Sun. Anything smaller simply couldn’t fall in on its own. So the first clumps to fall in were big, and so, very probably, were the first stars. Computer simulations of them, run with growing care since about 2000, give stars of tens to hundreds of times the Sun’s mass, some alone and some in pairs or small groups, where the falling gas broke into a few pieces. Nobody knows the masses for sure, and the simulator didn’t pretend to.

Too thick to cool

On the screen, a single knot of gas fell. For several hundred thousand years it shrank, glowing feebly in the infrared, keeping itself at a few hundred degrees. And as it shrank, its middle grew denser and denser, until at last it was so thick that its own glow couldn’t get out: the light was caught on its way, again and again, as the warmth under a duvet is caught.

Now nothing stopped the squeezing from heating it. The middle heated fast, through thousands of degrees and then tens of thousands, and its push grew, until it was pushing as hard as the weight above it pressed. The falling stopped. What was left was a ball of hot gas in balance, glowing from its surface: a protostar, not yet a star, shining by the heat of its own squeezing.

Hotter by losing heat

Here the story turns strange.

A cup of tea, left on the table, loses heat and cools. Everything in everyday life does: the fryer after closing, the plateau’s lava, the oldest light. A ball of gas holding itself up by its own heat does the opposite.

Its surface is hot, so it shines, and shining carries heat away. With less heat, its gas pushes a little less hard, and its weight squeezes it a little smaller. But squeezing heats it. And when the sums are done, it comes out hotter than it was before it lost the heat: of the energy set free by the shrinking, half goes into heating the gas, and only half leaves as light. So for as long as it shines, the ball gets smaller and hotter. Losing heat is what makes it hot.

“So if I leave a doughnut on the terrace,” said John, “it’ll get hotter?”

“Your doughnut,” said Albert, “is not holding itself up by its own weight.”

“Not for long, anyway,” said Cal, and ate it.

Two ways to stop

The ball shrinks, and its middle grows hotter, year after year. Can it ever stop?

Only if one of two things happens.

  • Something replaces the heat it shines away. When the middle reaches about ten million degrees, its protons are moving fast enough to tunnel into one another often enough for fusion, as Through the Wall found. Fusion sets heat free there, by E = mc², and once it sets heat free as fast as the surface shines it away, the ball no longer needs to shrink. It stops. That’s a star. (The heaviest first stars, with the most weight to hold up and nothing but hydrogen and helium to burn, needed more: their middles squeezed on to about a hundred million degrees before their fusion caught up. The maths page, under Hotter by losing heat, has the reason.)
  • Or something other than heat holds it up. Squeeze electrons together and, as Room for Only Two found, no two of them can share the same state, so squeezing them closer takes more and more energy, until they hold up a weight all by themselves, hot or cold. In a big ball, that never happens in time: its middle reaches fusion first. In a small one, below about a twelfth of the Sun’s mass, the electrons get there first. They take the weight, the shrinking stops with the middle too cool for fusion, and since nothing is replacing the heat it shines away, it just cools, for ever. It’s a failed star, and astronomers call it a brown dwarf.

There’s no third way. A ball of gas held up by its heat must shine, because its surface is hot and the universe round it is cold, and shining makes it shrink and heat, so it can’t sit still. If the ball is big enough that its electrons can’t stop it, the shrinking goes on until the middle is hot enough for fusion, and then it’s a star. So any clump of gas big enough to fall in on itself, and too big to be held up by its electrons, becomes a star, whether anyone is watching or not.

Brown dwarf

A ball of gas too small to become a star. Below about a twelfth of the Sun’s mass, its electrons, which won’t share a state, take up its weight before its middle is hot enough for hydrogen to fuse, so the shrinking stops short of fusion. It glows faintly with the heat of its own squeezing, and then cools for ever. Brown dwarfs weigh from about thirteen times as much as Jupiter up to that line, and most are about Jupiter’s size.

The first brown dwarf anyone was sure of was found at the observatory up the road. In 1995 three astronomers of the Canary Islands’ own institute, Rafael Rebolo, María Rosa Zapatero Osorio and Eduardo Martín, announced a faint red speck in the Pleiades, found with the observatory’s 80-centimetre telescope and named after the observatory: Teide 1. It’s about 55 times Jupiter’s mass, about Jupiter’s size, and its light still shows the dark line of lithium, which any small true star, mixed right down to its hot middle, burns away within its first hundred million years or so. Teide 1’s middle has never been hot enough. It’s in John’s picture of the Pleiades, too faint to show.

The first light

At about a hundred million years after the beginning, perhaps a little later, the middle of the first knot on the screen grew hot enough for fusion, and the knot stopped shrinking. The simulator was careful about what it showed.

FIRST STAR: RECONSTRUCTED. NOBODY HAS SEEN ONE

It drew a star of about a hundred times the Sun’s mass, about five times the Sun’s width, its surface at about a hundred thousand degrees, about seventeen times as hot as the Sun’s. It shone more than a million times as brightly as the Sun.

Albert looked at it for a long time.

“A hundred Suns’ worth,” he said, “turning a little of itself into light. By E = mc².” He seemed very pleased about something.

Cal had his hand on his shirt pocket, where the grain of sugar was, folded into its napkin with DO NOT EAT on the outside.

“None of this is here yet,” he said. “Is it? The grain’s hydrogen is. But there’s no carbon anywhere in the universe. No oxygen.”

There wasn’t. Out of the third journey’s oven had come hydrogen and helium, and traces of the next few lightest nuclei, and nothing else. The first carbon and oxygen the universe ever had were made inside stars like this one, and thrown out when they died, as the tenth and eleventh journeys find. Everything heavier than the oven’s handful began in stars like it.

They didn’t have long. A star of a hundred Suns burns through its fuel in less than three million years, a blink beside the Sun’s ten billion. But in those three million years it changed everything round it.

Bubbles

Something a hundred thousand degrees hot glows mostly in the ultraviolet, as Red Hot, White Hot’s oven would at that temperature: its brightest waves are about a thirteenth of the length of violet light’s, the shortest an eye can see. And ultraviolet light is made of big lumps.

The fourth journey found the price of a hydrogen atom’s electron: 13.6 electronvolts, paid all at once by a single lump of light, Albert’s big note. When the fog cleared, leaving the wall of the oldest light, the surface of last scattering, the glow had grown too cool to pay it, and the electrons settled into atoms for good. The first star’s light could pay it easily. Most of its lumps of ultraviolet carry more than that, and every one that meets a hydrogen atom strips it of its electron again.

So round the first star, a bubble of stripped hydrogen grew. The light ate its way out through the knot of gas round the star and into the thin gas beyond, stripping atom after atom, and the bubble swelled as long as the star shone. A star of a hundred Suns, in its three million years, stripped tens of thousands of times its own weight of hydrogen. On the screen its bubble grew to nearly twenty thousand light-years across before the star went out.

There’s something very like it in John’s pictures, in a galaxy of today.

R136 is only about one and a half million years old, and its stars are the most massive known. Their ultraviolet light strips the hydrogen round them, and the glow in John’s picture is what happens next: now and then a stripped electron settles back onto a proton, dropping down the atom’s levels, and gives out light at hydrogen’s own colours. (John’s picture shows the light of hydrogen, oxygen and sulphur through filters that pass only their colours, and gives each a colour of its own.) The first stars’ bubbles hardly glowed at all, because the gas round them was so thin that their electrons rarely found a proton again. But they were the same thing, the stripping of hydrogen by a giant star’s light, on an even larger scale.

The fog lifts again

By the time the first stars died, there were others. The knots of gas in the dark matter’s web went on falling in, more and more of them, and bigger, and each lit up with stars of its own, and each star blew a bubble. The bubbles grew. Where two met, they joined.

At about midday John and Cal took their coffee out onto the terrace, and below them the sea of clouds was breaking up, as it does on some days when the sun gets to work on it. In the morning it had lain unbroken to the horizon. Now there were holes in it, through which they could see the dark slopes and the coast far below. The holes grew as they watched, and where two met they ran together, until there was more hole than cloud, and the last of it lay in ragged strands between them.

The universe cleared in the same way. Each first galaxy’s stars made a hole in the hydrogen round it. The holes grew, and met, and joined, until by about a billion years after the beginning there was more hole than hydrogen, and then hardly any hydrogen left whole between the galaxies at all. The hydrogen between the galaxies has stayed stripped ever since, kept that way by the light of all the galaxies there have been.

Reionisation

The stripping of the electrons from nearly all the hydrogen between the galaxies by the ultraviolet light of the first stars and galaxies, which ended the dark ages. It began round the first stars, about a hundred million years after the beginning, in bubbles of stripped gas, which grew and joined; it was half done by about 700 million years, and finished by about a billion. It’s called reionisation because it was the second time the hydrogen’s electrons had been free: the first was before the fog cleared, at 380,000 years.

There’s a puzzle in it. The fourth journey found that atoms make the universe clear: free electrons were the fog, and once they had settled into atoms, the oldest light flew straight. Now the stars were tearing the atoms apart again. So what fog did the first stars clear? Whole hydrogen atoms are clear only to the light they don’t take. As the first journey found, every atom takes the colours of its own fingerprint, and hydrogen’s strongest are in the ultraviolet: the very colours of the first stars’ light. To the first stars’ ultraviolet, a universe full of whole hydrogen atoms was a fog as thick as the old one had been to every colour, and stripping the atoms cleared it. Once stripped, the hydrogen took no colours at all, and the ultraviolet went straight.

That is how reionisation can be seen. In 1965 James Gunn and Bruce Peterson pointed out that if there were whole hydrogen between us and a distant quasar (the brilliant heart of a young galaxy, which the next journey explains), it would take a band out of the quasar’s light just on the blue side of hydrogen’s strongest ultraviolet colour, stretched by the expansion until it arrives as red or infrared light. Even one atom in ten thousand left whole would be enough to take all of it. For decades, every quasar anyone looked at let at least some of that light through: the hydrogen between the galaxies was stripped. Then in 2001, Robert Becker and his colleagues took the light of the most distant quasar then known, found by the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, whose light had set out about 870 million years after the beginning, and the band was black. The light had passed through the last of the fog. A survey of dozens of distant quasars in 2022 found the last ragged strands of it lingering until about 1.1 billion years after the beginning.

The oldest light tells the other end of the story. When the hydrogen was stripped, its electrons were free again, and free electrons knock light about, so a little of the oldest light, crossing the universe since, has been knocked off course on its way to us: about one lump in twenty. Light knocked aside comes away partly polarised, like the glare off the café’s tables in Three Pairs of Sunglasses, and the Planck satellite measured exactly that faint polarisation in the oldest light on the largest scales. From how much there is, the stripping was half done by about 700 million years after the beginning.

Listening for the long night

There is one more way of seeing the long night, and The Van had it on a shelf above the kettle.

Its little old television, the one with the snow, had a dial with channels on it, and the lowest of them, channels two to four, were made for the earliest television broadcasts, on waves five or six metres long, and like every other channel they had been silent since 2010. Cal turned the dial down to channel three, and the snow came back, hissing.

CHANNEL 3: SNOW. MOSTLY THE GALAXY

The hydrogen between the stars hums at a single radio note, a wavelength of 21 centimetres, given out when an atom’s electron flips its little magnet over, as John and Cal found in Three Pairs of Sunglasses. The hydrogen of the long night had that note too, and on its way to us the expansion of the universe has stretched it, as it stretched the oldest light. Hydrogen’s note from about 130 million years after the beginning, when the first stars were lighting up, has been stretched about 26 times, and arrives with waves about five and a half metres long: exactly the waves of The Van’s channel three.

The note can be seen against the oldest light. During the long night the gas between the knots grew colder even than the oldest light passing through it, a few degrees above absolute zero, because a gas cools faster as it spreads out than light does as it stretches, as a spray can goes cold in the hand. When the first stars lit, their ultraviolet light, bouncing off hydrogen atoms far and wide, set the atoms’ little magnets in step with that cold, and the cold gas began to take a little of the oldest light at its own note. That makes a dark line, the first journey’s kind, at 21 centimetres, stretched by the expansion: a dip in the radio sky’s brightness, at the wavelengths of the time the first stars lit. How deep it is says how cold the gas was. Where on the dial it falls says when.

The trouble is the snow. At five metres, our own galaxy hisses tens of thousands of times more loudly than the dip, and the dip is spread smoothly across many channels, so the galaxy’s hiss has to be taken away with extraordinary care to find it. In 2018 an experiment called EDGES, a small aerial in the Western Australian desert, reported a dip at waves of about 3.8 metres, from about two hundred million years after the beginning, twice as deep as anyone had expected. In 2022 another, SARAS 3, an aerial floating on lakes in southern India where the water beneath it made its own hiss easier to account for, looked at the same waves and found nothing there, and rejected EDGES’s dip with 95 per cent confidence. The argument isn’t settled, and other experiments, in South Africa’s Karoo and elsewhere, are still listening.

“So the first stars are on The Van’s television,” said Cal, “somewhere under the snow.”

“So is everything else,” said Albert.

Has anyone seen one?

“The simulator says nobody has seen one,” said John. “Has anybody tried?”

They have, hard. The James Webb Space Telescope was built partly to look for them, and the light of a first star’s galaxy would be distinctive. A glow from nothing but hydrogen and helium would show the lines of those two and of nothing heavier, none of the oxygen and carbon in the light of every galaxy of today. And light as hot as a first star’s could do something the light of today’s stars hardly ever can: strip both of helium’s electrons, so that the gas round the stars would glow in a particular ultraviolet line of helium’s, stretched into the infrared by the time it reached Webb.

There have been candidates. In 2015 a team using the Very Large Telescope in Chile found a bright, distant galaxy called CR7, whose light seemed to show helium’s line and nothing heavier. Closer looks found heavier atoms in it after all: in 2017 the ALMA radio telescopes found carbon. In 2023 a team using Webb found a small clump of gas beside the galaxy GN-z11, whose light set out about 430 million years after the beginning, glowing in helium’s line with no sign of anything heavier, and in 2026 they looked again, more closely, and found the same. In 2025 another team looked closely at a clump called LAP1-B, whose light set out about 800 million years after the beginning, magnified about a hundred times by the gravity of a cluster of galaxies in front of it, with almost nothing in its light but hydrogen and helium. Its mixture of stars, as far as anyone can tell, is just what the simulations predict for a group of first stars.

Neither is a first star seen directly. Each is gas lit up by something hot, and the something could still turn out to be ordinary stars in very nearly pure gas, or gas falling into a black hole.

And there’s another way of looking, closer to home. A first star smaller than about four fifths of the Sun’s mass would burn its fuel so slowly that it would still be shining today, somewhere in our own galaxy, with nothing but hydrogen and helium in its light. Astronomers have searched among the oldest stars for decades, and they have never found one. Every old star has at least a trace of heavier atoms. The closest anyone has come was found in 2014 by the SkyMapper telescope in Australia: a star called SMSS J0313−6708, about 6,000 light-years away, with less than a ten-millionth of the Sun’s share of iron. The other heavier atoms in its light, carbon above all, are just the mixture a single first star of about sixty Suns would have thrown out in a feeble explosion, as its core collapsed into a black hole. It’s a second-generation star, made from the ashes of one of the first. Since no small first stars have turned up, it seems there were few of them, or none. The first stars were heavy, as the warm gas said they would be.

“So nobody has seen a first star,” said John, “but somebody has found one’s ashes.”

“In another star’s light,” said Cal. “The first journey’s kit.”

Back to Teide

By evening the sea of clouds had gone altogether, and from the terrace the coast lay clear below, its towns lighting up one by one as the sun went down behind La Palma.

The Van’s simulator had run on through the afternoon. It had shown the bubbles growing and joining until the fog of the long night was gone, and then Cal had wound it back to where John wanted it, a few hundred million years after the beginning, and left it there. On the dashboard screen the first stars glittered in the dark, not scattered evenly, but in little crowds, each in a knot of the dark matter’s web, which the simulator still drew in grey, because nobody can see it.

“I had it wrong this morning,” said John. “It wasn’t a power cut.”

“It was,” said Cal. “It’s just that the power came back on.”

He was watching the coast, where the lights were still coming on, a few at a time, here and there, until the whole of it was lit. When the power comes back after a cut, it doesn’t come back everywhere at once. It comes back a street at a time.

Albert brought out the last of the doughnuts and set them down on the terrace wall, and then stood and looked at the screen through The Van’s windscreen, at the little crowds of first stars in their knots of grey.

“And what do the crowds do?” said Cal.

“The same as everything else in the dark,” said Albert. “They fall.”

Cal looked again. Along one of the grey threads, two of the little crowds had begun, very slowly, to drift towards each other.
