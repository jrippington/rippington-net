The probe at the slits

The probe was in its cupboard behind The Van’s kettle, its green light blinking.

On the first journey of all, far out in empty space, it had watched John throw a doughnut across the cabin. Since then it had watched a great many stranger things, and only three nights before it had counted single photons in the car park, and in all that time it had never once broken down. It was the only thing on board of which that could be said.

John lifted it out of the cupboard and set it on the dashboard.

“Last night,” he said, “we agreed that the arrows tell you where the electron lands, and not what it does on the way.”

“You agreed,” said Cal. “I went to bed.”

“So today we find out. We put the probe beside the slits, and it watches each electron go through. Then we’ll know which slit it really took.”

The simulator could shrink anything to any size, and it could shrink the probe. John asked it to rebuild the experiment from the fourth journey: the electron gun, firing one electron at a time; the two slits, A and B, a thousandth of a millimetre apart; the screen beyond them, where each electron lands as a single dot. Then he asked it to set the probe down beside the slits, shrunk to fit, with its lamp shining on them.

The idea was simple, and it’s the obvious way to find out where something goes: look. Each electron passing a slit is lit by the probe’s lamp, and scatters a little of the light, a single photon, as it goes by, a tiny flash at the slit it is passing. The probe’s camera sees the flash and where it came from. A flash at the top slit means A; a flash at the bottom slit means B.

John set the gun firing.

PROBE: WATCHING. ELECTRON 1: SLIT A

ELECTRON 2: SLIT B

ELECTRON 3: SLIT B

Every electron made one flash, at one slit. Not one was ever seen at both, and none slipped through as half an electron at each. Whenever the probe looked, each electron had gone one way or the other.

“There you are,” said John. “It does go through one slit. We just had to look.”

“Look at the screen,” said Cal.

The dots were arriving one at a time, as before. But they weren’t making stripes. They piled up into one broad smooth patch, brightest in the middle, with no dark bands anywhere: exactly the pattern the fourth journey had worked out for particles going one way or the other, slit A’s patch and slit B’s patch added together.

STRIPES: NONE

John stared at it.

“The probe’s broken the experiment,” he said.

“The probe has never broken down,” said Cal.

“There’s a first time for everything.” John turned the probe off. The dots, starting again, built stripes. He turned it on. One smooth patch. Off: stripes. On: none.

FAULT REPORTED BY USER: PROBE. FAULTS FOUND: NONE

“It’s working perfectly,” said Cal. “That’s the problem.”

Is it the kick?

John wasn’t ready to give up on his probe, and he had a good reason. When the probe watches, something really does happen to the electron. To be seen, it has to be hit by light.

In The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe, the seventh journey of the series before this one, John and Cal found that light carries momentum: a beam of sunlight pushes on a sail, and a single photon pushes, very slightly, on whatever it hits. An electron is very light, and a photon bouncing off it gives it a real kick, enough to knock it slightly off course. Perhaps that’s all that’s happening. The stripes depend on very fine details of where each electron goes. Kick it, even a little, and you jumble its course, and the fine stripes blur away, as a photograph blurs if you jog the camera.

That’s the obvious explanation, and the physicists of the 1920s took it very seriously. If it’s right, the fix is just as obvious. Be gentler.

Dimmer

First, John turned the probe’s lamp down, so that its light was very dim. Now there weren’t enough photons to catch every electron. Some electrons went past without scattering any light at all, and the probe saw nothing. Others scattered a photon and were seen, at A or at B.

The stripes came partly back: faint stripes over the smooth patch. Then Cal had the idea of asking the simulator to sort the dots. For every dot on the screen, the probe’s log said either “seen at A”, “seen at B”, or “not seen”. Cal asked for the dots of the seen electrons, on their own. They made a smooth patch with no stripes at all. Then the dots of the unseen ones, on their own. They made perfect stripes.

SEEN AT A OR B: 5,012. STRIPES: NONE

NOT SEEN: 4,987. STRIPES: FULL

Notice how strange that is. Both heaps of dots came from the same gun, through the same two slits, to the same screen, at the same time, all mixed up together. Nothing about the slits or the screen was different for the two kinds. The only difference was whether the probe had a record of each electron’s way. Every electron with a record made a dot belonging to the smooth patch, and every electron without one made a dot belonging to the stripes.

That’s a strong clue, but it doesn’t settle anything yet. An electron the probe didn’t see is one no photon hit, so it wasn’t kicked either. Seen and kicked, or unseen and left alone: the two always go together, so far.

Redder

So John tried the other way of being gentle. Instead of fewer photons, he used gentler ones. Redder light has longer waves, and each of its photons carries less momentum than a photon of blue light, so it gives a smaller kick. Make the light red enough, and each kick is too small to jumble the electron’s course.

He turned the lamp back up, and slid its colour slowly from blue towards red.

With blue light, the probe saw every electron, at A or at B, and there were no stripes. As the light grew redder, the probe’s pictures of the two slits began to blur. That’s no fault of the probe. It’s the problem from the first journey: light can’t show you anything much smaller than its own waves, which is why no microscope will ever show an atom by light. The two slits are a thousandth of a millimetre apart, and red light’s waves are not much shorter than that. As the light reddened, the flash at A and the flash at B began to look alike, two blurs running into one another. The probe’s guesses about which slit grew less reliable: right nine times in ten, then eight, then seven.

And as the probe grew less able to tell, the stripes came back. Faint at first, then stronger. When the light was deep in the infrared, its waves far longer than the gap between the slits, the probe’s picture showed one blur where both slits were, and it could no longer tell A from B at all. Its guesses were right half the time, no better than tossing a coin. And the stripes were back in full.

Richard Feynman, whose arrows filled the last journey, told this story in his lectures to students at the California Institute of Technology in the early 1960s, with an imaginary light behind the slits, and with exactly this ending. Each time you make the light gentle enough to leave the stripes alone, you find you’ve made it too blurred to tell you which slit.

“It’s a conspiracy,” said John.

It’s certainly a coincidence, if it is one. The kick and the blurring go together: redder light gives smaller kicks and blurrier pictures, both at once, and you can’t have one without the other. So, so far, there’s still no way to tell which is to blame. Is the kick washing out the stripes? Or is it the knowing?

To find out, you’d need to learn which slit an electron took without kicking it at all.

A record without a kick

That sounds impossible, but in 1998 three physicists at the University of Konstanz, in Germany, found a way to do it.

Stephan Dürr, Tilman Nonn and Gerhard Rempe didn’t use electrons, but atoms of rubidium, cooled with lasers until they were drifting along almost still. Instead of slits cut in a sheet of metal, they used beams of laser light, arranged so that each atom’s way was split into two and then, a little later, brought back together, which makes stripes in where the atoms arrive, just as two slits do. And they used something an atom has and an electron doesn’t: a sort of switch inside it.

An atom of rubidium can sit in either of two slightly different inner states, two settings, roughly speaking, of how its parts are arranged, and a short pulse of microwaves can flip it from one to the other. Dürr, Nonn and Rempe set things up so that the atom’s switch was set one way if it took the first route and the other way if it took the second. Each atom carried away inside itself a record of which way it had gone.

Flipping the switch does give the atom a kick, but an extraordinarily small one. Dürr, Nonn and Rempe worked out exactly how much it could jumble each atom’s course, and it was far too little to blur the stripes. If the kick were the cause, the stripes should have stayed.

The stripes vanished.

And the stripes didn’t wait for anyone to look at the switches. Nobody needed to read off, for any atom, which way it had gone. The record was simply there, inside each atom, readable in principle. That was enough.

So it isn’t clumsiness. Watching doesn’t destroy the stripes by pushing the particle about. It destroys them by leaving a record, anywhere in the world, of which way the particle went.

Measurement

In quantum mechanics, any happening that leaves a record of something, whether or not anyone ever reads it: a photon scattered off an electron into a camera, a switch set inside an atom, a mark on a screen. A measurement doesn’t need a person, an instrument or a laboratory; it only needs the world to be left different according to the answer. And a measurement of which way something went, however gentle, ends the adding of the arrows for those ways.

Two different endings

Why should a record matter? A record is just a fact about the probe, or about the switch. It isn’t doing anything to the electron. To see why it makes all the difference, go back to the arrows of the last journey, and look closely at what the rule actually says.

The rule is: when something can happen in several ways, add the arrows of all the ways, and the final arrow’s length gives the chance. But “something” means one particular outcome: one particular ending. To find the chance of an electron arriving at a point X on the screen, you add the arrows of all the ways that end with the electron at X. You never add the arrows of ways that end differently. The chance of landing at X and the chance of landing at some other point Y are different outcomes, and each is worked out on its own: the arrows of the ways to X are added together, and the arrows of the ways to Y are added together, but X’s arrows are never added to Y’s.

Now put the probe beside the slits. Without the probe, the way through A and the way through B both end in exactly the same situation: the electron at X, and nothing else in the world different. So they’re two ways to one outcome, and their arrows are added. They can cancel, and there are stripes.

With the probe watching, the two ways don’t end in the same situation any more. The way through A ends with the electron at X and the probe’s record saying “A”. The way through B ends with the electron at X and the probe’s record saying “B”. Those are two different endings, as different as X and Y, as different as heads and tails. So their arrows are never added. Each ending has its own arrow, and its own chance, and the chance of the electron landing at X at all is the chance of the first ending plus the chance of the second. Chances add, as Cal’s rule says, and chances can’t cancel. No stripes.

Put another way, a record turns the electron back into the coin under Albert’s cup. Once the probe has its record, the electron has gone one way or the other, with a chance for each, and the world holds the answer, whether or not anyone looks. That is exactly the situation in which chances add. The stripes need the other situation, the superposition of the last journey, in which both ways are open and nothing anywhere tells them apart.

There’s no way out of it. You might say that the record doesn’t count unless someone reads it; but whether anyone reads it or not, the two endings are different, and the rule doesn’t add arrows for different endings. You might say the electron must be kicked after all; but the switch in Dürr, Nonn and Rempe’s atoms made the endings different without any kick worth the name, and the stripes went anyway. The record itself is the difference.

And the half-and-half cases fall out on their own. With the probe’s red light, its pictures of the two slits overlapped: the flash from A and the flash from B were partly alike and partly different. The two endings were partly the same and partly not. So the arrows could partly add, and there were partly stripes. The more alike the two records, the stronger the stripes; the more different, the weaker. When the records are identical, as when the light was so red the probe saw one blur, the endings are the same, the arrows add in full, and the stripes are perfect.

The still figure below draws the two cases. On the left, nothing keeps a record: both ways end in one situation, and their arrows are added head to tail. On the right, the probe keeps one: the two ways end in different situations, each keeps its own arrow, and the chances add.

The live figure is the probe at the slits, as the simulator showed it. The probe is off to begin with; turn it on, and set how well it can tell the two slits apart, which in the simulator comes down to the colour of its lamp: violet or blue for a sharp picture, red for a blurred one, infrared for one blur. Its picture is in the small box under it. The table gives how often its guess of the way is right and how strong the stripes are, as a share of their full strength. However you set it, the two pull against each other: a probe that’s right nine times in ten leaves the stripes at about 60 per cent; a probe that guesses no better than a coin leaves them whole. There’s no setting at which the probe knows the way perfectly and the stripes survive.

The trade-off isn’t approximate. There’s an exact rule connecting how well anything can tell the two ways apart and how strong the stripes can be, worked out by several physicists between 1979 and 1996, and every experiment that has tested it has found it holding: the better the one, the worse the other, by a definite amount.

Tea

Niels arrived in the middle of the afternoon, when the coaches had gone and the café was quiet.

He was a big man, though he didn’t seem it, because he moved quietly and stood a little stooped, as people do who have spent their lives leaning forward to listen. He came in from the car park, saw Albert, and smiled, and Albert came out from behind the counter faster than John and Cal had ever seen him move, and shook his hand for a long time.

“This is Niels,” said Albert. “We have been disagreeing for a long time.”

“Not about everything,” said Niels.

“About most of the important things.”

Niels considered this. “About most of them,” he agreed, “in a sense.”

He asked for tea. The café was not really set up for tea. Albert found a box of tea bags at the back of a cupboard, and a teapot that had been used for some years to keep teaspoons in, and washed it out, and in the end produced, with an air of quiet achievement, a pot of something that was recognisably tea. Niels thanked him warmly and sat at the window table with John and Cal, and asked them what they had been doing.

He spoke so softly that they had to lean in to hear him, and he qualified almost everything he said, as if each sentence were a step onto ice that he wanted to test before trusting it with his weight. But he listened with complete attention while John told him about the probe: the flashes, every electron at A or at B, the stripes vanishing, the dimmer light, the redder light, and the atoms of Konstanz with their switches.

“And so,” said John, “we know which way each one went, and we’ve lost the stripes. Or we keep the stripes, and we don’t know which way. We can’t have both.”

“No,” said Niels. “You can’t. Not at once.” He turned his cup a little on its saucer. “But I would be careful about saying that you have lost something. You set up the experiment one way, with the probe, and you asked it: which way? It answered. You set it up the other way, without the probe, and you asked: where do they land? It answered with the stripes. Each answer is complete. They are answers to two different questions, and the two questions cannot be put to the electron at the same time, because the arrangement for asking one is the arrangement that makes the other impossible to ask.”

Cal worked it through out loud. “So the path and the stripes aren’t two halves of one picture, where we’ve only managed to see one half at a time. They’re two different pictures. Each one’s true for the experiment it comes from. And there’s no experiment that gives you both.”

“That is very well put,” said Niels. “Though I would want to say it more carefully.”

“How?”

Niels thought for some time. “No,” he said at last. “I think that was careful enough.”

“There is one more thing,” he added, after a moment. “When you told me about the experiment, you used words like probe, and lamp, and flash, and screen. Ordinary words, for ordinary things you can see and touch and write down. That is not an accident. Whatever we say about an electron, we can only say it by describing an experiment in words like those: what was set up, and what was recorded. The electron never speaks for itself.”

Complementarity

The idea that some descriptions of a quantum thing are each complete and correct for the experiment that shows them, yet can never be shown together, because the arrangement that reveals one rules out the other. Which way a particle went and the stripes it makes are complementary: an experiment can show the path, or the interference, never both at once. The wave picture and the particle picture are complementary in the same way. The word is Niels Bohr’s, from 1927.

The word was introduced, as it happens, by the Danish physicist Niels Bohr, in a lecture at a conference at Como, in Italy, in September 1927, and he spent the rest of his life defending it, refining it and qualifying it. The wave and the particle, he said, are not rival accounts of what an electron really is, one of which must be wrong. They’re complementary: each is needed, each answers a different question, and no experiment can call on both at once.

“I once had it written on a coat of arms,” said Niels.

“In Latin,” said Albert.

“It seemed to help.”

Albert brought a plate of lemon doughnuts to the table and set it down in front of Niels, who had never had one. He picked one up and held it, and looked at it. He looked at it from above. Then he turned it in his fingers and looked at it from the side, which was not at all the same thing. Then he looked at it from above again. He considered it for so long that the table fell quiet, and even Albert, who had seen a great many people consider his doughnuts, began to look a little anxious.

“There are two ways of looking at it,” he said at last, “and they are complementary.”

Cal choked on his coffee.

“He has been saying that for years,” said Albert.

What Albert thinks

Albert, it turned out, did not agree.

“You know my view,” he said to Niels, sitting down with his own coffee. “The electron goes through one slit or the other. Of course it does. It is a thing, and things are somewhere. We are only too clumsy to find out which without spoiling the stripes.”

“And the atoms in Konstanz?” said Niels mildly. “No clumsiness there.”

“The experiment in Konstanz,” said Albert, “is very clever.” He said it in the tone of a man conceding that a chess move was well played, without conceding the game.

“And where each one lands,” said Cal, “with no probe at all? One dot here, one there, and no way to say which?”

“That,” said Albert, “I like even less. I have never liked games of chance.”

The argument between them is an old one, and a real one. In October 1927, a month after Bohr’s lecture at Como, the leading physicists of the world met in Brussels, at the fifth of the Solvay conferences, to argue about the new quantum theory. Einstein, who had done as much as anyone to start it, with his lumps of light, didn’t believe it was the whole story. At breakfast each morning he brought Bohr a new imagined experiment, designed to beat the rules: to learn more about an electron than the theory said could be known. Each evening, by the account of those who were there, Bohr brought back an answer. The two of them went on arguing, in letters, at conferences and in each other’s houses, with great affection and without either quite convincing the other, until Einstein’s death in 1955.

Albert’s view isn’t foolish, and the probe and Konstanz don’t overturn it. They show that the stripes can’t survive any record of the way, however gentle. They show that the arrows of the last journey must be added only for ways that end in the same situation, and that a record makes the situations different. They show that if you set up the world to tell which slit, the answer is always one slit, never both. What they don’t show is what the electron is doing between the gun and the screen when nobody keeps a record: whether it’s “really” going one way, or both, or neither, or whether, as Niels would say, the question has no answer outside an experiment that asks it. That is still argued over today, and the fifteenth journey takes it on.

“I once suggested,” said Albert, “hanging the wall with the slits in it on springs, very loosely. Then the electron would give the wall a little kick, one way if it went through one slit, the other way if it went through the other, and you could tell which by measuring the wall, without ever touching the electron.”

“And I said,” said Niels, “that a wall hung loosely enough to feel such a kick would shake too much to keep the stripes in one place.”

“You did,” said Albert. “You always do.”

Exactly why the wall must shake, and by how much, is a matter for the next journey. But it’s the same story again. A wall that can keep a record of the way is a wall that spoils the stripes.

Records are everywhere, once you start looking for them. The probe’s photon flew off into its camera, but it would have done just as well flying off into space, where nobody could ever catch it: the record would still exist, written in where that photon went. A stray molecule of air bumping into the electron on its way through would keep a record of its own. It’s a thought that comes back, with great force, in the fourteenth journey, where it explains why nothing as big as a doughnut ever makes stripes.

Back to Teide

By evening, the light had gone soft and gold over the plateau, and the shadows of the old lava flows lay long across the ground. Niels had moved out onto the terrace with a second pot of tea, which was better than the first, and sat looking at the mountain. The probe was back in its cupboard behind the kettle, its green light blinking.

“So you can have the path,” said Niels, “or you can have the stripes. Never both.”

“Then we’ll measure more cleverly,” said John. “Find out which slit without leaving any record that spoils the stripes. There must be a way.”

Niels smiled at him with great kindness.

“It is harder than you think,” he said.

“I have been trying for years,” said Albert, who had come out to collect the cups.

“He has,” said Niels. “He is very good at it. He comes up with a new way every time I see him, and every time it is beautiful, and every time there is a reason.” He lifted his cup to Albert, very slightly.

“Then come back tomorrow,” said Albert. “I have been thinking about a box.”

Niels looked at him over his tea, and for a moment the two of them might have been much younger men, in a much colder city, arguing in a corridor at a conference until long after everyone else had gone to dinner.

“I always come back,” said Niels.

He finished his tea, and stood, and thanked Albert for it as though it had been the best tea he had ever had, and walked out to the car park and was gone.

Cal looked out at The Van for a long time. Through the windscreen, in the dusk, he could see the speedometer, glowing faintly, reading 17 mph, as it had read since anyone could remember, while The Van sat perfectly still.

“Right,” he said.
