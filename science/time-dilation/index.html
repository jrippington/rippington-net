Why everybody's second belongs to them

The clocks on The Van seem to run slow. Albert insists they are behaving perfectly, which is not what he says about the kettle.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 3 of 14

Two mirrors and an argument

John had been sitting outside the café looking at his watch for so long that Cal finally asked whether he was expecting someone.

“Time,” said John.

“Ambitious appointment.”

“The watch says a minute has passed. But after yesterday, I don’t see how everyone can agree on what a minute is.”

Yesterday they had raced The Van past the probe at nine-tenths of the speed of light and flashed its headlights, and both The Van and the probe had measured the same flash going at the same speed. Speed is distance divided by time. If two people moving past each other measure the same speed for the same flash, then something about their distances, or their times, isn’t what everyday life had led them to expect. John had spent the evening suspecting his watch.

Albert came out with coffee and two lemon doughnuts, and set down beside them the little wooden box he had produced the evening before.

Cal opened it. Inside were two small mirrors facing each other, about fifteen centimetres apart, with a tiny lamp set into the lower one.

“Wouldn’t a proper clock be easier?” said John.

“This is a proper clock,” said Albert. “It’s just one that shows you what it is doing.”

He pressed a switch. Nothing visible happened, which was, Albert explained, entirely the point. The lamp had sent a single pulse of light up to the top mirror. It bounced back down to the bottom mirror, which bounced it up again, and so on, up and down, for as long as the box was switched on. Each round trip, up and back, counted as one tick. The mirrors were set so that the light’s round trip was 30 centimetres, which light covers in a billionth of a second: one nanosecond. The box ticked a billion times a second, and a little counter on its side kept count.

“Nothing mysterious yet,” said Cal.

“An excellent time to become worried,” said Albert.

What a clock is

Every clock, under the decoration, is something that repeats steadily, and something that counts the repeats. A pendulum clock counts swings. A quartz watch counts the vibrations of a tiny sliver of quartz crystal, usually 32,768 a second. The world’s official second is defined by atoms: it is the time taken for 9,192,631,770 oscillations of the radiation from a caesium atom. Albert’s box counts the trips of a pulse of light between two mirrors. It’s called a light clock, and physicists have been reasoning with imaginary ones since at least 1909, when the American chemists Gilbert Lewis and Richard Tolman used one to explain Einstein’s ideas.

Nobody would build a wristwatch this way. The light clock’s great virtue is that it is the one clock we can reason about completely. A pendulum depends on gravity, and quartz on the springiness of crystals. The light clock depends only on the distance between two mirrors, and on the speed of light, which, as the second journey found, is the same for everybody.

The Station

“To find out whether it keeps time,” said Albert, “you’ll need something to compare it with.” He wrote a set of coordinates on a paper napkin, slid it across the table, and went back inside.

The coordinates led to a stretch of empty space a little beyond the Moon, and to The Station.

It was everything The Van was not. It was a single straight girder, just under twelve kilometres long, floating in the dark with no planet nearby to disturb it, keeping still relative to the distant stars. Along its whole length, every hundred metres, was a clock: more than a hundred identical clocks in a line, each with a little light that blinked once a second. At each end of the girder stood a gate, a square frame big enough for The Van to fly through, and on each gate a larger clock. A plaque on the first gate read:

MEASURING TRACK: 11,992 METRES. LIGHT CROSSES IT IN 40 MICROSECONDS.

A calm voice came over the radio. It belonged to The Station’s computer, which spoke slowly and clearly, in the manner of something that had never once had a fault.

“Welcome,” it said. “This station times fast things. Please don’t touch the clocks.”

John and Cal looked at the long line of blinking lights. Every clock blinked at the same moment, all the way down the girder, as if the whole line were breathing together.

“Why so many?” said Cal. “We’ve got the probe. Couldn’t it just watch?”

He had found the reason for The Station without meaning to. The probe was one clock in one place. It could time anything that happened right beside it, but The Van, once it got going, would not stay beside anything for long. To time a journey properly you need to know when it started, using a clock where it started, and when it finished, using a clock where it finished. The Station was built to do just that: wherever along the girder something happened, there was a clock right there to time it.

How The Station set its clocks

That only works if the clocks agree with each other. A clock at one end of the girder that ran ahead of a clock at the other end would make every journey look shorter or longer than it was.

The Station’s handbook, which was clearly written for The Station and not for a commercial dishwasher, explained how its engineers had set the clocks going together. They couldn’t carry each clock to the middle and set it by hand, because carrying clocks about is the kind of thing this article is about to question. Instead they had sent a single flash of light from the middle of the girder. Each clock, when the flash reached it, set itself to the time of the flash plus the time light takes to travel from the middle to that clock: the further away the clock, the more time it added. Light’s speed is the same in every direction, so every clock then agreed. They had checked by sending flashes back and forth, and the clocks had stayed in step ever since.

There is more to setting distant clocks going together than this. Whether two clocks a long way apart “agree” turns out to depend on who is asking, and it gets the next journey to itself. For this article, The Station’s word is good enough: its clocks are in step, as far as The Station and anything at rest beside it are concerned.

The Station also had one more thing: its own light clock, in a glass case beside the first gate, the twin of Albert’s. The Station’s computer offered to compare them. Cal held Albert’s box against the glass, and the two counters ticked up together, a billion a second each, without drifting apart.

“Identical,” said the computer, sounding as pleased as it was possible to sound. “Please take it back off the glass.”

Events

One word will help from here on. Physicists call anything that happens at one particular place, at one particular moment, an event.

Event

Something that happens at a single place at a single moment: a flash of light, the tick of a clock, The Van passing a gate, a doughnut landing in Cal’s hand. An event has no duration and no size; it is a point in space and time. To say where and when it happened, you need a ruler and a clock right there.

The Van passing the first gate would be an event, and so would The Van passing the second gate. Measuring how long the journey took means measuring the time between those two events, and there are two ways to do it. The Station can use two of its clocks, the one at the first gate and the one at the second, and subtract one reading from the other. The Van can use one clock, the one it carries, which is present at both events.

A sideways journey

The plan was to coast The Van straight down the measuring track at a steady speed, engines off, with Albert’s light clock ticking on the dashboard.

The Van’s own dashboard clock was no use for this. It had never once agreed with any other clock, and currently claimed that it was a quarter past seven on a Thursday. (It wasn’t.) Albert’s box, on the other hand, they trusted completely.

They backed The Van away from the first gate, a long way, and brought it up to eight-tenths of the speed of light. Then John cut the engines. The Van coasted, its speed steady, its direction straight: an inertial frame, as John and Cal had learned to call it on the first journey. Inside, nothing showed how fast they were going.

As The Van shot through the first gate, two things happened at the same place and the same moment. The Station’s computer noted the reading on the gate’s clock, and Cal pressed a button that set the counter on Albert’s light clock to zero. No argument was possible about either: each clock was right there, at the event it was timing.

A moment later The Van shot through the second gate. Again two clocks were together at one event: the second gate’s clock, and the light clock in The Van.

The second gate’s clock read 50 microseconds (millionths of a second) later than the first gate’s had. That made sense: light crosses the track in 40, and The Van was going at eight-tenths of light’s speed.

The light clock in The Van read 30.

Cal read it out. Then he read it out again.

The Station’s computer, which had been watching through all its clocks at once, confirmed it in its calm voice: The Van had taken 50 microseconds to cross the track. And The Station’s own light clock, the twin of Albert’s, had ticked away perfectly normally the whole time.

“So our clock is slow?” said Cal.

“Relative to The Station’s frame,” John said.

“And to us?”

“Perfectly normal.” John tapped the box. “Look at it. Up, down, a billion times a second, just as it was in the café.”

“That’s infuriating.”

“Not as infuriating as the kettle,” said John. “It’s started boiling something that isn’t water.”

“Is the light clock broken?” Cal asked The Station.

“Your clock is working normally,” said The Station’s computer. There was a short pause. “So are mine.”

Why the clock ticks slowly

It would be easy to decide that the light clock had simply gone wrong: shaken loose by the speed, perhaps. It hadn’t. The reason it ticks slowly, as The Station measures it, needs nothing more than the light clock, the constancy of the speed of light, and a right-angled triangle. First, though, consider what everyday experience has taught us to expect.

Time as a river

Most of us carry around a picture of time as a kind of great invisible river, flowing at the same rate everywhere in the universe. In that picture, clocks simply dip into the river and measure how fast it flows, and a good clock anywhere, moving or not, measures the same flow. Isaac Newton put it formally in 1687: absolute time, he wrote, “flows equably without relation to anything external”.

Everything in ordinary life agrees with this. Synchronise two watches, carry one to the shops and back, and they still agree. Pilots and passengers step off a flight with their watches still matching the airport’s clocks. If time weren’t the same for everyone, how would trains run to timetables?

The picture is so natural that we rarely notice it is a picture at all, an assumption rather than an observation. It is also wrong, though only by amounts far too small to notice at the speeds of trains and aeroplanes. At The Van’s speeds, it fails completely.

One tick, seen from inside The Van

Start with the light clock inside The Van. Relative to The Van, the clock isn’t moving. The light leaves the bottom mirror, goes straight up 15 centimetres to the top mirror, and comes straight back down: 30 centimetres in all, at the speed of light. That takes one nanosecond, as it did in the café. Whatever The Van’s speed past The Station, John and Cal see the same thing, because, by the principle of relativity, nothing done inside a steadily moving van can tell how fast it is moving. To them, the clock is perfectly normal, because it is perfectly normal.

The same tick, seen from The Station

Now watch the same clock from The Station, as The Van flies past.

The light leaves the bottom mirror. While it is on its way up, the whole clock is moving sideways with The Van, so by the time the light reaches the top mirror, the mirror is no longer directly above the place where the light set off: it has moved along. To hit it, the light, as The Station sees it, has to travel on a slant. On the way back down, the bottom mirror has moved on again, so the light slants down too. Relative to The Station, the light zigzags along the track, and a zigzag is longer than a straight up and down.

How fast does the light go along that longer path? Everyday intuition, with the ball and the train in mind, says faster: it should have The Van’s sideways speed added to it, as a ball thrown straight up in a moving train does. But the second journey showed that light can’t work like that. Everyone measures light moving at the same speed, whatever the motion of its source, and The Station measures this light at c too. Part of that speed has to go sideways, to keep up with the moving mirrors, so less of it is left for going up and down. (Walk diagonally across a room at your usual pace, and you reach the far wall later than someone walking straight across at the same pace: nobody slowed you down, but part of your walking went sideways.)

So, as The Station sees it, the light in The Van’s clock has further to go on every trip, at exactly the same speed as ever. Each trip must take longer. Every tick of The Van’s clock lasts longer, by The Station’s clocks, than a tick of The Station’s own identical clock. At eight-tenths of the speed of light the zigzag is two-thirds longer than the straight path, so each tick of The Van’s clock lasts two-thirds longer too. That is why, across the track, Albert’s clock counted 30 while The Station’s counted 50.

There is no mistake in either measurement. Relative to The Van, a tick is the ordinary tick it always was. Relative to The Station, the same tick is stretched.

The figure below shows the idea with a single flash of light. The two clocks start side by side, and one flash from where their bottom mirrors meet spreads out at the speed of light: a growing circle, the same in every direction. The Station’s top mirror, standing still, catches it first. The Van’s top mirror has moved on, so the same circle of light reaches it a little later. Every bounce sends out a new circle, and all of them grow at the same speed, but The Van’s light always has further to go, so its clock ticks less often. The arrow on each pulse is its speed, the same length for both; The Van’s tilts, because part of its speed goes sideways.

Speed The Van up and its light has further still to go. Slow it down and the two clocks tick almost together.

How much longer?

How much longer the slanting path is depends on one thing only: how fast The Van is going, compared with light. (The working, which needs only a right-angled triangle and Pythagoras’s theorem, is on the maths page.) At everyday speeds the slant is so slight that the difference is far too small to measure. At half the speed of light the path, and so the tick, is about 15 per cent longer; at eight-tenths, two-thirds longer; at 99 per cent, about seven times as long. As The Van’s speed gets closer and closer to light’s, the zigzag lies down flatter and flatter, and the stretch grows without limit.

That stretch, how many of The Station’s ticks one of The Van’s ticks lasts, has a name.

The Lorentz factor

The number that says how much a moving clock is slowed, as measured from a frame it moves through: one tick of the moving clock lasts that many ticks of the clocks at rest. It is usually written with the Greek letter γ, gamma, and that is the γ in the figures. In the light clock, it is how many times longer the light’s slanting path is than the straight one.

It depends only on the speed, as a fraction of the speed of light. It is 1 at rest, and so close to 1 at any everyday speed that the difference can’t be noticed. It is 1.25 at six-tenths of the speed of light, about 1.67 at eight-tenths and about 7 at 99 per cent, and it grows without limit as the speed approaches c. It is named after Hendrik Lorentz, who used it in his theory of the ether before Einstein’s paper of 1905.

The result, that a moving clock ticks slowly by the Lorentz factor as measured by the clocks it moves past, has a name of its own. So does the time a clock measures for itself.

Proper time

The time between two events measured by a single clock that is present at both of them: the clock that travels from one event to the other. Every clock measures its own proper time, and to the clock, and anyone travelling with it, that time is perfectly ordinary. The 30 microseconds on The Van’s light clock between the two gates is The Van’s proper time for the journey.

Time dilation

The stretching of time intervals between frames: a clock moving relative to an inertial frame ticks slowly, as that frame’s clocks measure it. If the moving clock measures a certain time between two events on its journey (its proper time), the frame it moves through measures that time multiplied by the Lorentz factor between the same two events, using two of its own clocks, one at each. “Dilation” means widening: the moving clock’s ticks are wider apart.

So The Station’s 50 microseconds are The Van’s 30, stretched by the Lorentz factor for eight-tenths of the speed of light.

Do other clocks slow down too?

At this point, a sensible objection is that we’ve only shown that light clocks run slow. Perhaps light clocks are odd. Perhaps a good mechanical watch, or John’s heart, or the kettle, would carry on at the normal rate.

They can’t, and the reason is the principle of relativity from the first journey. Suppose John took a quartz watch on The Van, alongside the light clock, and suppose the watch were immune to all this and kept The Station’s time. Then inside The Van the light clock and the watch would drift apart, by 30,000 ticks against 50,000, and John could watch them do it, without looking outside. By comparing the two, he could tell how fast The Van was moving, and the faster it went, the faster they would drift apart. That would be an experiment inside a closed, steadily moving van that revealed its motion, and the principle of relativity says there is no such experiment. So the watch must keep step with the light clock. So must every other clock in The Van: atomic clocks, pendulums, heartbeats, the chemistry of the doughnuts going stale, and the kettle, though nobody could say what it was boiling.

Time dilation, then, happens to every process in The Van, as The Station measures it; clocks are simply the processes we can read. If The Van kept going at eight-tenths of the speed of light for a while, a kettle that took three minutes to boil by The Van’s clocks would, by The Station’s clocks, take five. And John and Cal wouldn’t feel a thing. Their thoughts, their heartbeats and the kettle would all be slowed together, as The Station measures them, so that among themselves nothing would have changed at all. Three minutes of their lives would simply have passed, while The Station assigned five minutes to the same stretch of their journey.

This settles the observation that the first journey asked you to hold on to: that the doughnut’s throw across The Van took a second and a half, measured from The Van or from the probe outside. That was very nearly right. A throw is a process in The Van like any other, so by the probe’s clocks it was stretched by the Lorentz factor. The Van, though, was only drifting past the probe at about two metres a second, where the Lorentz factor is so close to 1 that the difference was far too small for any clock on The Van or the probe to show, least of all the one on the dashboard. But it isn’t zero.

But isn’t The Station moving too?

On the way back, Cal found the obvious objection.

“Hang on,” he said. “We’ve just spent two days learning that nobody gets to say who’s really moving. If we’re moving past The Station at eight-tenths of the speed of light, The Station’s moving past us at eight-tenths of the speed of light. So doesn’t the same argument work the other way? Doesn’t our side say The Station’s clocks are slow?”

“Yes,” said John.

“Then surely that’s impossible. They can’t both be slow.”

Albert’s voice crackled over the radio. Nobody had given him the frequency. “A considerably more interesting problem,” he said. “But don’t steal a later adventure.”

Cal is right, and so is John. Go back to the light clock figure and press As The Van sees it. Now it’s The Station’s clock that moves past, its light that zigzags, and its ticks that are stretched. The figure is completely symmetrical, because the situation is. Each frame measures the other’s clocks running slow.

How can that not be a contradiction? Notice what each measurement actually involves. When The Station measured The Van’s clock, it used two of its own clocks, one at each gate, and trusted that they were in step. To measure The Station’s clock the same way, The Van would need a line of its own clocks, in step with each other, moving along with it. And, as the next journey finds, two lines of clocks, moving relative to each other, cannot agree about which clocks are in step. Clocks that are perfectly in step as The Station sees them are out of step as The Van sees them, and the other way round. Each side’s measurement of the other is correct, by its own clocks; each side thinks the other’s clocks were set wrongly. Until the two clocks are brought back together in one place, the question “whose clock is really behind?” has no answer, any more than “who is really moving?” had one on the first journey.

And if they are brought back together? Then one of them must have turned round, which means accelerating, and the two are no longer on equal terms. That is the sixth journey, and it is where time dilation stops being a matter of description and starts leaving people different ages.

We have actually measured it

So far, all of this has happened in an imaginary van. Outside it, time dilation has been measured many times, in many ways, and it has always come out as the light clock says.

Why so many muons reach the ground

High in the atmosphere, about fifteen kilometres up, cosmic rays (fast particles from space) smash into the nuclei of air atoms, and the debris includes particles called muons. A muon is a heavy cousin of the electron, about 207 times as massive, discovered in 1936. It is also unstable: on average, a muon at rest lives for about 2.2 microseconds before it decays into an electron and two neutrinos. Like radioactive decay, the moment of decay is random; 2.2 microseconds is the average.

In 2.2 microseconds, even light only travels about 660 metres. The muons from cosmic rays are travelling at very nearly the speed of light, but even so, by everyday reasoning, very few should get much more than a few kilometres below where they were made before decaying. Of all the muons made fifteen kilometres up, practically none should reach the ground: fewer than one in a billion.

Yet muons pour down on the ground all the time. Roughly one passes through each square centimetre of the Earth’s surface every minute, through you included: through the palm of your hand, more than once a second.

Time dilation explains it. A muon carrying a typical energy is moving so fast that its Lorentz factor is in the tens, so its 2.2 microseconds of life, as measured by clocks on the ground, are stretched to tens of microseconds, plenty of time to fall fifteen kilometres. By their own reckoning, the muons live their ordinary 2.2 microseconds, on average; it is the Earth’s clocks that measure those microseconds as long ones.

After a first measurement by Bruno Rossi and David Hall in Colorado in 1941, David Frisch and James Smith did a cleaner version in 1963. They counted the muons arriving at the top of Mount Washington in New Hampshire, nearly two kilometres up, and then the muons arriving at sea level. The trip down takes those muons several of their average lifetimes, so without time dilation only about one in twenty should have survived it. In fact nearly three-quarters did. Their clocks were running slow, as the ground measured them, by just the amount relativity predicts.

What does a muon make of all this? Its own clock runs perfectly normally: as far as it is concerned, it lives its ordinary 2.2 microseconds. How it still gets to the ground in time is a question for the fifth journey, which finds that distances, too, depend on who measures them.

The figure below drops the same batch of muons from fifteen kilometres up, twice over, side by side, and above each column it puts one muon’s own clock: once round its face is a muon’s average lifetime. On the left, their clocks keep the same time as ours, as everyday experience says they should. The clock whirls round again and again during the fall, and the muons vanish one by one, long before the ground. On the right, their clocks run slow as the ground measures them, just as the light clock’s did. The clock turns only about one and a half times, and a good share of the same muons reach the ground.

The most precise muon measurement of all came in 1977, at CERN in Geneva, where muons were sent racing round a ring at 99.94 per cent of the speed of light. Muons at rest live 2.2 microseconds on average; these lived about 29 times as long, just as relativity predicts at that speed. Going round in a circle meant they were being accelerated enormously, and it made no difference: only their speed mattered, a result the sixth journey relies on.

The map on your phone

You probably carry the best evidence of all in your pocket. The map on your phone finds you by timing. Each satellite of the Global Positioning System carries an atomic clock and broadcasts the time, and your phone works out how far away each satellite is from how long its signal took to arrive. The signals travel at the speed of light, about 300 metres in a millionth of a second, so a satellite clock that is out by a millionth of a second puts your position out by hundreds of metres.

The satellites travel at about 3.9 kilometres a second, and from their speed alone their clocks run slow by about 7 millionths of a second a day, as measured from the ground. If nobody allowed for that, the dot on your map would drift by about two kilometres in a single day, and by another two the next day, and the next. After a week, it would put you in the wrong town.

There is a second effect, from the satellites’ height above the Earth, which pulls the other way and is even bigger: it belongs to the ninth journey, which tells the whole story. Left uncorrected, the two together would put your map out by about eleven kilometres, some seven miles, every day. The system’s engineers allow for both, and your phone finds the right street.

The effect has been measured directly too. In October 1971, Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating took four atomic clocks round the world on ordinary airliners, once flying eastwards and once westwards, and compared them with the clocks they had left at home. The clocks that flew east came back a little behind, and the ones that flew west a little ahead: by billionths of a second, but just as relativity predicted. (Westbound clocks gain, rather than lose, partly because of the Earth’s spin, and partly because a clock higher up runs faster, the ninth journey’s subject again.) Today’s clocks are so good that they can see it at the speed of a bicycle.

None of this is clocks being imperfect. Relativity predicts exactly how differently they run, from nothing but their speed (and, as we’ll see, their height), and every experiment so far has agreed.

Back to Teide

The Van came home by a deliberately short route. The kettle had stopped boiling whatever it had been boiling, and was now making a faint noise like a disappointed owl.

Albert had already made fresh coffees. Beside them, on the counter, lay a long strip of paper covered in numbers.

“The Station sent its log,” he said.

Nobody asked how.

“How much time passed for you,” he asked, “between the two gates?”

“Thirty microseconds,” said John. “On your clock.”

Albert ran a finger down the log. “And fifty, says The Station, for the same two events.”

“So the clock lied,” said Cal.

“Your clock,” said Albert, “behaved perfectly. It told you exactly how much time passed for it, and for you, and for the doughnuts. That is all any clock can do.” He paused. “Your kettle, on the other hand, I would not trust with anything.”

Cal frowned. “I can accept the numbers,” he said. “I still don’t like it.”

“Nature,” said Albert, “has never asked for approval.”

The plateau was almost motionless in the cooling evening. A glass cup clicked against its saucer. John listened to his watch tick. The sound was exactly as it had been that morning. A second was a second, to the watch on his wrist, and to him.

But somewhere, travelling differently, another clock could make a different and equally honest accounting of the same arrival and departure.

Cal pointed at a bright star rising beyond the ridge.

“All right,” he said. “Then explain this. The Station’s clocks were all in step. They all said now at the same moment, all the way down that girder. How do clocks in two different places agree about what’s happening now?”

Albert looked pleased.

“Ah,” he said. “They don’t always.”
