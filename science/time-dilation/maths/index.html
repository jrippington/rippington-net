The light clock’s triangle

Let the mirrors be a distance hhh apart, measured in the clock’s own frame. Relative to The Van, the light goes straight up, so one upward trip takes

τ=hc.\tau = \frac{h}{c}.τ=ch​.

Relative to The Station, suppose the same upward trip takes a time ttt, while The Van moves sideways at speed vvv. In that time the clock moves sideways by vtvtvt, and the light, moving at ccc along its slanting path, goes a distance ctctct. The mirrors’ separation is still hhh, because the motion is sideways. These three distances make a right-angled triangle, and Pythagoras gives

(ct)2=h2+(vt)2.(ct)^2 = h^2 + (vt)^2.(ct)2=h2+(vt)2.

Gather the terms in ttt on one side:

t2(c2−v2)=h2,t=hc2−v2=h/c1−v2/c2.\begin{gathered} t^2 (c^2 - v^2) = h^2, \\ t = \frac{h}{\sqrt{c^2 - v^2}} = \frac{h/c}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}. \end{gathered}t2(c2−v2)=h2,t=c2−v2​h​=1−v2/c2​h/c​.​

The top of the last fraction is τ\tauτ, the time in The Van’s frame. So

t=γτ,γ=11−v2/c2.t = \gamma \tau, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}.t=γτ,γ=1−v2/c2​1​.

This γ\gammaγ (the Greek letter gamma) is the Lorentz factor, and τ\tauτ (tau) is the clock’s proper time: the time between two events measured by the one clock that is present at both. Time dilation, in symbols, is t=γτt = \gamma\taut=γτ.

At eight-tenths of the speed of light, v2/c2=0.64v^2/c^2 = 0.64v2/c2=0.64, so the square root is 0.36=0.6\sqrt{0.36} = 0.60.36​=0.6, and γ=1/0.6=5/3\gamma = 1/0.6 = 5/3γ=1/0.6=5/3. A tick that takes 1 nanosecond aboard The Van takes 5/3, about 1.67, nanoseconds of station time; 30 microseconds aboard takes 50.

With the article’s numbers, taking light’s speed as 30 centimetres a nanosecond: the mirrors are h=15h = 15h=15 centimetres apart, so τ=h/c\tau = h/cτ=h/c is half a nanosecond. At v=0.8cv = 0.8cv=0.8c, γ=5/3\gamma = 5/3γ=5/3, and the upward trip takes t=γτ=5/6t = \gamma\tau = 5/6t=γτ=5/6 of a nanosecond of station time. In that time

Time dilation at everyday speeds

At everyday speeds, γ\gammaγ is so close to 1 that it is easier to use the approximation

γ≈1+v22c2.\gamma \approx 1 + \frac{v^2}{2c^2}.γ≈1+2c2v2​.

For an airliner at 250 metres a second, v2/2c2v^2/2c^2v2/2c2 is about 3.5×10−133.5 \times 10^{-13}3.5×10−13: on a ten-hour flight, a clock on board falls behind one on the ground by about 12 nanoseconds from speed alone, if we ignore gravity and the Earth’s spin. Which is why nobody noticed for a few thousand years.

The same approximation gives the article’s other small numbers.

The doughnut’s throw, on the first journey. The Van drifted past the probe at about 2 metres a second, so

v22c2=221.80×1017≈2.2×10−17,\frac{v^2}{2c^2} = \frac{2^2}{1.80 \times 10^{17}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-17},2c2v2​=1.80×101722​≈2.2×10−17,

and γ=1.00000000000000002\gamma = 1.00000000000000002γ=1.00000000000000002, to seventeen decimal places. Over the throw’s 1.5 seconds, the probe’s clocks measure about 1.5×2.2×10−17≈3.3×10−171.5 \times 2.2 \times 10^{-17} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-17}1.5×2.2×10−17≈3.3×10−17 seconds more than The Van’s: about thirty billionths of a billionth of a second.
