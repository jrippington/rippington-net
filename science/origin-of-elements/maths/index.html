Carbon’s state

The ladder. Measured from carbon-12’s lowest state, three helium nuclei at rest have 7.2747 MeV more energy; a nucleus of beryllium-8 and one of helium, 7.3666 MeV (beryllium-8 is 91.8 keV heavier than two heliums, which is why it falls apart). The state Hoyle predicted, now called the Hoyle state, is at

E∗=7.6542 MeV,E^* = 7.6542\ \text{MeV},E∗=7.6542 MeV,

so it sits Er=7.6542−7.2747=0.3795E_r = 7.6542 - 7.2747 = 0.3795Er​=7.6542−7.2747=0.3795 MeV, about 380,000 electronvolts, above three heliums at rest (and 288 keV above beryllium-8 and a helium). Hoyle’s prediction of 1953 was “near 7.68”.

In a giant’s core, at about 10810^8108 K and 10510^5105 grams to the cubic centimetre, pairs form and fall apart so fast that a balance is struck, with about one beryllium-8 for every billion heliums.

The rate. The thermostat. A star doesn’t make carbon at a set temperature: it heats or cools its core until it burns helium as fast as it must. To make carbon at a real giant’s rate with the state 100 keV higher, at the same density, the core would need

129 million degrees. Carbon and helium make oxygen at a rate that climbs steeply with temperature too (Caughlan and Fowler’s fit goes as T9−2e−32.12 T9−1/3T_9^{-2} e^{-32.12\, T_9^{-1/3}}T9−2​e−32.12T9−1/3​, roughly), and at 129 million degrees it’s about 170 times faster than at 100 million, so far more of the carbon would be burned. With no Hoyle state at all, the next state up (more than two million electronvolts higher) is all there is, and the core would need about 670 million degrees, where carbon becomes oxygen trillions of times faster than in a real giant.

What’s left. How much carbon a star leaves depends on the race between these two rates through the whole of its helium burning, and on how the core is stirred, and that needs a model of the whole star. Such models (Oberhummer, Csótó and Schlattl, 2000; Schlattl and others, 2004) find that a real giant’s core ends its helium about a third carbon and two-thirds oxygen, and that shifting the Hoyle state by about 100 keV, about 1.3 per cent of its energy, leaves far less carbon (if it’s higher) or far less oxygen (if it’s lower).

Oxygen’s near miss. Oxygen-16 has a state at 7.117 MeV, just below the 7.162 MeV that a carbon-12 and a helium bring at rest, so it can’t act as a resonance for carbon and helium in a star, and carbon is turned into oxygen only slowly.

Radioactive iron

An exploding star makes its iron first as nickel-56, a nucleus of 28 protons and 28 neutrons, which is the natural end of burning silicon. It isn’t stable. It decays to cobalt-56 with a half-life of 6.08 days, and cobalt-56 decays to iron-56 with a half-life of 77.2 days. The decays give out gamma rays, which heat the debris, which glows.

After the first few weeks, when the nickel has gone, the light is powered by cobalt decaying, and so it fades exactly as the cobalt does:

2.5log⁡10e×100111.4≈0.98 magnitudes every 100 days.\frac{2.5 \log_{10} e \times 100}{111.4} \approx 0.98\ \text{magnitudes every 100 days}.111.42.5log10​e×100​≈0.98 magnitudes every 100 days.

SN 1987A’s light faded at just that rate from about 120 days after the explosion to about 800, and its brightness then says it made about 0.07 Suns of nickel-56; the Solar Maximum Mission satellite caught cobalt-56’s gamma-ray lines, at 847 and 1,238 thousand electronvolts. A white dwarf exploding makes far more, about 0.6 Suns, which is why such explosions are so bright, and why they make most of the universe’s iron.

How fast neutrons are caught

A nucleus catches neutrons at a rate λ=nnσv\lambda = n_n \sigma vλ=nn​σv, where nnn_nnn​ is the number of free neutrons in each cubic centimetre, σ\sigmaσ the nucleus’s target area for catching one (its cross-section), and vvv the neutrons’ speed.

Slowly, in giants. In the helium-burning shells of dying small stars, reactions such as carbon-13 with helium free about nn≈107n_n \approx 10^7nn​≈107 to 10810^8108 neutrons in each cubic centimetre. At about 100 million degrees a neutron moves at about v≈1.2×108v \approx 1.2 \times 10^8v≈1.2×108 cm a second, and typical cross-sections are about σ≈10−25\sigma \approx 10^{-25}σ≈10−25 cm² (a tenth of a barn). Then

λ≈108×10−25×1.2×108≈1.2×10−9 a second,\lambda \approx 10^8 \times 10^{-25} \times 1.2 \times 10^8 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{a second},λ≈108×10−25×1.2×108≈1.2×10−9 a second,

one neutron every 1/λ≈8×1081/\lambda \approx 8 \times 10^81/λ≈8×108 seconds, about 25 years (every few years to centuries, as the numbers vary). An unstable nucleus made on the way usually turns a neutron into a proton within minutes, days or years, long before the next neutron arrives, so the path keeps close to the stable nuclei: this is the s-process, for slow.

Technetium. Its longest-lived forms, technetium-97 and -98, have half-lives of about 4.2 million years, and technetium-99, the one made in giants, 211,000 years. A red giant is billions of years old, so any technetium in it when it formed would long since have gone: by (1/2)t/T(1/2)^{t/T}(1/2)t/T, after even a hundred million years, (1/2)100/0.211≈10−143(1/2)^{100/0.211} \approx 10^{-143}(1/2)100/0.211≈10−143 of technetium-99 would be left. Seeing its lines, as Merrill did in 1952, means it was made in the star, recently.

Suddenly, in collisions. In the neutron-rich debris of colliding neutron stars, nnn_nnn​ is perhaps 102010^{20}1020 to 102410^{24}1024 in each cubic centimetre, and the rate is trillions of times greater, or more: a nucleus catches a neutron every microsecond or so, far faster than it can decay, and piles up dozens or hundreds in a second before the flood ends. This is the r-process, for rapid, which makes the heaviest elements, gold, platinum and uranium among them.
