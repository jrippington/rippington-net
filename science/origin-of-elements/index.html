Cal unfolds the napkin marked DO NOT EAT and asks where the grain of sugar's atoms came from. The hydrogen is as old as the universe. The carbon is harder, because the universe's first minutes couldn't make it, and Fred, in for tea, explains with great enjoyment how he worked out that it could only be made one way, and was proved right within months. By evening the gold, the iron and the calcium have been accounted for too.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 11 of 18

The grain on the table

In the morning, before anyone had ordered anything, Cal took the napkin out of his shirt pocket and unfolded it on the table, very carefully, the way Albert had once lifted the glass off it. The words were still on the outside, in Albert’s handwriting.

DO NOT EAT

Inside, in the crease, was the grain of sugar: the same grain, glittering and slightly crooked, that Cal had licked off his finger under the stars at the end of the relativity series, and that they had spent the whole of the last series going down into, through its crystal and its molecules and its atoms to the fields at the bottom of everything.

“Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen,” said Cal. “Twelve, twenty-two and eleven to a molecule. We know what they are. Where did they come from?”

“The hydrogen’s easy,” said John. “The third journey. It’s what the oven didn’t bake: a proton on its own since the first three minutes, and nothing’s changed it since. Every hydrogen atom in that grain is about as old as the universe.”

He was right, and it’s one of the stranger things there is to know about a grain of sugar: twenty-two of every forty-five atoms in it are 13.8 billion years old, older than the Sun, the Earth and the galaxy, left over from the third journey’s oven, which cooled the moment it was opened.

“And the rest?”

“The rest is harder,” said John. “The oven stopped at helium. There’s no stable nucleus with five particles in it, or eight, so there was nothing to step on to get any further. Yesterday the stars burned helium into carbon in their cores. But they’d have the same problem. Two heliums make eight particles, and eight falls apart.”

“So how do the stars do it?”

Albert came over with a pot of tea, which nobody had ordered either, and set it down beside the grain. It was very strong. He looked at the door.

“Ask him,” he said.

Fred

Fred came in out of the bright morning with his coat over his arm and an air of having been looking forward to something.

He was Albert’s other old friend: blunt, funny, brilliant and contrary, the one who had come for tea twice before in this series, once in the second journey to pour scorn on the whole idea of a beginning, and once in the fourth, when the universe’s oldest light had shown that there was one and he had declined to be impressed. He took his tea as he took everything, strong, and he took it now, with a nod to Albert, and sat down opposite the grain of sugar as if it were an old acquaintance.

“Carbon,” he said. “Albert tells me you want to know about carbon.”

“It’s for the sugar,” said Cal.

“It’s for everything,” said Fred. “Carbon is the fourth commonest element there is. For every ten thousand atoms of hydrogen in the Sun there are about three of carbon. That’s a great deal of carbon. It’s in you, it’s in the soot on Albert’s fryer, it’s in the stars’ own light. And your big bang,” he added, to John, “didn’t make any of it.”

“It’s not my big bang,” said John. “You named it.”

“On the radio, in 1949. And I didn’t mean it rudely.” He drank some tea. “Nobody believes me.”

Nobody did, quite. On 28 March 1949, on the BBC’s Third Programme, Fred had called the idea of a universe beginning in a hot, dense state “this big bang idea”, and the name had stuck to it ever since, to his lasting irritation. He had his own idea, worked out the year before with Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold: a steady universe, the same at every time, with no beginning, new matter appearing slowly to fill the gaps as it expanded. It was a fine theory, honestly argued, and the oldest light had done for it. Fred knew the evidence as well as anyone at the table. He still didn’t like it.

“But that’s why I know about carbon,” he said. “If the universe had no hot beginning, then every element heavier than hydrogen had to be made somewhere else. In stars. All of it. So somebody had to work out how, and I thought it might as well be me.”

Three heliums

“The trouble,” said Fred, when Albert had told him what John had said, “is exactly that. In the core of a red giant, at a hundred million degrees, helium nuclei are crashing into each other all the time. Two of them stick, and make beryllium-8, with eight particles. And beryllium-8 falls apart again almost at once.”

“How soon?” said Cal.

“In less than a ten-millionth of a billionth of a second.”

It has been measured: about eight hundred-millionths of a billionth of a second, a time so short that light could cross only about a hundred atoms in it. Edwin Salpeter had already found part of a way out, in 1951. In the heat and crush of a giant’s core, two heliums are meeting so often that, even though each pair falls apart at once, there’s always a tiny number of pairs in existence at any moment, about one for every billion heliums. And if a third helium happens to hit one of them in that instant, the three together have the twelve particles of carbon.

“So that’s it,” said John. “Three at once.”

“That’s what Salpeter said,” said Fred. “And I did the sums, and it doesn’t work. It’s far too slow. Three heliums don’t stick just because they’ve met. They arrive with a certain amount of energy between them, and a carbon nucleus can only take that energy in if it has somewhere to put it.”

He looked at John and Cal as if they ought to know what came next, and they did, because they had spent a whole journey on it. In Why the Atom Survives, John and Cal found that an atom’s electrons can only hold certain amounts of energy, on certain rungs of a ladder, and that’s why each element’s light comes in its own sharp colours. A nucleus is the same. Its protons and neutrons are waves held in by the strong force, and they too can only be in certain states, each with its own energy. A nucleus hit with energy matching one of its states takes it in readily, as a wine glass takes up a note and rings with it. Hit with any other energy, it can’t hold it, and the pieces fly apart.

It’s the same as pushing a child on a swing. Push at the swing’s own rhythm, and every push adds to the last, and the swing goes higher and higher. Push at any other rhythm and the pushes fight each other, and nothing much happens, however hard you push. Physicists call it resonance: something with a natural rhythm of its own takes up energy readily only at that rhythm. For a nucleus, the rhythm is an energy, and its states are its natural rhythms.

Try it

If you have a piano, hold down its right-hand pedal, which lifts the felt dampers off all the strings, and sing a single loud note into it, close to the strings. Then stop. The piano goes on singing your note back to you, quietly: the strings tuned to it, and to its higher harmonics, took up the energy of your voice, and the rest stayed silent. A carbon nucleus with a state at the energy three heliums bring is a string tuned to their note.

“So the three heliums only stick,” said Cal slowly, “if carbon has a state at just the energy they bring.”

“If not,” said Fred, “they bounce, and the pair falls apart, and there’s no carbon. Or so little that a star would have to be far hotter to make any. And in a star that hot, the carbon would be eaten as fast as it was made, turned into oxygen by one more helium. No carbon to speak of. No soot, no sugar, no you.”

He put his cup down.

“But there is carbon. Lots of it. So I said: carbon must have a state, at just about the right energy, about seven point seven million electronvolts above its lowest. Nobody had seen one there. It didn’t matter. It had to be there, or there wouldn’t be any carbon.”

Kellogg

That was in early 1953, when Fred was spending some months at the California Institute of Technology. Across the campus was the Kellogg Radiation Laboratory, where William Fowler and his colleagues spent their days firing nuclei at one another with machines, and measuring exactly the states nuclei have. Fred went and asked them to look.

“They thought I was mad,” he said with satisfaction. “A cosmologist, of all things, walking in off the street to tell them what was in their own nuclei. Willy Fowler didn’t believe a word of it.”

But a young physicist in the laboratory, Ward Whaling, was willing to look, and he and his group set up an experiment. Within months they had found it: a state of carbon at 7.65 million electronvolts, almost exactly where Fred had said. A few years later Fowler’s own group showed that the state could indeed be made from beryllium-8 and a helium, as the stars needed. Fowler stopped being sceptical, and spent the rest of his career working out how the stars make the elements, much of it with Fred.

The simulator, which had been listening from the dashboard through the open window, felt it ought to say something.

FRED’S ACCOUNT: OBSERVED. MOSTLY

Fred looked at it for a long time.

“It was very nearly seven point seven,” he said.

“He was right once,” said Albert, to nobody in particular.

“Once,” said Fred, “is all it takes.”

It’s a famous prediction, one of the very few times anyone has worked out a fact about a nucleus from looking at the stars and had a laboratory prove it right. And a story has grown up round it since, that Fred predicted the state because life needs carbon: that since we’re here, made of carbon, the state had to exist. It’s a lovely story, and the historian Helge Kragh has shown that it’s a later gloss. The connection with life was made only much later, and became popular in the 1980s. Fred’s argument in 1953 was the carbon in the stars, measured in their light, and it was that argument the laboratory tested.

“Because life needs carbon?” said Cal, who had read the lovely story.

“Because there’s carbon,” said Fred. “Life came later.”

Put together, Fred’s argument has six steps, and each one has been met before.

  • There’s a great deal of carbon in the universe: about one atom of it for every two of oxygen, in the light of the Sun and the stars.
  • The first minutes made none: the oven stopped at helium, at the gap at eight (the third journey).
  • So it was made in stars, from helium, the only fuel there’s enough of.
  • In a star, it can only be made by three heliums meeting, two of which fall apart almost at once.
  • Three heliums meeting with just any energy fly apart again, so carbon would be made far too slowly, except in a star so hot that it would burn its carbon into oxygen as fast as it made it.
  • So carbon must have a state at the energy three heliums bring in a giant’s core.

The only way out is another road to carbon, and there isn’t one. Lithium, beryllium and boron, the elements between helium and carbon, are so fragile that stars destroy them, not build on them, and there are only traces of them anywhere. Carbon has to be made from helium, three at a time, and the state has to be there.

Higher, and carbon is made far more slowly; a star would have to run hotter to make it, and the table says how much faster it would then burn its carbon into oxygen. Lower, and carbon comes easily, but little of it goes on to oxygen. Try Fred’s prediction, and try taking the state away altogether.

For the mathematically inclinedCarbon’s state

There was a second piece of luck, which Fred noticed too. Once there’s carbon, one more helium can turn it into oxygen, and if oxygen had a state at just the energy a carbon and a helium bring, it would do so readily, and the carbon would all go. Oxygen does have a state nearby. It’s just below the energy they bring, not on it, so it can’t help, and carbon and helium make oxygen only slowly. The ash a giant’s core is left with, when its helium is gone, is about a third carbon and two-thirds oxygen. How finely all this is balanced has been worked out since in models of stars: move carbon’s state up or down by about a hundred thousand electronvolts, a little over one per cent of its energy, and the universe would have far less carbon, or far less oxygen.

Stellar nucleosynthesis

In 1957 Fred, Fowler and the astronomers Margaret and Geoffrey Burbidge published a paper more than a hundred pages long, called simply “Synthesis of the Elements in Stars”. It set out, element by element, how the stars could make nearly everything heavier than helium. In Canada, the same year, Alastair Cameron worked much of it out on his own. Astronomers still call the four authors’ paper by their initials, B²FH, and it’s the founding document of the subject.

Stellar nucleosynthesis

The making of the chemical elements inside stars. In their cores, stars fuse light nuclei into heavier ones: hydrogen into helium, helium into carbon and oxygen, and in the heaviest stars on through neon, magnesium, silicon and sulphur to iron. Elements heavier than iron are built up by catching neutrons, slowly in ageing giants and suddenly in the most violent events there are. Dying stars throw what they made back into space, where new stars form from it. Every element but hydrogen, most of the helium and a little lithium was made this way.

William Fowler shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for the work in 1983, with Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, whose voyage the last journey followed. Fred didn’t, which many of his colleagues thought unjust. When Cal asked him about it, he said only that the tea was stewing nicely.

There’s a twist in the tale, and Fred told it against himself. Stars make helium too: every star does, burning its hydrogen. But a few years after B²FH, measurements began to show that there’s far too much helium in the universe, about a quarter of all ordinary matter, for the stars to have made it in the time there’s been. In 1964, as the third journey heard, Fred and a young colleague, Roger Tayler, did the sum the other way. They worked out how much helium a hot, dense beginning would make, and got a third or more, about as much as the measurements of the day suggested.

“I didn’t like the answer,” said Fred. “We published it anyway.”

“And then?”

“And then I looked for somewhere else hot enough to make it.” He smiled into his tea. “I’m still looking.”

So the stars made everything from carbon on, as Fred said, and the hot beginning made the hydrogen and most of the helium, which he never accepted. The third journey’s oven and this journey’s stars are the two halves of the same answer.

Oxygen, and The Van’s iron

Most of the oxygen in the grain of sugar wasn’t made in a star like the Sun. A small star turns its helium into carbon and oxygen, but at the end, as the last journey found, its core is left behind as a white dwarf, and most of what it made is locked in there for good. What a small star throws out is its outer layers, with some of the carbon and nitrogen that’s been stirred up into them from below.

Oxygen is thrown out by the heavy stars. In the last journey, a star of 25 Suns burned through its fuels in shells, one after another, each faster than the last: helium into carbon and oxygen, carbon into neon and magnesium, oxygen into silicon and sulphur, silicon into iron. When its core collapsed, the blast threw everything outside the core into space: a Sun’s worth of oxygen or more from a single star, with the neon, the magnesium, the silicon, the sulphur and the calcium. The oxygen in every breath John and Cal took was made like that, and so was the calcium in their bones.

And iron. Some of the iron in The Van’s bodywork was made in those same explosions, in the last moments, as the shock wave raced through the star. Most of it, probably about two-thirds, came another way: from white dwarfs, the dead cores of small stars, exploding.

A white dwarf on its own just cools. But many stars are born in pairs, and a white dwarf with a companion can pull gas off it, or eventually merge with it, and grow. As it nears Chandrasekhar’s limit, the carbon and oxygen it’s made of are squeezed hot enough to fuse, all at once, through the whole star, and there’s nothing to stop it: a white dwarf’s push doesn’t come from heat, so it can’t swell and cool to put the fire out, as an ordinary star’s thermostat would. In a second or two the whole star burns, and is blown apart, leaving nothing behind. About half a Sun of its carbon and oxygen ends up as nickel-56, a radioactive kind of nickel, which within months has decayed into iron.

How could anyone know that? The light of the explosion says so. A supernova’s light, after the first few weeks, fades at a steady pace, and the pace is exactly the half-life of cobalt-56, 77 days, the step between that radioactive nickel and iron. SN 1987A, the supernova of the last journey, did exactly that for two years, and satellites caught the gamma rays of cobalt-56 decaying, coming from its wreck. The light of a supernova, for most of the months it shines, is radioactive nickel turning into iron, the half-life of Through the Wall written across the sky.

The stars stop at iron because iron is where fusion stops paying. Every nucleus weighs a little less than the protons and neutrons it’s made of, and that missing mass is the energy given out in making it, by E = mc². Divide by the number of particles, and the energy given up for each one tells you how tightly they’re held. From hydrogen it rises steeply: most of all the energy fusion can ever give is given in the first step, hydrogen to helium, which is what makes hydrogen such good fuel. After that it rises more slowly, to iron and nickel, the most tightly held of all. Beyond iron it falls again, gently: a uranium nucleus holds its particles less tightly than iron does, which is why splitting uranium, in the power station of The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe, gives out energy too. Both run downhill, towards iron.

Beyond iron

“Then where does the gold come from?” said John. “If you can’t fuse past iron.”

“You don’t fuse,” said Fred. “You feed it neutrons.”

Fusing a nucleus with another means pushing two positive charges together, up the electric hill of Through the Wall, and for big nuclei, with dozens of protons, the hill is enormous. But a neutron has no charge. There’s no hill for it to climb. Any nucleus, however big, will take in a neutron that wanders close enough, and as it takes in more, it gets heavier, of the same element. Then, with too many neutrons, it isn’t stable any more, and sooner or later one of them turns into a proton, by the weak force the Sun’s slow burning depends on. With one more proton, it’s the next element up.

Neutron capture

The way elements heavier than iron are made: a nucleus takes in a neutron, which, having no charge, isn’t pushed away, and becomes a heavier form of the same element; when it has too many neutrons, one turns into a proton, and it becomes the next element up. It happens slowly in ageing giant stars, a neutron every few years or decades, and suddenly, dozens in a second, in collisions of neutron stars, where neutrons are packed far more densely. Gold, platinum and uranium are made the sudden way.

There are two ways it happens, and they make different elements. In the shells of dying small stars, at the second giant stage of the last journey, neutrons are freed by reactions in the burning helium, and a nucleus there catches one only every few years or decades. That’s slow enough that any unstable nucleus it makes has time to turn its extra neutron into a proton first, so the elements are built up one careful step at a time, through strontium, barium and lead. Proof came in 1952, when Paul Merrill, at Mount Wilson, found the lines of technetium in the light of some red giants. No form of technetium lasts longer than a few million years, far less than those stars’ ages. It can’t have been there when they were born. It must have been made inside them, recently, and stirred up to their surfaces: the first sight of a star making an element.

The other way is sudden. Pack neutrons densely enough and a nucleus catches them one after another, dozens in a second, far faster than it can turn any into protons, until it’s bloated with neutrons. Then, when the flood stops, they turn into protons in a rush, and the nuclei settle as gold, platinum, europium and uranium. It needs a place with more free neutrons than almost anywhere in the universe, and there’s a natural candidate: two neutron stars colliding.

For the mathematically inclinedHow fast neutrons are caught

Echoes of Spacetime told how one was caught: on 17 August 2017 the gravitational-wave detectors heard two neutron stars spiral together and collide, 130 million light-years away, and telescopes found the glowing wreckage, a kilonova, shining with the heat of freshly made radioactive heavy elements. Its light reddened over the following days as heavy atoms in it soaked up the bluer colours, and in 2019 astronomers found in its spectrum the lines of strontium, made, as far as anyone could see, right there. By one estimate that single collision made several times the Earth’s mass in gold.

“Most of it, as far as anyone can tell,” said Albert. “Whether some came from rarer kinds of exploding star is still argued.”

Every generation richer

So each generation of stars makes elements and gives them back. The heavy stars explode within a few million years of being born, the small stars shed their outer layers after billions, the white dwarfs and the neutron stars go off when their partners let them, and all of it goes back into the gas between the stars, to be swept up into the next clouds, and the next stars.

You can see it in the stars themselves. The oldest, like the stars of M13 in the last journey, have only a fortieth of the Sun’s share of iron, because they were made from gas that few stars had been through. The Sun, made later, has more; stars being born now, more still. And you can see the gas being enriched, in John’s pictures.

In his picture of the Eastern Veil, in Cygnus, the blast wave of a star that exploded about 20,000 years ago is still running into clouds of gas, and where it hits them, the gas glows: a thin edge of oxygen’s blue-green, just ahead of hydrogen’s red. Most of that glowing oxygen isn’t the dead star’s own. It’s oxygen already in the gas between the stars, made by stars that died before it, and swept up and lit by the blast. The dead star’s own debris is inside the shell, too hot to glow in visible light at all, shining in X-rays at about seven million degrees; its make-up says the star was about fifteen times as heavy as the Sun.

In another of his pictures, of the Large Magellanic Cloud’s neighbourhood round the Tarantula, there’s a faint blue shell called N132D: the wreck of a heavy star which exploded about 2,500 years ago, as we see it, and there the blue really is the oxygen the star made and threw out.

Carbon leaves its own traces. The brightest star in John’s picture of the Crescent Nebula, RS Cygni, is a carbon star, a cool giant whose atmosphere has had so much carbon dredged up into it from inside that it soaks up blue light, which is why it looks so orange. And carbon made in stars and blown out of them doesn’t stay as single atoms. It makes soot, and grains of dust, and molecules: in John’s picture of the Iris Nebula, in Cepheus, the Spitzer Space Telescope found in 2010 the first firm sign, between the stars, of carbon’s footballs, the cages of sixty carbon atoms which, in One at a Time, went through two slits at once.

The history of a doughnut

Cal had been looking at the grain of sugar all this time. Now he looked at the plate of lemon doughnuts Albert had put down while Fred was talking, and asked the simulator the obvious question.

ORIGIN OF THIS DOUGHNUT: RECONSTRUCTED. LEMON: NOT YET ACCOUNTED FOR

“It’s a start,” said Albert.

The doughnut’s history is the grain’s, with additions. Its hydrogen is from the first three minutes. Its carbon is from giants’ cores, by Fred’s state, about half of it thrown out by small stars shedding their outer layers and half by heavy stars exploding. Its oxygen is from heavy stars’ last years. The salt in the café’s pot by the till, whose sodium made the yellow flame of the first quantum journey, is from heavy stars too, sodium and chlorine both. The iron in Albert’s fryer is mostly from white dwarfs that blew themselves apart. The lemon is a story for later.

The figure below is a periodic table of every element found in nature, from hydrogen to uranium, each square coloured by where the Sun’s share of it was made: the first minutes, cosmic rays breaking heavier atoms into lighter ones (the only way, as it happens, to make most of the beryllium and boron), dying small stars, exploding heavy stars, exploding white dwarfs and colliding neutron stars. Press any square, and the table says where that element came from; the grain of sugar’s three, The Van’s iron and a ring’s gold are marked, and have buttons of their own. Then play it, or move the slider, and run cosmic time from the first minutes to the day the Sun was born, 9.2 billion years later, and watch the table fill. Hydrogen and helium are there at once. Nothing else comes for a hundred and fifty million years, through the long night of the sixth journey, until the first stars. Then the heavy stars’ elements fill in fast, because heavy stars die young, and the small stars’ and the white dwarfs’ more slowly, because they take longer.

The shares in the figure follow the astronomer Jennifer Johnson’s periodic table of where the elements came from, and they’re the best current account, not the last word: where lithium comes from is still argued, and so is how much of the sudden neutron-catching happens in colliding neutron stars rather than somewhere rarer.

Back to Teide

Fred stayed for lunch, and for most of the afternoon, and argued with Albert about almost everything, with great pleasure on both sides. He had the long drive down to the coast ahead of him, or said he had. At the door he looked back at the grain of sugar, still on its napkin on the table.

“That novel of mine,” he said. “The cloud that turned out cleverer than all the astronomers put together.” He considered the grain. “That one has been through rather a lot of clouds.”

And then he was gone.

The sun was low over the western ridge. John and Cal sat on the terrace wall with Albert, and the grain of sugar lay on its open napkin on the wall between them.

“So by the time the Sun came along,” said John, “nine billion years after the beginning, the gas had been through stars over and over.”

“Generation after generation,” said Albert. “Heavy stars living a few million years each and exploding, small ones shedding their layers, white dwarfs going off, neutron stars colliding. Every time, a little more carbon, a little more oxygen, a little more iron.”

“So when it made the Sun,” said Cal, “it was already rich.”

“Rich enough,” said Albert, “for planets. And for sugar.”

Cal picked up the napkin and folded the grain back into it, with the words on the outside where he could see them, and put it in his shirt pocket.

“Can the simulator show us that?” he said. “The day the Sun was born?”

Albert looked at The Van, its dashboard glowing faintly in the car park.

“It has been waiting,” he said, “four and a half billion years.”
