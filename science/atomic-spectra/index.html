Why the Atom Survives

The notes an atom can play

On the first day, the rules said every atom in the grain of sugar should have collapsed in a flash. Today an old guitar with one string, a pinch of salt and the streetlights of La Palma show why it hasn't, and why atoms shine in only a few sharp colours: an electron in an atom is a wave held in, and a wave held in can only play certain notes.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Field Guide to the Very Small, part 8 of 16

The flame, again

Albert lit the gas ring behind the counter before he had even made the coffee. Then he took a pinch of salt from the pot by the till, held it over the blue flame for a moment, as if to make sure everyone was watching, and let it fall.

The flame flared yellow: not orange, not white, but a clear, pure yellow, exactly as it had on the evening of the very first day.

“On the first evening,” he said, “I asked you why salt makes it that colour, and only that colour. You said sodium. I said that was a name.” He turned the gas down. “Today you can have the reason.”

The grain of sugar was on its saucer at the end of the counter, under its upturned glass, with its napkin, DO NOT EAT. It had sat there through seven journeys now, through lumps of light and electrons going through two slits at once, through Niels’s tea and Albert’s box, and it hadn’t collapsed. That was the trouble.

On the first day, in A Grain of Sugar, John and Cal had found two things wrong with atoms, and nobody had explained either of them since.

The first was that atoms last. By every rule physics had in 1911, an atom built like a tiny solar system, its electron going round its nucleus as a planet goes round the Sun, can’t last. Going round in a circle is accelerating, and an accelerating electron sends out light, so it loses energy, sinks closer to the nucleus, goes round faster, sends out more and sinks faster still. The Van’s simulator had built one by those rules and watched it spiral in and vanish in about sixteen trillionths of a second: ATOM NOT FOUND. Yet here was the grain of sugar, made of atoms that had lasted, as far as anyone could tell, for billions of years.

The second was the colours. On its way in, that planet-style atom should have given out a rising whistle of light, every colour in turn. Real atoms, when they give out light at all, give out only a few sharp colours, always the same ones, with nothing in between. Hydrogen glows a deep red, a blue-green and two violets. Sodium, in Albert’s flame, glows yellow.

“Last night,” said Cal, “we got as far as this. Squeezing something makes it jiggle. If an electron fell into the nucleus, it would be squeezed into a space tens of thousands of times smaller than the atom, and it would have to jiggle enormously. So perhaps it can’t fall in at all.”

“Perhaps,” said Albert. “And the colours?”

Nobody had an answer to the colours.

“Then,” said Albert, “we need a musical instrument.”

He looked round the café, which had none, and then out of the window at The Van.

John and Cal went out to look. The Van had a locker under its back seat that nobody opened, because it was full of things nobody wanted and nobody could throw away: a single walking boot, a tide table for 2009, the handbook for a commercial dishwasher. At the bottom, under a folded tarpaulin, Cal found an old acoustic guitar, its varnish crazed, with five of its six strings gone. Nobody could remember whose it was.

The one string left was the second from the top, the A string. Cal brought it in and plucked it, and the café filled with a low, slightly mournful note.

“Perfect,” said Albert.

A string held at both ends

A guitar string is a wave held in. Pluck it, and a wave runs along it, from where you plucked to the bridge at one end and the nut at the other, where it’s held still. There it’s turned back, runs the other way, is turned back again at the other end, and so on, hundreds of times a second. Each trip adds to what’s already there.

Now think about what those trips do to one another. Suppose the wave is of some arbitrary length, nothing in particular to do with the string. After one trip there and back, it returns a little out of step with the wave that’s just setting off: its crests where the new wave’s troughs are, or somewhere in between. After two trips it’s out of step by twice as much, after three by three times, and soon, among all the trips piled on top of one another, there are as many crests as troughs at every point along the string. They cancel. A wave of that length washes itself out in a few trips, and the string barely moves.

But suppose the wave is exactly the right length: so that, after a trip there and back, it comes home exactly in step with itself, crest on crest. Then every trip adds to the last, and the string rings. Which lengths are those? The ones that fit: the ends are held still, so the wave must be still at both ends, and the only waves that are still at both ends are those with a whole number of half-waves along the string. One half-wave, the whole string swinging as one piece, is the lowest note the string can play. Two half-waves, the string swinging in two halves with a still point in the middle, is a note twice as high. Three half-waves, with two still points, is three times as high. And so on, up, but never in between.

Step by step:

  • The string is held still at both ends, so any wave on it is turned back again and again.
  • A wave of the wrong length comes back out of step with itself, and its trips cancel; it washes out.
  • Only a wave that comes back in step builds up and lasts, and that happens only when a whole number of half-waves fits between the ends.
  • So a string held at both ends can play only certain notes: its lowest, and two, three, four times that, and nothing between.

There’s no way round it, short of changing the string. A note between the allowed ones would mean a wave that doesn’t fit, and a wave that doesn’t fit cancels itself.

Standing wave

A wave held in, turned back and forth between ends or walls, that has settled into a steady pattern: it swings in place, with points along it that never move (the nodes) and points between that swing hardest, instead of travelling along. Only waves that fit can do it: on a string held at both ends, a whole number of half-waves. So anything held in can vibrate only in certain patterns, at certain frequencies, which is why a guitar string or an organ pipe plays particular notes and not the notes between.

Cal’s string, the guitar’s A string, is tuned so that its lowest note, one half-wave along its whole length, is 110 waves a second. Its other notes are 220, 330, 440 and so on: whole-number multiples, every one. (The fourth, 440, is the A an orchestra tunes to.) Musicians call them the string’s harmonics.

Try it: a string’s other notes

If you have a guitar, you can make the string play its other notes on purpose. Pluck an open string, then touch it very lightly, without pressing it down, exactly halfway along (on most guitars, directly over the twelfth fret), pluck it again, and lift your finger away just after. Instead of the open note you get a softer, bell-like one, an octave higher: your finger has made a still point in the middle, so only the patterns with a still point there can ring, and the lowest of those is two half-waves. Touch a third of the way along (over the seventh fret) and you get three half-waves, higher again; a quarter (the fifth fret), four.

Cal tried it, and got a clear, high note, and then a higher one, and then Albert asked him to stop.

Above, the string swings; below is a graph of how strongly each wavelength rings, once its trips back and forth have added up. Almost everywhere, the trips come back out of step and cancel, and the string barely moves; the ledger counts the trips and says it washes out. Only at a whole number of half-waves does every trip come back in step, the string ring, and its still points appear.

A wave held in by the nucleus

“So that’s a string,” said John. “What has it got to do with an atom?”

“Everything,” said Albert, “if the electron is a wave.”

And it is. That was the fourth journey’s discovery: an electron goes through two slits at once, as a wave does, and its wavelength is set by how fast it’s moving. In an atom, the electron’s wave isn’t free to spread out and travel away. The nucleus holds it in: its electric pull keeps the electron from leaving, unless something gives it enough energy to escape. A wave held in, turned back on itself, is exactly what a guitar string is. So it ought to behave the same way: settle into a steady pattern that fits, or wash itself out.

A wave that goes round

The first person to say so was Louis de Broglie, in 1924, in the same work in which he proposed that matter has a wavelength at all, and his picture is a good place to start, even though it turned out not to be the right one.

John took a napkin and laid it over a doughnut, so that the doughnut showed through as a ring, and drew a wave round it: up and down, up and down, all the way round the ring, until the pen came back to where it had started.

“Suppose the electron’s wave goes round the nucleus like that,” he said. “If it’s going to stay steady, it has to join up with itself when it gets back to the start. Crest on crest.”

“Otherwise,” said Cal, “it comes round out of step and cancels itself out. Like the string.”

“So only rings where a whole number of waves fits all the way round. Five here.” John counted them. “Or four, or six. Not four and a half.”

That was de Broglie’s picture: the electron’s wave going round the nucleus, and fitting only where a whole number of its waves fits round the circle. Each ring that fits has its own size, its own speed, and its own energy, and the rings in between can’t exist. It explained, with waves, the rule Niels Bohr had guessed eleven years before for which rings were allowed, and it got hydrogen’s numbers right.

Albert removed the doughnut from under the napkin, inspected the ink, and replaced it on the plate. “The numbers are right,” he said. “The picture is not. The electron doesn’t go round in a ring.”

A wave that fills the space

The trouble with de Broglie’s ring is that it keeps the planet: an electron’s wave running round a track, like a train round a circle of rails. The real wave isn’t confined to a track. It fills the space round the nucleus in every direction, the way the vibration of a struck bell fills the whole bell, or the ripples on a drum’s skin fill the skin. A drum, struck, doesn’t vibrate in any old shape either. Its skin is held in at its rim, so it too has only certain patterns: the whole skin rising and falling together, or two halves see-sawing about a still line across the middle, or a centre rising while a ring round it falls, and so on, each pattern with its own frequency. An atom’s electron is a wave held in, in three dimensions, by the nucleus’s pull, and it has its own set of patterns, each with its own energy.

Put it together:

  • An electron is a wave (the fourth journey).
  • In an atom, its wave is held in by the nucleus’s pull, which it hasn’t the energy to escape.
  • A wave held in can only last in a steady pattern that fits; any other washes itself out, as a wrong note does on a string.
  • An atom has only certain patterns that fit, and each has its own energy.
  • So an electron in an atom can have only certain energies, and none in between.

These allowed energies are the atom’s energy levels, and they’re the whole answer to both of the first journey’s clues.

Energy level

One of the particular energies an electron (or anything held in) is allowed to have, because its wave can settle into only certain patterns. An electron in an atom can be in one level or another, but never in between: it can’t have just any energy, any more than a guitar string can play just any note. Each kind of atom has its own set of levels. The lowest is the one an atom settles into when it’s left alone.

Why the atom can’t collapse

Start with the first clue: why the atom doesn’t collapse.

The planet-style atom of the first journey fell in because it could keep losing energy a little at a time, sinking lower and lower, all the way to the nucleus. An electron that’s a wave held in can only lose energy by dropping from one pattern to a lower one. And the lowest pattern has no lower pattern beneath it. There’s nowhere further to fall.

“But why can’t the lowest pattern just be smaller?” said Cal. “Closer in, with less energy?”

This is where the squeeze comes in, which the seventh journey found. Two things pull in opposite directions. The nucleus’s pull favours the electron being close: the closer it is, the less energy it has, as a ball has less energy the further down a hill it rolls. But a wave held in a small space has to jiggle, and the smaller the space, the harder it jiggles, and jiggling is energy. Squeeze the electron’s wave in towards the nucleus and the pull gives back energy, but the jiggle costs more. Let it spread out and the jiggle costs less, but the pull gives back less. Somewhere in between, the total is as low as it can be, and that’s where the lowest pattern sits.

For hydrogen, the balance comes at about a twentieth of a nanometre from the nucleus. That’s the size of a hydrogen atom: not a measurement put in by hand, but the place where the pull and the squeeze balance. The energy it would take to pull the electron out of that lowest pattern and free it altogether comes out at 13.6 electronvolts (an electronvolt is a unit for the tiny energies of single atoms; a lump of red light carries about two), which is exactly what it takes, measured in the laboratory. The full calculation gives both numbers, and even a rough version of the balance, on the maths page, gets them.

And to fall into the nucleus, the electron would have to be squeezed into a space tens of thousands of times smaller. Its jiggle would cost more than a hundred times what the pull could give back. Cal was right, the night before: it can’t fall in.

That leaves one thing to settle. The first journey’s rule was that an accelerating electron sends out light, and an electron in an atom is certainly moving: it’s jiggling hard. Why isn’t it sending out light all the time, and losing energy that way?

Because nothing about the steady pattern changes. What sends out light is a change in the electric push round a charge, as a radio mast’s electrons, shaken up and down, change the push in the space round them. An electron in a steady pattern is a wave that’s the same from one moment to the next, so the charge it spreads round the nucleus is the same from one moment to the next: nothing is shaking. The rule isn’t broken. A steady pattern simply gives it nothing to work with. So the lowest pattern can’t lose energy by sinking, because there’s nothing lower, and it can’t lose energy by shining, because nothing in it changes. It lasts. That is why the grain of sugar is still there.

Clouds, not orbits

What do the patterns look like? In 1926 Erwin Schrödinger, in Zurich, wrote down the rule the electron’s wave obeys (it’s known as the Schrödinger equation), and worked out its steady patterns for hydrogen. They are nothing like orbits.

The lowest is a ball: the electron’s wave strongest at the centre and fading smoothly outwards in every direction, the same all the way round, with nothing in it going round the nucleus one way rather than the other. The next level up has a ball with a shell round it and a still surface between them, where the wave is always zero, like the still point in the middle of a guitar string playing its second note. It has other patterns too, of the same energy: two lobes, one either side of the nucleus, with a still plane between them, like the drum skin see-sawing. Higher levels have more shells, more lobes, rings, and still surfaces between them, and they’re bigger: an electron in a high level spends its time far further out.

What does the wave mean? The same as it meant at the slits. The fifth journey found that where a particle’s wave is strong, it’s likely to be found if you look, and where the wave is weak, unlikely. So the patterns are pictures of where the electron might be found: clouds, thick where it’s likely, thin where it isn’t, and empty at the still surfaces.

Orbital

One of the steady patterns an electron’s wave can settle into in an atom, pictured as a cloud: thick where the electron is likely to be found if you look, thin where it’s unlikely, empty where the wave is always zero. Orbitals come in shapes (a ball, a pair of lobes, and more elaborate shapes for higher levels), each with its own energy. The name keeps an echo of the old idea of orbits, but there’s no track: the electron isn’t going round anything.

The clouds have one more oddity, and it matters in the next journey. They have no edge. A planet’s orbit has a definite largest distance from the Sun; a ball rolled up a hill has a definite highest point, where it runs out of energy and stops. By the old rules, the electron would have such a limit too: a distance beyond which it hasn’t the energy to go, against the nucleus’s pull. Its cloud doesn’t stop there. It fades, quickly but smoothly, out past that limit, into places the electron hasn’t the energy to reach.

Can anyone see one? Not by looking at it with light, as the first journey found: an atom is thousands of times smaller than light’s waves. But in 2013, a team led by Aneta Stodolna and Marc Vrakking, at the AMOLF laboratory in Amsterdam, found a way to make one hydrogen pattern visible. They set hydrogen atoms in an electric field, lifted their electrons into a high level with a laser, and let them out, gently, through a kind of electric lens that magnified the pattern more than twenty thousand times, onto a screen. The picture is a set of bright rings with dark rings between them: the still surfaces of the electron’s wave, just where the theory said they would be.

One lump of light, one colour

Now the second clue: the colours.

An electron in a higher pattern can drop to a lower one. When it does, it has less energy than before, and the difference has to go somewhere. It goes into light: a single lump of it, a single photon, carrying exactly the difference. And as John and Cal found in the second journey and the third, the energy of a lump of light sets its colour. Bigger lumps are bluer; smaller lumps, redder.

Follow it through:

  • An electron in an atom can only have certain energies, its levels.
  • So when it drops from one level to another, the energy it loses can only be one of certain amounts: the differences between the levels.
  • It gives that energy out as one lump of light, and a lump’s energy sets its colour.
  • So an atom can give out light of only certain colours, one for each drop, each one sharp, with nothing in between.

That’s why hydrogen glows in a few sharp colours and not a smear of them all. There’s nothing in between, because there are no energies in between.

There’s a second way to see the same thing, which comes back to the first journey’s rule about accelerating charges. While the electron is in one steady pattern, nothing changes and nothing shines. But while it’s changing from one pattern to another, it’s partly in both, and its cloud of charge sloshes back and forth between their two shapes. Sloshing charge is exactly what a radio mast’s electrons do, and it sends out light. It sloshes at a rate set by the difference between the two patterns’ energies, which is exactly the frequency of a lump of light carrying that difference. Both ways of looking give the same colour, which is a good sign that the picture holds together. The rule about accelerating charges was right all along. An atom only obeys it while it’s changing levels, and then only at one frequency.

The reverse happens too. An electron can climb from a lower level to a higher one, but only by taking in exactly the energy of the difference: a lump of light of exactly the colour it would give out falling back, or a knock of just enough energy from another atom, in a hot flame or a spark.

Spectral line

A single sharp colour that an atom gives out, or takes in, and no other: the light from one particular drop (or climb) between two of its energy levels, carried by lumps of light that each have exactly the energy of the difference. Spread an atom’s light out into its colours, and each such colour shows as a thin bright line, with darkness either side. Every element has its own set of lines.

Hydrogen’s colours

Hydrogen has the simplest set, because it has only one electron. Its levels crowd together towards the top: the gap from the lowest level to the next is by far the biggest, and the gaps above get smaller and smaller, until near the top the levels are packed together just beneath the point at which the electron is free.

A drop all the way to the lowest level releases a big lump, too blue for any eye: ultraviolet. A drop to the third level from higher ones releases small lumps, too red to see: infrared. Drops onto the second level are the ones in between, and they make the four colours hydrogen glows with, the four the first journey’s simulator showed beside its collapsing atom: from the third level, a deep red; from the fourth, a blue-green; from the fifth and sixth, two violets. Drops from higher still crowd together towards the violet end, and beyond it, into the ultraviolet.

A schoolteacher’s numbers

Hydrogen’s four visible colours were measured long before anyone knew why they were where they were. In 1885 Johann Balmer, a mathematics teacher at a girls’ secondary school in Basel, in Switzerland, and sixty years old, played with the four wavelengths that had been measured, and found a single simple formula that gave all four of them, to within a tiny fraction of a per cent. Nobody knew what it meant. He used it to predict a fifth line, just beyond the fourth, and was told that a line had already been seen in very nearly that place.

The levels explain his numbers: Balmer’s formula is the energy ladder, written in wavelengths. The first person to get it right from the rules of physics was Niels Bohr, in 1913, with an atom whose electron went round in circles of only certain sizes and jumped between them, giving out light as it jumped. He couldn’t say why only those circles; he simply declared that they were the only ones allowed. But his atom got Balmer’s numbers exactly, and even the number that scales them, from nothing but the electron’s charge and mass and Planck’s constant.

“It was right in its numbers,” said Albert, “and wrong in its picture. He says so himself.”

“Who does?” said Cal.

Albert was busy with the coffee machine.

John’s red

Hydrogen’s red line, the drop from the third level to the second, is the light of John’s own picture of Orion. Orion Wide, on Nyx, was made through a narrowband filter that lets through only that one red colour, for twenty-one and a half hours, so that it shows the great clouds of hydrogen behind Orion’s belt and sword, glowing where hot young stars have stripped their electrons away and they fall back down the levels, and nothing else. A filter like that works only because the line is sharp: a narrow slot in the spectrum, round that one colour, lets the hydrogen’s light through and shuts out nearly everything else.

Fingerprints

Every other kind of atom has more than one electron, and a nucleus with more charge, so its patterns are different, its levels are different, and the drops between them give different colours. How several electrons share an atom’s patterns between them is the story of the eleventh journey, and it’s the story of chemistry. For now, all that matters is that every element has its own set of levels, and so its own set of sharp colours. No two elements share a set. A set of colours is like a fingerprint: find it, and you know which element made it.

The salt in the flame

Which brings them back to Albert’s gas ring.

Salt is sodium and chlorine. In the heat of the flame, the salt comes apart and its sodium atoms are knocked about hard enough that their outermost electron is bumped up a level. It drops back almost at once, giving out a lump of light, and is bumped up again, and drops back again, over and over. Sodium’s easiest drop, the one a flame’s knocks most often set off, gives a yellow of about 589 nanometres, and in visible light it has very little else to give. So the flame turns yellow, and only yellow.

Look closely enough, with an instrument that spreads the colours out, and that yellow is two lines, not one, side by side and almost touching, at 589.0 and 589.6 nanometres. The pattern the electron drops from turns out to be two patterns, of very slightly different energy, for a reason that has to wait for the tenth journey.

Try it: salt in a flame

If you have a gas hob, you can see it for yourself. Light a burner, turn it down to a small, steady blue flame, and sprinkle a small pinch of ordinary table salt into it from above. The flame flashes a vivid yellow where the grains fall.

Warning

Use a small pinch, keep your face, hair and sleeves well back from the flame, and never do it near anything that can catch fire. Children should only try it with an adult.

The streetlights of La Palma

For years, after dark, La Palma and the side of Tenerife that faces it across the sea glowed with the same yellow as Albert’s flame.

The mountain on La Palma, the Roque de los Muchachos, is one of the best places in the world for telescopes, and in 1988 Spain passed a law to keep the skies over the Canaries’ observatories dark: the Sky Law, as it’s known. Its rules on lighting cover all of La Palma and the part of Tenerife that can be seen from it. For years the streetlights there were low-pressure sodium lamps, which make their light the way the salt flame does, with electricity knocking sodium atoms up a level instead of a flame. Almost all their light is that one doubled yellow line. To a telescope, that’s a gift. Stray light spread across every colour is very hard to get rid of in a picture of the sky; stray light of one exactly known colour can be blocked with a filter, or simply allowed for. Sodium lamps have now fallen out of use, and the rules now favour amber LEDs, which give out almost no blue.

Try it: a lamp’s colours in a CD

If you have an old CD or DVD, you can look at the colours of the lamps around you. Hold the disc near a lamp, shiny side up, and tilt it until the lamp’s light is spread into colours across its surface, the rainbow sheen that the fifth journey explained. An ordinary white LED or a filament bulb spreads into a smooth rainbow, every colour blending into the next. An old fluorescent tube or a low-energy bulb, if you can find one, spreads into separate bright bars of colour with dark gaps between them: the lines of the mercury and other atoms glowing inside it. And a low-pressure sodium streetlight, where any are left, would give a single yellow bar, and almost nothing else.

The figure below is a set of fingerprints. At the top are five elements’ own visible colours: hydrogen, helium, sodium, neon and mercury, each a strip of sharp lines with darkness between. Below is a mystery lamp. Every line it shows must match a line of the element that made it. Name the element, and the figure tells you where the lamp was. Then try Another lamp.

Fingerprints work at any distance. The light from a distant star or a glowing cloud in space carries the lines of the atoms it came from, so you can read what it’s made of without ever going there. A later series of journeys does exactly that, with the light of the stars.

The sugar survives

So both of the first journey’s clues have their answer, and it’s the same answer.

The atom lasts because its electron is a wave held in, and a wave held in can only take certain patterns. The lowest of them has nothing beneath it to fall to. It can’t shrink, because squeezing the wave closer to the nucleus makes it jiggle harder than the pull can pay for. And it doesn’t shine its energy away, because a steady pattern doesn’t change. The atom gives out only certain sharp colours because it can only drop between certain patterns, and each drop sends out one lump of light carrying exactly the difference.

The rules of 1911 weren’t wrong about electric charges, or about light. What they got wrong was the electron. They treated it as a tiny planet, a little ball that could be anywhere and go round at any distance, and lose its energy a little at a time. An electron isn’t that. It’s a wave, and a wave held in can’t do what a planet can.

Cal got up and went over to the counter, and looked at the grain of sugar under its glass for a long time.

“Every atom in that,” he said, “is doing this. Settled in its lowest pattern, as small as the squeeze allows.”

“Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen,” said John. “Each with its own set.”

“And each with more than one electron,” said Albert, “except the hydrogen. How they share the patterns between them is another day’s question.”

Out in the car park, John asked The Van’s simulator to do what it had done on the first day, and build a hydrogen atom: this time not as a little solar system by the rules of 1911, but as a wave held in by its nucleus, by the rules they had found since. The screen showed a single, soft, round cloud, thickest at the centre and fading outwards. They watched it for a long time. It didn’t move. It didn’t shrink. It didn’t do anything at all.

Back to Teide

By evening the coaches had gone, and the plateau was quiet. The sun went down behind the mountain, and across the sea, low on the horizon, the lights of La Palma came on, too far away to show their colour.

Cal sat on the terrace wall with the old guitar across his knees, playing its one string’s notes, the open note, then the octave, then the one above, very quietly, so that Albert wouldn’t hear.

“There’s something I don’t understand,” he said.

“Only one?” said John.

“Two.” Cal put the guitar down. “The electron’s held in by the nucleus. Fine. But what holds the nucleus in? It’s all positive charge, packed into a space tens of thousands of times smaller than the atom. Everything in it ought to be pushing everything else apart as hard as it can.” He frowned. “And some nuclei do come apart. That radium on the first day was spitting out alpha particles. If something holds a nucleus together, how does anything ever get out?”

“And the second?”

“The Sun.” Cal looked up at where it had been. “Albert told us once, the day of the expensive doughnut, that the Sun shines by joining hydrogen into helium. But hydrogen’s nuclei are single protons, all positive, all pushing each other apart. How do they ever get close enough to join?”

John didn’t know. They went in to pay.

Albert took their money at the till, in the slow, careful way he had, and gave them their change. Then, as they were turning to go, he said a single sentence, as if it were an answer to both questions at once.

“A wave cannot stop dead at a wall.”

He said nothing more, and they couldn’t get him to.

Outside, The Van sat in its corner of the car park, where it always sat, its nose against the low wall of lava blocks along the car park’s edge. On the other side of the wall, the old lava ran away into the dark towards the mountain. Cal looked at the wall for a while, and then at The Van, and then at the wall again.

Behind them, in the café window, the grain of sugar sat on its saucer under its upturned glass, made of atoms that had lasted for billions of years, and would go on lasting.
