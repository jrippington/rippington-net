The worst day

Albert had promised them the worst day the Earth ever had, and in the morning he brought it out to the car park with the doughnuts.

That is, he brought the doughnuts, on a plate, and set them on The Van’s dashboard. The worst day was already on the screen. The simulator had stopped where the last journey left it, four and a half billion years ago, with the planets nearly made, and it was showing the young Earth.

It was nearly as big as it is now, and it wasn’t a place anyone would want to be. Its skin was a thin black crust over molten rock, cracked into slabs whose edges glowed red, still hot from all the crashes of its making. There was no sea, and no Moon. Cal touched his shirt pocket, where the grain of sugar sat folded in its napkin, as though to make sure of something solid.

“There’s no Moon,” he said.

“Not yet,” said Albert, and climbed into the back with his coffee.

There was something else in the Earth’s sky. Low over its glowing edge was a second world, dark and much larger than any moon: a young planet about the size of Mars, half the Earth’s width. For millions of years the two had gone round the Sun on nearly the same path, tugging each other off course every time they passed. Geochemists call it Theia, after the Titan who in Greek myth was the mother of the Moon.

“It’s going to miss,” said John. “Isn’t it?”

Nobody answered. The simulator slowed itself down, which none of them had ever seen it do.

Theia came in at about ten kilometres a second and struck the Earth a glancing blow. For a moment the two worlds seemed simply to lean into each other. Then a flash ran out from where they touched, the rock there turned to glowing vapour, and a great arm of white-hot rock was flung out sideways into space, longer than the Earth was wide, curling round behind it. Theia’s iron heart, heavier than anything round it, sank through the Earth’s molten insides like a drop of honey through tea, and within a day it had joined the Earth’s own core. Theia was gone. What was left was a lopsided, glowing ball, much of it melted and some of it boiled away, wrapped in a white-hot sky.

The energy let go that afternoon was about as much as the Sun gives out, in every direction, in a whole day.

Some of the splash fell back. Some escaped altogether. And some was left going round the Earth, close in, as a glowing ring of molten rock and vapour. The ring didn’t last. Its pieces met, stuck and swept each other up, and within a few hours, by one recent simulation, or a century or so by others, they had gathered into a single round body, glowing a dull red, hanging over the Earth.

“Medium?” said Cal.

“You read the evidence yesterday,” said Albert. “On the Moon. You simply didn’t know what it was evidence of.”

Four clues and a collision

They had come home from the Moon the day before with four clues and no explanation, and John had written them on a napkin. He took it out now and smoothed it on the dashboard beside the doughnuts.

The first was the Moon’s pale highlands. Their rock, anorthosite, is built of crystals light enough to float, and it’s found all over the Moon, so the Moon’s whole outer part must once have been molten: an ocean of molten rock hundreds of kilometres deep, on which the light crystals floated up and gathered into a crust, as ice gathers on a freezing pond. One of John’s own pictures shows it, dug up.

The simulator had just answered it. A Moon gathered from a glowing ring in hours, or even in a century, has no time to cool while it forms. Every lump falling onto it adds the heat of its fall, as a hammer warms a nail, and it starts life molten.

The second clue was iron, or the lack of it. The Moon’s iron core is about a fifth of its width, where the Earth’s is more than half of the Earth’s. But by the time of the crash, both worlds had sorted themselves out, as anything molten does: the heavy iron had sunk to the middle and the lighter rock floated above it. A glancing blow splashes out the outsides. So the Moon was made of rock from the two worlds’ outer layers, and Theia’s iron went into the Earth, as they had just watched it go.

The third was that the Moon is dry, with almost none of the water and other easily boiled things in the Earth’s rocks. The splash was white-hot, much of it vapour, and a small, hot Moon has too weak a pull to keep hold of anything that boils easily.

The fourth was the hard one. Oxygen comes in slightly heavier and lighter kinds, the isotopes of the third journey, and the worlds of the Solar System each seem to have their own mixture: rocks from Mars, which reach us as meteorites, have one of their own. The Moon’s oxygen has exactly the Earth’s mixture. Yet in most simulations of the crash, most of the ring comes from Theia. Either Theia was made from the same stuff as the Earth, a near neighbour from the same part of the disc, or the crash was so violent that it boiled a great part of both worlds and stirred them together, so that the Earth and the Moon both condensed from one cloud.

John asked the simulator to show the second idea. It drew what its inventors, in 2017, called a synestia: the Earth swollen into an enormous, flattened, spinning cloud of glowing rock vapour, thick and round at its rim, thinning towards a hollow in its middle, where the Earth was.

SYNESTIA: SPECULATION. ASK AGAIN LATER

Cal looked at the cloud’s shape for some time. Then he looked at the plate on the dashboard.

“I had nothing to do with it,” said Albert.

So that was why the simulator said medium. A Moon made somewhere else and captured, or made alongside the Earth from the same disc, would have no reason to be molten, short of iron and dry all at once. A crash gives all three. The fourth clue needs the crash to have been between near twins, or extraordinarily thorough, and which, nobody yet knows.

There was a fifth clue: the Moon is drifting away from the Earth, about 3.8 centimetres a year, measured by laser. That one needed the rest of the morning.

It rained rock

The simulator ran on, slowly. The Earth was molten to a depth of hundreds of kilometres, perhaps far more, and over it hung a sky of rock: rock boiled into vapour, at more than two thousand degrees. Where the top of that sky cooled into space, the vapour condensed into droplets of molten rock, and they fell. It rained glowing rock on the Earth for about a thousand years.

Above the rain, enormous and close, was the new Moon. It was perhaps twenty-five thousand kilometres away, a fifteenth of its distance now, and it looked fifteen times as wide as the Moon does from the plateau, glowing red itself. The Earth was spinning so fast that the Moon swung up over the horizon and down again every few hours.

THE EARTH, 4.5 BILLION YEARS AGO: RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM

“It’s the plateau,” said Cal. “Everywhere.”

He meant it as a joke, but it was nearer the truth than he knew. The café stands on a volcano. The plateau round it is the floor of an enormous old crater, Teide rises out of it, and the lava fields between the café and the mountain, black and brown and rust-red, are the cooled skin of rock which came up molten from below. For a while, the whole Earth was a lava field that hadn’t cooled yet, glowing the colours Red Hot, White Hot found in the island’s lava.

Geologists call the Earth’s first half a billion years the Hadean, after Hades, the Greek underworld. It was named in 1972, before anyone had found a single thing that old.

The first air

When the rock rain stopped, the sky was steam and carbon dioxide, and it came out of the rock itself.

Molten rock deep inside a planet holds gas dissolved in it, as a bottle of fizzy water does. When the rock rises and the weight on it eases, the gas comes out, as it does when you open the bottle. The young Earth’s whole molten surface breathed out its gas at once, and once a crust had formed, the breathing went on through volcanoes. What comes out of a volcano today is mostly steam, then carbon dioxide, then sulphur gases and a little nitrogen, and no oxygen at all. That was the Earth’s first air: poisonous to anything which breathes today, and nothing like the air in the café.

There’s a volcano you can watch doing it, from the right place on the plateau, and it isn’t Teide, which hasn’t erupted in historic times. It’s on La Palma, across the sea to the west. On 19 September 2021, on La Palma’s western slopes, the ground split open, and for eighty-five days, until 13 December, the volcano now called Tajogaite poured out lava and gas. John and Cal hadn’t seen it, but the simulator could rebuild what the plateau had seen from photographs and measurements made at the time.

LA PALMA, AUTUMN 2021: OBSERVED. BY A GREAT MANY PEOPLE

It was night on the screen, and across the dark sea a spark of orange sat low on the edge of La Palma, lighting its own plume from below. By day the plume showed for what it was, a dark column of ash and gas rising for kilometres and leaning away on the wind.

It was a great deal of gas. By the best measurements, from satellites and from the ground, the eruption breathed out about 1.8 million tonnes of sulphur dioxide, and somewhere between 20 and 28 million tonnes of carbon dioxide: more carbon dioxide than all the cars, power stations, ships and aircraft of the Canary Islands, with their two million people, put out in a whole year. That was one small volcano, for three months. The young Earth had thousands, for hundreds of millions of years.

The first sea

The steam was so thick that for about two million years it kept the molten rock below it from freezing, like a lid on a pan. Then the crust thickened, the surface cooled at last, and the steam came down. It rained, hot and heavy and without stopping, onto new black rock, until the low places were full.

The first sea was hot. By one careful reckoning it was about 230 degrees, and stayed liquid only because the air pressing down on it was about a hundred times as heavy as ours, mostly carbon dioxide, as the lid of a pressure cooker keeps water from boiling far above a hundred degrees. Over the next ten to a hundred million years, the sea and the rock under it drew most of that carbon dioxide out of the air, and the world cooled.

Some of the water may have arrived from outside, on icy bodies from beyond the last journey’s frost line, and how much is still argued. Its hydrogen is the clue: a little of all hydrogen is the heavy isotope deuterium, water from different parts of the Solar System carries different shares of it, and the oceans’ share matches some kinds of asteroid well and most comets badly.

How does anyone know any of this, when almost nothing of that world survives? The oldest pieces of the Earth anyone has found are not rocks at all but single crystals of a mineral called zircon, from the Jack Hills of Western Australia. Zircon is extraordinarily tough. When the rock it grew in wears away, its crystals survive as grains of sand and get swept into new rocks, again and again. It’s also one of the crystals the last journey’s rock clock works on: it takes in uranium as it grows and shuts out lead, so its lead says how long ago it grew. The oldest of the Jack Hills grains grew 4.4 billion years ago, a hundred million years or so after the Moon was made.

Cal took the napkin out of his pocket and unfolded it on the dashboard, beside the screen, where the simulator had drawn that zircon at its true size: a clear, slightly brownish crystal, about two tenths of a millimetre by four. The grain of sugar beside it was bigger.

“Older than every rock on Earth,” he said, “and smaller than this.” He folded the sugar away again.

Its oxygen carries a hint. Rock which has lain in cool water at the surface takes up a little more of the heavy kind of oxygen than rock fresh from the depths, and the zircon’s oxygen is a little too heavy, as if the molten rock it grew from had been made partly from rock soaked in cool water. If that reading is right, and it’s argued, there were already seas on the Earth 4.4 billion years ago. The oldest rock still in one piece is younger: the Acasta Gneiss in northern Canada, about four billion years old.

A fainter Sun

“That can’t be right,” said John. “The Sun was fainter then.”

He was right about the Sun. A star like the Sun brightens slowly as it ages, as the helium piling up in its core makes the core shrink, heat up and burn a little faster. When the Earth was young, the Sun gave out only about seventy per cent of the light it does now.

“So the Earth got less than three quarters of the warmth it gets now,” said John. “At that, the seas should have frozen solid. The whole world would have been a snowball. So the zircon’s wrong.”

It’s a good argument, and its numbers are right. With today’s air, and the Sun at seventy per cent, the Earth’s average would be several degrees below freezing; and ice, once it forms, reflects sunlight back into space, so the cold would feed on itself. Astronomers call it the faint young Sun puzzle, and it has worried them since the 1970s.

“With today’s air,” said Cal.

That was John’s hidden assumption: that the air didn’t matter. Sunlight passes through the air and warms the ground. The warm ground gives its warmth off again as infrared, the glow Red Hot, White Hot found every warm thing gives. Some gases, carbon dioxide and water vapour above all, absorb infrared and send some of it back down, as a blanket slows the warmth leaving you on a cold night. Without its blanket the Earth would average about eighteen degrees below zero; with it, about fifteen above. And the young Earth’s blanket was far thicker, its air full of the volcanoes’ carbon dioxide. It kept the seas liquid under a fainter Sun.

Days of five hours

That left the fifth clue: the Moon drifting away.

The Moon’s pull is a little stronger on the side of the Earth facing it than on the far side, so it stretches the Earth very slightly, and stretches its seas much more: the tides, two bulges of water, one towards the Moon and one away. But the Earth spins under the bulges much faster than the Moon goes round, and drags them along, so they sit a little ahead of the Moon. The near bulge, a great mass of water just ahead of the Moon, pulls the Moon forward along its orbit, and the Moon pulls back on the bulge. Pulled forward, the Moon swings out into a wider orbit. Pulled back, the Earth spins a little more slowly. On the last journey, with two bags of sugar on a swivel chair, John and Cal found that an amount of turning is never lost, only handed on, and this is a handover: the Earth’s spin passes, a little at a time, into the Moon’s going round. So the day gets longer and the Moon gets further away, together, and because the total is fixed, knowing one gives the other.

Run it backwards, and the Moon comes closer and the day gets shorter. Just after the crash, with the Moon a fifteenth of its present distance, the Earth must have turned in about five hours. And the closer the Moon, the stronger its tidal effects, very steeply: halve the distance, and they’re eight times as strong. The first sea’s tides must have been enormous, though how enormous depends on how far the Moon had got by then.

For the mathematically inclinedThe Moon’s drift and the length of the day

The evidence is in the rocks, and some of it was once alive. A coral adds a thin layer to its skeleton every day, and the layers change with the seasons, so they come in yearly bands. In 1963 the American palaeontologist John Wells counted the daily layers in the yearly bands of fossil corals about 380 million years old, and found about four hundred a year. The year’s length is set by the Earth’s going round the Sun, which tides don’t change, so if the year held four hundred days, each day was shorter: about twenty-two hours.

Tides leave their own records. In South Australia, layers of sand and silt laid down by tides about 620 million years ago are thicker in each fortnight’s strongest tides than in its weakest, and counting them gives about four hundred days a year again. And in Western Australia there’s a striped rock of iron, 2.46 billion years old, whose layers keep the slow rhythm of a wobble in the Earth’s spin. The wobble’s pace depends on how close the Moon was and how fast the Earth turned, and in 2022 a team led from Utrecht read the answer from its stripes: the Moon about 321,800 kilometres away, and a day of about seventeen hours. Layered rocks in China, 1.4 billion years old, give about eighteen and a half hours, and tidal sands in South Africa, 3.2 billion years old, perhaps thirteen, though that reading is argued.

The Moon hasn’t always drifted at today’s pace. Today’s oceans happen to slosh nearly in time with the tides, which makes them an unusually good brake; with the continents elsewhere, the oceans had other shapes, and the Moon drifted more slowly.

Alfred

A little before noon a tall man in a heavy coat came into the café, stamped his feet as though he had come a long way through snow, and stood for a while at the window, looking down at the sea of clouds below the plateau with every sign of professional pleasure.

“A beautiful inversion,” he said.

“We get it most mornings,” said Albert.

The sea of clouds is weather: cool, damp air from the ocean, held down under a lid of warmer, drier air. The visitor explained this to nobody in particular, and seemed delighted by it.

“An old friend,” said Albert. “Alfred.”

Cal looked from one to the other. “So that’s what Fred’s short for,” he said. “I’d always wondered.”

“Fred,” said Albert, “is not short for anything. Nobody has ever managed to make Fred any shorter.”

“We have met,” said Alfred mildly, hanging up his coat. “He told me I was wrong about the weather.”

Alfred had a weathered face, as if he had spent years squinting at snow, and the quiet manner of a man used to being disbelieved. He accepted a coffee, and then he noticed the old map of the world by the café door, put there for the coach parties, and went and stood in front of it, his coffee forgotten.

“Look at that,” he said, and put one finger on the bulge of Brazil and another on the great curve of Africa’s western coast. “I saw it in an atlas in 1910. I have never been able to stop seeing it since.”

Once anyone has pointed it out, it’s hard not to see: the bulge of Brazil looks as though it would fit into the bight under West Africa.

“Coincidence,” said John. “Coastlines are wiggly. Some of them were bound to look like others.”

Alfred smiled, and asked if he might borrow Albert’s newspaper. Albert, who had been doing the crossword, handed it over with some reluctance. Alfred tore it across in a long, ragged line, pulled the halves apart and laid them on the table. Then he pushed them together again. The torn edges fitted, and across the tear the lines of print ran on unbroken, from one half to the other.

“If the edges fit,” he said, “that might be chance. If the print runs on across the tear as well, line after line, you have no choice. They were one page.”

“I hadn’t finished the crossword,” said Albert.

The print, for continents, is everything written on the land before it tore. In 1912 a German meteorologist and polar explorer, Alfred Wegener, set out the case, and over the years it grew.

  • The coasts fit, and better still a kilometre or so under the sea, at the true edge of each continent, where its shelf drops away to the deep ocean floor. In 1965 a computer found the best fit there, and along thousands of kilometres of coast the gaps and overlaps average little more than a hundred kilometres.
  • The same fossils turn up on both sides. Mesosaurus, a small reptile about a metre long which lived in shallow inland waters about 280 million years ago, is found in southern Brazil and Uruguay, and in southern Africa, and nowhere else. It couldn’t have swum an ocean. The fossil leaves of a plant called Glossopteris are found in South America, Africa, India, Australia and Antarctica. Robert Scott’s party collected some on their way back from the South Pole in 1912 and carried them to the end; the rocks were found beside them in their tent.
  • Mountain belts run on across the ocean. The worn-down mountains of the Scottish Highlands and Norway continue in Greenland, Newfoundland and the Appalachians, the same rocks folded the same way at the same time.
  • Ice was where it shouldn’t be. About 300 million years ago, glaciers scraped scratches into rock in India, southern Africa, South America and Australia, places now warm or tropical, and Antarctica has seams of coal, made from forests.

The figure below is Alfred’s newspaper, with continents. Africa stays where it is; slide South America back across the ocean and watch it turn as it goes, and its coast settle against Africa’s. Two lines of print are drawn on it: where Mesosaurus is found, and the edge of West Africa’s very old rock, about two billion years old, against younger rock to its east. In 1967 geologists predicted where that edge should come out in Brazil if the fit was right, dated the rocks there, and found it, near the city of São Luís.

Laughed at

Wegener first set out his drifting continents in a lecture in Frankfurt on 6 January 1912, and in a book in 1915. Most geologists thought it nonsense, and some said so with feeling: one eminent American is said to have called it “utter, damned rot”.

They had reasons, and at full strength they were good ones. The floor of the ocean is solid rock, and a continent can no more plough through it than a ship can sail through a frozen sea. And nothing seemed strong enough to push one. Wegener suggested the Earth’s spin, flinging the continents towards the equator, and the Moon’s and the Sun’s tidal effects. In Cambridge the geophysicist Harold Jeffreys worked out how strong those pushes were, and found them far too weak. He was right.

“Then the fossils crossed on land,” said John. “There were bridges between the continents, and they sank.”

Alfred nodded, as though he had heard it before.

“Bridges of what?” said Cal. “The continents are a lighter kind of rock than the sea floor. That’s why they stand up out of it. A bridge made of continent would float, like ice. It couldn’t sink.”

“And a bridge,” said Alfred, “does not make the coasts fit, or carry mountains from Scotland to Newfoundland, or put glaciers in India.”

So the critics were right about the push and wrong about the movement, and it took about fifty years to tell the two apart. Wegener didn’t live to see it. He died in November 1930 on the Greenland ice, aged fifty, on his way back from taking supplies to a weather station in its middle.

“Did it bother you?” Cal asked Alfred. “Being laughed at?”

Alfred considered it.

“A little,” he said. “But the coasts still fitted. I found it is possible to be laughed at and right at the same time. It is only uncomfortable.”

Rocks that remember north

The answer came, in the end, from the ground under the café.

The plateau’s dark lava is full of tiny grains of magnetite, an iron mineral which is a natural magnet. While the lava is molten, the grains are too hot to hold any magnetism. As it cools, at about 580 degrees each grain locks its magnetism in the direction of the Earth’s own magnetic field at that moment, and keeps it. A cooled lava flow is full of millions of tiny compasses, frozen pointing at north as it was when the lava set.

They point up or down, too. The Earth’s field rises out of the ground in the southern half of the world and dives back into it in the northern, so near the poles it’s steep and at the equator it lies flat. A lava’s frozen compasses remember not only which way north was, but how far it was from the equator when it cooled.

In the 1950s geologists read those compasses in old rocks across Europe and North America, and found something impossible. Each continent’s rocks said the North Pole had wandered over the last few hundred million years, but they disagreed about where it had wandered to, and there’s only one North Pole. Close the Atlantic, though, and the two wandering paths fall into one. The pole had stayed put; the continents had moved. India’s old rocks remembered being south of the equator.

And the rocks remembered something stranger. In 1906, in France, Bernard Brunhes found lava magnetised the wrong way round, its frozen compasses pointing south. In 1929, in Japan, Motonori Matuyama found more, and saw that the reversed lavas were always the older ones. Every so often, the Earth’s magnetic field flips, north and south changing places. By the 1960s, lavas dated on land by their radioactive clocks gave a timetable of the flips. The last was about 780,000 years ago, and before it they came a few times every million years, irregularly.

Stripes on the sea floor

During the Second World War, ships began crossing the oceans with echo sounders, timing pings off the sea floor. In 1952 Marie Tharp, plotting soundings of the Atlantic in New York, found a great chain of mountains running down the middle of the ocean, and along its crest a valley, a crack, as though the floor were being pulled apart. Her colleague Bruce Heezen first dismissed it as “girl talk”. It was the rift along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, one stretch of a chain of ridges which runs right round the world.

In 1962 Harry Hess, a geologist who had commanded a ship in the Pacific during the war with its echo sounder running, put it together. The sea floor is made at the ridges, from molten rock welling up into the crack, and it moves slowly away from them on both sides, and goes back down into the Earth at the deep trenches on the oceans’ edges. The continents don’t plough through the sea floor. They ride on it.

Ships had also been towing magnetometers, which measure the strength of the magnetic field, back and forth across the oceans, and found the sea floor striped: long bands where the field was a little stronger, alternating with bands where it was a little weaker, parallel to the ridges. In 1963 Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews, in Cambridge, saw what the stripes were, and in Canada Lawrence Morley saw it too, though his paper was turned down. If Hess was right, the new rock at the ridge freezes the field’s direction into itself as it cools, and is then carried away, half to each side. Each flip of the field is written into the rock at the ridge, split in two, and the halves carried off in opposite directions. The sea floor is a tape recording of the field’s flips, and it should be a mirror image either side of the ridge.

It is. In 1966 a record made by the research ship Eltanin across the ridge in the South Pacific showed the stripes on one side as the mirror image of the other, and their widths matched the timetable of flips dated in lavas on land, stripe for stripe.

“They’re the same either side,” said Cal, before anyone else had seen it. “Exactly the same.”

The figure below is a ridge from above. New floor made at the ridge moves away to each side, freezing the field as it was: amber where north was where it is now, dark where it was flipped. Above it is what a ship’s magnetometer reads crossing it; below it, the flips dated in lava on land, laid out as though the floor had carried them away at a speed you choose. Play it and watch the stripes come out of the ridge in pairs. Then change your speed: only at one speed does every flip line up with its stripe, and that’s how fast the floor is moving. Try the slow ridge off Iceland, and the fast one Eltanin crossed.

Is there any way out? Suppose the sea floor stays where it is. Then there’s no reason for the stripes to be mirror images about the ridges, or for their widths to keep time with flips dated in lavas thousands of kilometres away on land. Drilling settled the rest. In 1968 a drilling ship, the Glomar Challenger, bored into the South Atlantic’s floor at a row of places either side of the ridge, and the oldest mud on the rock got older the further each hole was from the ridge, at the rate the stripes gave. Almost nowhere is the floor of the open ocean older than about two hundred million years, a twentieth of the age of the oldest continental rock. A permanent sea floor would be nearly as old as the Earth, and buried in mud. This one is young, and moving.

Plate tectonics

The way the Earth’s outer shell works. It is broken into a dozen or so great plates of rock, each about a hundred kilometres thick, which move slowly over the hotter, softer rock beneath, a few centimetres a year. New sea floor is made where plates pull apart, at ridges along the oceans’ middles, and old sea floor sinks back into the Earth where plates meet, at deep trenches. The continents, made of lighter rock, ride on the plates and never sink, so they drift, collide and tear apart. Earthquakes and most volcanoes happen along the plates’ edges.

Alfred had been watching the stripes come out of the ridge in pairs for a long time.

“Of course,” he said quietly. “The floor moves with them. I had them pushing through it. It never occurred to me to move the floor.”

Standing in the rift

“Can you stand on one?” said John. “A ridge?”

“In one place,” said Alfred, “you can walk across it.”

That place is Iceland, where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge comes up out of the sea, so The Van went to Iceland, with Alfred in the back holding his coffee steady. It rose from the car park with its usual noise, as of a lift deciding which floor it wanted, out over the sea of clouds and north. With nothing to measure against, the speedometer said 17 mph the whole way.

They came down at Þingvellir in the middle of the day, in a thin October light, the hills round the valley already white. The Van parked on frozen grass at the foot of a cliff of grey lava, tens of metres high, running away north and south as straight as a wall. It’s called Almannagjá, and it is the edge of North America: the plate the Americas ride on, with Greenland and the western half of Iceland. Five kilometres across the valley, the land belongs, near enough, to the plate Europe rides on. The valley floor between is cut by about a hundred cracks, and slowly sinking as the two sides pull apart.

NORTH AMERICA: THAT WAY. EUROPE: THAT WAY. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

Iceland’s first parliament met under that cliff, from the year 930.

One of the cracks, Silfra, is full of water melted from a glacier fifty kilometres away and filtered through the lava for decades before it seeps in: so clear that you can see a hundred metres through it, and two to four degrees above freezing all year. Cal knelt, put his hand in, and took it out again quite quickly. Down in the blue, the crack’s walls went on and on, sixty metres deep.

The Van had been parked with its front wheels on one side of a smaller crack and its back wheels on the other.

DISTANCE BETWEEN AXLES: INCREASING. PLEASE CONSULT YOUR DEALER

“By how much?” said John.

“About a millimetre a year,” said Cal, reading on. “It says it would like to be moved before Christmas.”

Across Iceland, the two plates pull apart about two centimetres a year, shared between several rifts, and this valley takes a few millimetres of it. Over the whole Atlantic it’s two or three centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow. John looked at his own for a while. It’s nothing at all, yet given a hundred million years, a fingernail’s pace makes an ocean three thousand kilometres wide.

Alfred had gone to the foot of the cliff and stood with one hand flat on the rock of North America, looking across the valley towards Europe.

“My measurements said that Greenland was moving tens of metres a year,” he said. “They were wrong. The old measurements I used were bad. It is a thousand times slower than I said.” He took his hand away. “But it is moving.”

All the land in one

On the way home the simulator ran the map backwards in the windscreen, over the real ocean below.

The Atlantic closed. Greenland met Europe, going back about fifty million years; South America met Africa, as on Alfred’s newspaper, about 130 million years back; and about 180 million years back North America met Africa, and there was no Atlantic at all. Every continent was joined into one, from pole to pole: Pangaea, “all lands”. It wasn’t the first, and there will be others. The continents gather, break, scatter and gather again, every few hundred million years.

Supercontinent

A single great landmass made when all, or nearly all, of the Earth’s continents drift together and join. The plates carry them together, hold them for a hundred million years or more, and tear them apart again, and the cycle repeats every few hundred million years. The most recent was Pangaea, from about 335 to 175 million years ago; before it came Rodinia, from about 1.1 billion to 750 million years ago, and Columbia, also called Nuna, about 1.8 billion years ago. Earlier ones are guessed at.

Then the Canaries came up under them. The sea floor they stand on is some of the oldest in the Atlantic, made at the ridge in its first few tens of millions of years, about 170 million years ago, and carried east since. The islands came much later, from a hot spot in the Earth beneath, where molten rock wells up through the moving floor and builds volcanoes, one after another. Tenerife’s oldest lavas are about twelve million years old. And Teide, the mountain over the café, is younger than our species: it began to grow less than two hundred thousand years ago, in the scar left when a great landslide carried the island’s northern side into the sea.

In two or three hundred million years, by the best guesses, the continents will have gathered into one again.

Back to Teide

By evening the coaches had gone, and Alfred too, with the two halves of Albert’s newspaper under his arm. He said he wanted to finish the crossword. Albert said he was welcome to try.

The sun went down behind the western ridge, and the crescent Moon came out of the glow it left, a day older and a little fatter than the night before. Since breakfast it had moved away from the Earth by about the width of a hair.

Albert came out to the terrace with three coffees and something under his arm, which he set on the wall. It was a slab of polished stone about the size of a paperback, heavy, and striped from end to end in thin bands of red, black and glinting gold.

“I use it to keep the bills from blowing away,” he said.

“It’s the striped iron rock,” said John. “Like the one with the seventeen-hour days.”

It was. Rock like it, the striped iron of Western Australia and South Africa, was laid down on the floors of ancient seas, layer upon layer, in its greatest amounts about two and a half billion years ago. Its iron came out of the sea water, as rust: the sea had been full of dissolved iron, and something rusted it and dropped it to the floor, stripe after stripe.

“Rusting takes oxygen,” said Cal.

It does, and that wasn’t all. Pebbles of fool’s gold, a mineral which oxygen eats away, rolled about in rivers older than about 2.4 billion years and survived. In rivers after that, they don’t. And after about 2.4 billion years ago, for the first time, the land itself rusted red, in rocks still red today, while the striped iron in the seas dwindled and, by about 1.8 billion years ago, had all but stopped. The rocks say that the air, which had never held any oxygen, filled with it then.

“From where?” said John. “The volcanoes don’t make any. We’ve been watching them all day.”

“The rocks don’t make it either,” said Cal. “All they do is rust. They take it away.”

They looked at the stripes in the stone, red and black and red, for a long time. The light went off the mountain.

“Something was making it,” said John.

Albert picked up his coffee.

“I have a friend coming for breakfast tomorrow,” he said. “Charles. He has been wondering about that since 1871.”
