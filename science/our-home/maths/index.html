The impact’s energy

The young Earth, at the time of the crash, had about nine tenths of its present mass, and Theia about a tenth, a little more than Mars has today (Mars is 0.107 of the Earth’s mass). Two bodies falling together from far apart meet at least at their mutual escape speed,

vesc=2G(M+m)R+r,v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G (M + m)}{R + r}},vesc​=R+r2G(M+m)​​,

with R≈6,150R \approx 6{,}150R≈6,150 km and r≈3,400r \approx 3{,}400r≈3,400 km their radii:

vesc=2×6.67×10−11×5.97×10249.55×106≈9.1 km/s.v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24}}{9.55 \times 10^{6}}} \approx 9.1\ \text{km/s}.vesc​=9.55×1062×6.67×10−11×5.97×1024​​≈9.1 km/s.

Simulations of the Moon’s making use speeds from about that up to ten per cent more, about ten kilometres a second. The energy of the motion between the two, with μ=Mm/(M+m)\mu = Mm/(M + m)μ=Mm/(M+m) their reduced mass (0.89×0.110.89 \times 0.110.89×0.11 of the Earth’s mass, 5.8×10235.8 \times 10^{23}5.8×1023 kg), is

E=12μv2≈12×5.8×1023×(9.5×103)2≈2.6×1031 J.E = \tfrac{1}{2} \mu v^2 \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 5.8 \times 10^{23} \times (9.5 \times 10^{3})^2 \approx 2.6 \times 10^{31}\ \text{J}.E=21​μv2≈21​×5.8×1023×(9.5×103)2≈2.6×1031 J.

The Sun gives out L⊙=3.83×1026L_\odot = 3.83 \times 10^{26}L⊙​=3.83×1026 watts, so in a day

L⊙×86,400 s≈3.3×1031 J:L_\odot \times 86{,}400\ \text{s} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{31}\ \text{J}:L⊙​×86,400 s≈3.3×1031 J:

the crash let go about as much energy as the Sun’s whole output for most of a day. Shared over the whole Earth, it comes to about

2.6×1031 J6.0×1024 kg≈4 MJ per kilogram,\frac{2.6 \times 10^{31}\ \text{J}}{6.0 \times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}} \approx 4\ \text{MJ per kilogram},6.0×1024 kg2.6×1031 J​≈4 MJ per kilogram,

while heating cold rock to its melting point and melting it takes about 1.5 to 2 MJ a kilogram. It isn’t shared evenly, and the young Earth was hot already, but the crash had energy enough to melt much of the planet.

The faint young Sun

A planet’s surface settles where the sunlight it absorbs balances the heat it radiates. Add today’s 33 degrees of blanket, and the average is about 267 K, −6-6−6 °C, below freezing. Worse, ice reflects more: an ice-covered Earth with an albedo of about 0.6 would settle at

T=[0.4×0.71×13614×5.67×10−8]1/4≈203 K,T = \left[\frac{0.4 \times 0.71 \times 1361}{4 \times 5.67 \times 10^{-8}}\right]^{1/4} \approx 203\ \text{K},T=[4×5.67×10−80.4×0.71×1361​]1/4≈203 K,

and once frozen it would stay frozen. To keep the young Earth at today’s 15 °C takes a blanket of about 288−234=54288 - 234 = 54288−234=54 degrees, more than half as much again as today’s. Estimates of the carbon dioxide in the air between about four and two and a half billion years ago run from about ten to 2,500 times today’s (Catling and Zahnle 2020), enough, with some methane, to make up for the fainter Sun.

The Moon’s drift and the length of the day

The shared turning. The Earth’s spin and the Moon’s orbit share an amount of turning, angular momentum, which tides can only pass from one to the other. The Earth’s spin has CωC\omegaCω, with C=8.03×1037C = 8.03 \times 10^{37}C=8.03×1037 kg m² its moment of inertia and ω\omegaω its rate of turning; the Moon’s orbit, taken as a circle of radius aaa, has μGMa\mu \sqrt{G M a}μGMa​, with μ=7.25×1022\mu = 7.25 \times 10^{22}μ=7.25×1022 kg the pair’s reduced mass and GM=4.035×1014GM = 4.035 \times 10^{14}GM=4.035×1014 m³/s² for the Earth and the Moon together. Today, with the Earth turning once against the stars in 86,164 seconds and the Moon at a=384,400a = 384{,}400a=384,400 km,

Spreading rate from the stripes

Rock at a distance xxx from a ridge spreading at vvv on each side set a time t=x/vt = x/vt=x/v ago. So a stripe whose edge is dated by the same flip in lavas on land gives the speed directly:

v=xt.v = \frac{x}{t}.v=tx​.

The last flip, 780,000 years ago, lies about 35 kilometres from the Pacific-Antarctic Ridge, which Eltanin crossed, and about 8 kilometres from the Reykjanes Ridge off Iceland:

vPacific=35 km780,000 yr≈4.5 cm/yr,vIceland=7.8 km780,000 yr=1.0 cm/yr,v_\text{Pacific} = \frac{35\ \text{km}}{780{,}000\ \text{yr}} \approx 4.5\ \text{cm/yr}, \qquad v_\text{Iceland} = \frac{7.8\ \text{km}}{780{,}000\ \text{yr}} = 1.0\ \text{cm/yr},vPacific​=780,000 yr35 km​≈4.5 cm/yr,vIceland​=780,000 yr7.8 km​=1.0 cm/yr,

on each side. A stripe’s width is the speed times how long the field held still: the Jaramillo, the 84,000 years from 1.07 to 0.99 million years ago, is 1.0×0.84≈0.81.0 \times 0.84 \approx 0.81.0×0.84≈0.8 km wide off Iceland and 4.5×0.84≈3.84.5 \times 0.84 \approx 3.84.5×0.84≈3.8 km in the South Pacific, which is why slow ridges’ stripes are harder to read.
