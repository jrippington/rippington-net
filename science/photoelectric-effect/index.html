The evidence for something we took on trust

Cal has stopped taking Albert's word for it. Light, Albert once told them, comes in small packets; now Cal wants proof. Albert's cupboard provides an electroscope, a zinc plate and two lamps, and the brightest red light in the café can't do what a feeble ultraviolet one does at once. By night, The Van turns out to be made of dots.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Field Guide to the Very Small, part 3 of 16

Taken on trust

Cal was at the café before John the next morning, and before the coaches, and very nearly before Albert. The probe was still on the counter where they’d left it, beside the grain of sugar under its glass. Albert was laying out cups.

“You wanted evidence,” said Albert.

“I did.” Cal sat down at the counter. “I’ve been thinking about your box.”

In The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe, the seventh of their journeys through space and time, Albert had shown them a doughnut box with a lamp at each end, and used it to show where E = mc² comes from. On the way, almost in passing, he had told them that light behaves as a wave but is also delivered in small, separate amounts, packets of light called photons, each carrying a definite energy: more for bluer light, less for redder. They had nodded, and gone on to the next thing.

“We took it on trust,” said Cal. “I’d like to stop.”

John arrived, saw the two of them at the counter, and sat down beside Cal with the air of someone who suspected he’d missed the start of an argument.

“Yesterday,” said Cal, “we found that heat goes into light in lumps. Planck’s lumps. But that’s about the oven. It’s about how the hot walls hand their energy over. It doesn’t say what the light is like once it’s out.” He nodded at the tap behind the counter, which had a slow drip that Albert had been meaning to fix for some time. “That tap gives out water a drop at a time. That doesn’t mean water is made of drops.”

“And light’s a wave,” said John. “We know it is. In Chasing a Beam of Light we found it’s a wave of electric and magnetic forces. Shine it through two narrow slits and it makes stripes, bright and dark, where the waves from the two slits add up and cancel out, the way ripples from two pebbles do on a pond. Lumps don’t do that. And Maxwell’s equations got its speed right from a charged jar and a coil of wire.”

“Then you can’t both be right,” said Albert.

“One of us can,” said John.

“Perhaps.” Albert put down the last cup. “We shall not settle it by asking an oven. We must ask the light itself, after it has left the lamp.” He went to his cupboard.

Albert’s cupboard

What he brought out was a glass jar about the size of a large jam jar, with a brass rod through its stopper. At the bottom of the rod, inside the jar, hung two leaves of gold foil, side by side, so thin that they trembled when anyone breathed near them. On top of the rod, outside the jar, he screwed a round plate of dull grey metal, about the size of a saucer.

“An electroscope,” he said. “And a plate of zinc.”

He rubbed the zinc briskly with a scrap of emery paper until it shone. (Zinc left out in the air soon grows a dull skin, and the skin spoils the experiment.) Then he took a plastic comb from his apron pocket, ran it several times through the sleeve of his jumper, and touched it to the plate.

The two gold leaves sprang apart.

The comb had picked up electrons from the wool, as a balloon rubbed on a jumper does in the first journey, so it carried more negative charge than it should. Touching it to the plate let some of that extra charge spread out over the plate, down the rod and into both leaves. Both leaves now carried negative charge, and two charges of the same kind push each other apart. So the leaves pushed apart, and stood there in a narrow V. The jar kept damp air away from them, and they would stay like that for an hour or more. An electroscope is simply a way of seeing charge: while the leaves stand apart, the plate is charged, and if the charge leaks away, the leaves fall.

“Now,” said Albert. “Light.”

The brightest lamp in the café

The first lamp was the big red heat lamp from the doughnut warmer at the end of the counter, a heavy bulb in a polished reflector that glowed a deep, rich red and kept the morning’s doughnuts warm. It was easily the brightest thing in the café. Albert unhooked it, held it a hand’s width from the zinc, and switched it on.

The plate grew warm. The leaves didn’t move.

“Closer,” said John.

Albert held it closer, until the plate was too hot to touch. The leaves didn’t stir. He fetched a second heat lamp from the kitchen and shone the two together. Ten minutes went by. The plate was hot enough to fry an egg on, and the leaves stood exactly as far apart as before.

“Red light can’t do it,” said Albert, “however bright.”

A feeble ultraviolet lamp

The second lamp was much smaller: a thin tube of quartz, not much longer than a pencil, in a plain metal holder. It was the kind of lamp used to kill germs, and it gave out ultraviolet light, too short for any eye to see. Albert handed John and Cal each a pair of wraparound plastic glasses, put on a pair himself, and pointed the lamp carefully at the plate and away from all three of them.

Warning

Ultraviolet lamps made to kill germs give out short-wave ultraviolet light that can burn the eyes and skin within seconds, and you won’t feel it happening. This experiment is best left to Albert and to physics laboratories.

When he switched it on, the tube glowed a faint, cold blue, much fainter than the heat lamp; that blue was the small part of its light that the eye can see, and most of what came out of it couldn’t be seen at all. He stood a metre away from the plate.

The leaves fell. Not slowly, after a pause, but at once: they began to sag the moment the lamp came on, and within a few seconds they hung down together, side by side.

Albert recharged the plate with his comb. This time Cal carried the lamp right across the café, to the door, five or six metres away, where only a little of its light reached the zinc. The leaves began to fall the moment he switched it on. They fell more slowly, taking a minute or so to close, but they began at once.

“Now this,” said Albert. He took a small framed photograph off the wall, the café in some earlier decade, and gave it to John. “Hold the glass between the lamp and the plate.”

John held it up. Through the glass the lamp’s faint blue glow could still be seen perfectly well. The leaves didn’t move. John took the glass away, and they fell at once.

Ordinary glass lets visible light through but stops most ultraviolet: it’s why you don’t get sunburnt sitting behind a closed window. So it was the invisible ultraviolet part of the lamp’s light that was emptying the plate, not the faint blue glow you could see.

Last, Albert charged the plate the other way. He rubbed a glass rod on a silk scarf, which leaves the rod with too few electrons, so that it carries positive charge, and touched it to the zinc. The leaves sprang apart again, pushed apart this time by positive charge. He switched on the ultraviolet lamp, and nothing happened at all. The leaves stood apart for as long as anyone cared to watch.

“So whatever the light knocks out of the zinc is negative,” said Cal slowly. “Knock negative charge off a plate that’s already negative, and it loses its charge. Knock it off a positive plate, and the plate just pulls it back. It’s knocking out electrons.”

“It is,” said Albert.

He was right, and he wasn’t the first to find it. In 1887 Heinrich Hertz, the first person to make and detect radio waves, noticed that a spark jumped across a gap more easily when ultraviolet light fell on it, and that a sheet of glass in the way stopped the effect. In 1888 Wilhelm Hallwachs did exactly what Albert had just done, with a clean zinc plate on an electroscope: ultraviolet light emptied it of negative charge, and did nothing to positive. Once the electron had been found, in 1897, J. J. Thomson and Philipp Lenard showed, within a few years, that what the light knocks out are electrons.

Photoelectric effect

Light knocking electrons out of a material, usually a metal. Whether any come out at all depends on the light’s colour, not its brightness: from zinc, only ultraviolet light will do, however bright the red or blue. If they come out, they come out at once, even in very faint light; brighter light knocks out more electrons, but no faster ones, and bluer light knocks out faster ones. Explained by Einstein in 1905: light arrives in lumps, each sized by its colour, and each lump hands all of its energy to a single electron.

What would a wave do?

“Well,” said John, after a while, “waves can do that.”

“Show me,” said Cal.

John thought about the beach at the foot of the island’s cliffs, where the sea drags black pebbles up and down the shore. A big wave rolls big pebbles; a small one only stirs the gravel. But small waves, given long enough, rock even a heavy pebble loose in the end, each wave adding a little to its rocking.

That is how a wave delivers energy. It arrives spread out across the whole wave, steadily, all the time, a little at a time. A brighter light is a bigger wave, with more energy in it, arriving faster. A different colour is a different wavelength, crests closer together or further apart, but that changes how often the crests arrive, not how much energy they bring. So John’s picture made three predictions about Albert’s plate, and Cal wrote them on the back of a napkin.

  • Any colour should free electrons, if it’s bright enough, or if you wait long enough: red light, given long enough, would rock the electrons loose as surely as ultraviolet does.
  • Brighter light, a bigger wave, should shake the electrons harder and send them out faster.
  • Very faint light should take a while. Each electron would have to soak up energy from the light falling around it, a little at a time, until it had enough to escape, and in light as faint as Cal’s at the door, that would take minutes at least, and in fainter light hours.

Then Cal looked at the plate, and crossed all three out.

Red light freed nothing, however bright and however long. In 1902 Philipp Lenard, measuring how fast the electrons came out, found that making the light brighter changed how many came out but not how fast; only a different colour changed their speed. And the electrons came out at once, in light far fainter than the wave picture could explain. In 1928 Ernest Lawrence and Jesse Beams, flashing light at a metal surface in extremely brief pulses, found the electrons starting out within three billionths of a second of the light arriving. In light that faint, a wave would have kept them waiting for minutes, or hours.

Is there a way to rescue the wave? The obvious attempt is tuning. A wine glass rings at one particular note, and a singer who hits that note can make it shake hard enough to break, while louder singing at any other note does nothing. Perhaps the zinc’s electrons are tuned to ultraviolet, and only ultraviolet can shake them loose? But a tuned thing answers to one note, and the zinc answers to every colour beyond a threshold: every ultraviolet wavelength shorter than a certain one works, and the shorter it is, the faster the electrons leave. Tuning would also do nothing about the other two failures: the singer’s glass takes time to build up its shaking, and shakes harder for a louder note. Waves can’t be rescued that way, or, as it turned out, any other.

Each customer pays alone

The lumps explain everything at once.

Suppose light itself, not just the oven, comes in Planck’s lumps. Then a beam of light isn’t a smooth wave but a stream of separate lumps, each with a definite amount of energy, set by its colour: Planck’s constant times the light’s frequency. Red light’s lumps are small. Violet light’s are more than half as big again. Ultraviolet lumps are bigger still. A brighter light of the same colour is simply more lumps, each just as big as before.

Now suppose that when a lump is absorbed, it is absorbed whole, all at once, by one electron, which gets the lump’s entire energy. And suppose that to get out of the zinc, an electron has to pay a price: the metal holds on to its electrons, and it takes a definite amount of energy to tear one free (physicists call it the metal’s work function). For zinc, the price is about two fifths more than even a violet lump carries. Only lumps of ultraviolet light shorter than about 290 nanometres can pay it.

Albert turned over the card on the till. On the back, in the same handwriting, it said:

EACH CUSTOMER PAYS ALONE

Yesterday, at the till, the customers were the jostling atoms of an oven’s walls, and the things on sale were waves. Today the customers are the lumps of light, and what they’re buying is an electron’s freedom. Each lump arrives with exactly one note in its pocket, its own energy, and the size of the note is set by its colour. Freedom for one electron costs the zinc’s price.

A red lump has a note too small to pay. However many red lumps arrive, a thousand, a million, billions every second, none of them can buy an electron’s freedom, because each customer pays alone and no customer has enough. They can’t club together, and an electron can’t save up their lumps one by one, because each lump’s energy goes straight into jiggling the zinc’s atoms, and the plate just gets warm.

An ultraviolet lump has a big note. It pays at once, and its electron leaves the moment it arrives. And this time there’s change: whatever energy the lump had left over after paying the price goes with the electron, as its speed. The bigger the lump, the more change, and the faster the electron.

“You never give change,” said Cal.

“I don’t,” said Albert. “The zinc does.”

Brightness is the number of customers. A brighter ultraviolet lamp sends more lumps a second, so more electrons leave a second, and the leaves fall faster, but each lump carries the same note as before, so each electron leaves with the same change, at the same speed. A dimmer lamp sends fewer customers, but each one is served the moment it arrives, so the first electrons leave at once, however faint the light. And Albert’s sheet of glass was a doorman, turning away the customers with big notes and letting the others in.

Not every lump that could free an electron does. In real zinc most of them are simply absorbed by the metal and warm it, and only a small share knock an electron clean out. But no lump too small ever frees one, however many of them arrive.

“Hang on,” said John. “Yesterday the lumps were the oven’s doing. The walls could only hand energy over in lumps; the light was just carrying it. Couldn’t this be the zinc’s doing? The light arrives as a smooth wave, and the zinc can only take it in lumps.”

It was Cal’s dripping tap again, turned round, and it’s the one real way out. Suppose the zinc can only take energy in whole lumps, but the light’s energy arrives spread out, as a wave’s does. Then before an electron could take a lump, enough energy would have to have arrived in the patch round it to make up a whole lump. In bright light that might be quick. But in Cal’s faint light at the door, the energy falling on each electron’s patch arrives so slowly that it would take minutes, at the very least, for a lump’s worth to build up, and in fainter light hours. The electrons would have to wait, and they don’t: they leave at once, even in light so faint that, spread out evenly, it couldn’t have given any one of them a lump’s worth for hours. The only way an electron can have a whole lump’s worth of energy the moment the light arrives is if the energy arrived in that lump, all in one place. So the lumps aren’t the zinc’s doing. They’re in the light.

Put it as plainly as possible. Whether light can free an electron from zinc depends on its colour, not its brightness, and when it can, the electron comes out at once, however faint the light. A wave whose energy arrives spread out, more for brighter light, can do neither. Light that arrives in separate lumps, each sized by its colour and each absorbed whole by one electron, does both, and gets every other detail right too: more electrons for brighter light, faster electrons for bluer light, and the glass. There is no other way to read Albert’s plate. Light itself comes in lumps.

These lumps of light are what Albert, with his box of lamps, had called photons. Each carries a definite energy, more for bluer light and less for redder, exactly as he said; now Cal had watched a zinc plate prove it.

A young man in a patent office

The person who first read Albert’s plate this way worked, in 1905, as a technical expert at the Swiss patent office in Bern. In March that year, Albert Einstein sent the Annalen der Physik a paper about the production and transformation of light, whose title called its idea only “a heuristic point of view”: a way of looking at things that might prove useful. In it he took Planck’s lumps far more seriously than Planck did. Light, he proposed, really does consist of lumps of energy, each localised in space, moving without dividing, and absorbed or produced only whole. He showed that the idea explained the photoelectric effect, along with two other puzzles about light. Writing to his friend Conrad Habicht about the papers he was working on that spring, he called this one, alone of them, “very revolutionary”.

He also made a prediction sharp enough to test. If each lump hands its whole energy to one electron, then the fastest electrons’ energy of motion should rise in a straight line with the light’s frequency, and the line’s slope should be Planck’s constant itself: the same constant, measured from ovens, now measured from electrons, and the same for every metal. Only the starting point, the price, should change from one metal to another.

Hardly anyone believed it. Light was a wave; Maxwell’s equations said so, and the stripes from two slits showed it. Even in 1913, when Planck and three of his Berlin colleagues wrote to recommend Einstein for the Prussian Academy of Sciences, they asked that his hypothesis of light quanta not be held too heavily against him: a bold thinker, they said in effect, will sometimes overshoot.

In America, Robert Millikan set out to prove Einstein wrong. He spent about ten years on it, building apparatus in which he could clean his metal surfaces with a knife inside a vacuum, so that no skin of tarnish could spoil them. In 1916 he published the result. The electrons’ energy rose in an exact straight line with the light’s frequency, and its slope gave Planck’s constant to within about a per cent. Every prediction Einstein had made was confirmed. Years later Millikan wrote that he had been compelled to accept the result in spite of how unreasonable it seemed to him.

Einstein’s Nobel Prize in Physics, for 1921, was not awarded for relativity. It went to him “for his services to Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect”. (The committee held the 1921 prize over for a year, and announced it in November 1922, while Einstein was on his way to Japan.) Millikan’s own Nobel Prize, in 1923, was partly for confirming it.

“I hear they gave someone a prize for it,” said Albert, wiping the counter where it didn’t need wiping.

“You sound as if you were there,” said Cal.

“Everyone was somewhere,” said Albert, and went to see about the fryer.

How light can be a stream of lumps and also make stripes through two slits, like a wave, nobody in 1905 could say, Einstein included. That is the next journey’s question.

Counting the lumps

If light comes in lumps, why does it look so smooth? Because there are so many of them. A small red laser pointer, of the kind sold for presentations, gives out about a thousandth of a watt of light, and each lump of red light carries about a third of a billionth of a billionth of a joule. So the pointer sends out about three million billion lumps every second. Sand poured from a jug looks like a flowing liquid for the same reason: the grains are too small and too many to see.

For the mathematically inclinedHow many lumps a laser pointer sends out

Turn a light down, though, and the lumps don’t get smaller. A dimmer light of the same colour is the same lumps, fewer of them, as a light shower is the same raindrops as a downpour, fewer of them. Turn it down far enough and they come one at a time, at random moments, like the first drops on a dry pavement: one here, a pause, then two together, then one over there. Nobody can say when the next will arrive, or exactly where, only how often, on average, they come.

To see the lumps one at a time, make the light faint enough and use something that can count them. The photoelectric effect itself can. From the 1930s, physicists built photomultipliers: a lump of light frees one electron from a metal surface, and the electron is pulled at speed into a second surface, where it knocks out several more, which are pulled into a third, and so on, a dozen times over, until a single lump of light has become a pulse of millions of electrons, big enough to count. Each lump makes one click. Your own eyes come close: experiments on single rod cells, the cells at the back of the eye that see in dim light, have shown that one of them can respond to a single lump of light, though it takes several arriving together before you notice anything.

So does the camera on John’s telescopes. In his article on how astro cameras work, on Optica, every pixel of the sensor is a small patch of silicon, and the light works on it by the photoelectric effect, inside the silicon rather than out of it: each lump of light that’s caught frees one electron from its place in the silicon’s crystal, and the pixel holds the freed electrons until the camera counts them. Silicon has a price too, a much lower one than zinc’s, and lumps of infrared light longer than about 1,100 nanometres can’t pay it, so the camera is blind beyond there. Twice the light frees twice the electrons, so the count measures the light, lump by lump.

And for a faint nebula, the count is small. John’s article works through an example, with illustrative numbers: in a five-minute exposure, a faint nebula gives one pixel about 200 electrons. That’s fewer than one lump of light a second, for a picture that looks, once it’s finished, perfectly smooth. Every picture on Nyx is, underneath, a count of single lumps of light.

The Van, one speck at a time

That night, after the coaches had gone, Cal set the probe on the wall at the far end of the car park and pointed it back at The Van, with the café behind it.

The car park was dark. The only light came from the café’s window, the stars, the one headlight Cal had left on, and the dashboard screen inside The Van, which was showing, for reasons nobody had ever discovered, a picture of a kettle. Cal turned the probe’s camera up as far as it would go. The probe gave a small beep and lit a message on its side.

SENSITIVITY: MAXIMUM. PLEASE DO NOT SWITCH ON ANY LIGHTS

On Cal’s phone, the screen was black. Then a single speck of light appeared, in the bottom left-hand corner. Then another, somewhere near the top. Then three more, one after another, in no pattern at all.

“It’s broken,” said John.

“It’s the only thing in The Van that’s never broken,” said Cal. “Wait.”

The specks kept coming, each one whole, each one in a single place, each one a lump of light that had travelled from the café’s window or a star or the kettle on The Van’s screen into the probe’s camera. Where they landed was a matter of chance. But they landed more often where more light came from: a cluster grew where the café’s window was, another at the headlight. Slowly, the specks began to crowd into a shape, and the shape was The Van.

The figure below is the probe’s picture. Lengthen the exposure, or play it, and watch the picture build up from single photons. At first each dot is one lump of light, landing at random. Beyond a few thousand there are too many to draw, and each pixel’s square is shaded by how many have landed in it. Then press “If light were a smooth wave” and see what a wave would give instead: the whole picture at once, faint, every pixel getting a sliver of a lump’s worth from the very start.

With the lumps there is never a faint, smooth picture underneath, only dots. A short exposure isn’t a dim version of the picture; it’s a scatter of single photons, each one complete, and the picture is in where they land most often. A smooth wave would have given every pixel a little of the light at once, and even the shortest exposure would have shown The Van, only fainter. Cameras never do that.

Back to Teide

After an hour, the picture on Cal’s phone was The Van, unmistakably, with the café’s window, the dark bulk of Teide behind and a scatter of stars over it, and the kettle glowing faintly on the dashboard.

“So it’s lumps,” said John. “All of it. Every bit of light there is.”

“All of it,” said Cal, and then he frowned. “Then what about the stripes?”

“What stripes?”

“The two slits. You said it yourself this morning. Light through two slits makes stripes, because the waves from the two slits add up in some places and cancel in others. That’s how everyone knew light was a wave.” He held up the phone, with its picture of single specks. “But a lump goes somewhere. It can’t go through both slits.”

John opened his mouth, and closed it again.

Albert was turning off the lights in the café, one by one. On Cal’s phone, as each light went out, the specks from the window came more rarely.

“In the morning,” said Albert, from the doorway, “I shall need a laser pointer and a hair.”

Cal looked thoughtfully at Albert’s hair.

“Not mine,” said Albert.
