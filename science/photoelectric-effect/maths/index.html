How long a wave would take

If light were only a wave, its energy would arrive spread evenly over the wave’s front, at a rate given by its intensity III, in watts on each square metre. An electron in the zinc could only soak up the energy falling on a patch round it. A generous classical guess for the patch is the size of an atom, about

σ≈10−19 m2,\sigma \approx 10^{-19}\ \text{m}^2,σ≈10−19 m2,

a circle about 0.18 nanometres in radius. To escape, the electron needs zinc’s work function, about W=4.3W = 4.3W=4.3 eV =6.9×10−19= 6.9 \times 10^{-19}=6.9×10−19 J, so it would have to wait

t≈WIσ.t \approx \frac{W}{I \sigma}.t≈IσW​.

Suppose Albert’s lamp gives out about a watt of ultraviolet, spread evenly in every direction. A metre away, the intensity is 1/(4π×12)≈0.081/(4\pi \times 1^2) \approx 0.081/(4π×12)≈0.08 W m⁻², and

t≈6.9×10−190.08×10−19≈90 s.t \approx \frac{6.9 \times 10^{-19}}{0.08 \times 10^{-19}} \approx 90\ \text{s}.t≈0.08×10−196.9×10−19​≈90 s.

From the door, about five metres away, the intensity is 25 times smaller, 3.2×10−33.2 \times 10^{-3}3.2×10−3 W m⁻², and the wait would be about 2,2002{,}2002,200 s, over half an hour. In the figure’s dimmest light, a thousandth of a watt on each square metre, it would be about 6,9006{,}9006,900 s, nearly two hours; in its brightest, a watt on each square metre, about 7 s. In fact the leaves begin to fall the moment the lamp comes on, and Lawrence and Beams (1928) found electrons leaving within 3×10−93 \times 10^{-9}3×10−9 s of the light arriving. Making the patch a hundred times bigger wouldn’t rescue the wave: the wait would still be far longer than that.

The lumps need no waiting. In light from the door, the lumps landing on a plate of 5 by 4 centimetres, 0.0020.0020.002 m², number

IAhf=3.2×10−3×0.0027.8×10−19≈8×1012\frac{I A}{h f} = \frac{3.2 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.002}{7.8 \times 10^{-19}} \approx 8 \times 10^{12}hfIA​=7.8×10−193.2×10−3×0.002​≈8×1012

every second, and each one that frees an electron does so as it arrives.

(One honest exception: in the light of very powerful lasers, billions of times more intense than any lamp, an electron can occasionally absorb two lumps at once and escape with their combined energy. That is still lumps, two at a time.)

If one lump gives all its energy to one electron, and the electron must pay the metal’s work function WWW to escape, the fastest electrons leave with what’s left as energy of motion:

12mvmax2=hf−W.\tfrac{1}{2} m v_\text{max}^2 = h f - W.21​mvmax2​=hf−W.

This is Einstein’s equation of 1905. For zinc, W≈4.3W \approx 4.3W≈4.3 eV (measured values run from about 3.6 to 4.9, depending on the surface), so nothing escapes unless hf>Whf > Whf>W: light of wavelength shorter than

λ0=hcW=1,240 eV nm4.3 eV≈288 nm.\lambda_0 = \frac{h c}{W} = \frac{1{,}240\ \text{eV nm}}{4.3\ \text{eV}} \approx 288\ \text{nm}.λ0​=Whc​=4.3 eV1,240 eV nm​≈288 nm.

Red and violet lumps fall short, by however many of them arrive. At 254 nm, the fastest electrons have 4.88−4.3=0.584.88 - 4.3 = 0.584.88−4.3=0.58 eV, so

vmax=2×0.58×1.602×10−199.109×10−31≈4.5×105 m/s,v_\text{max} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 0.58 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}}{9.109 \times 10^{-31}}} \approx 4.5 \times 10^5\ \text{m/s},vmax​=9.109×10−312×0.58×1.602×10−19​​≈4.5×105 m/s,

about 450 kilometres a second; at 200 nm, with 1.90 eV to spare, about 820. Brightness doesn’t appear in the equation at all: a brighter light of the same colour sends more lumps, so more electrons leave, each with the same energy.

against frequency, the stopping voltage is a straight line, with slope h/e=4.14×10−15h/e = 4.14 \times 10^{-15}h/e=4.14×10−15 V s for every metal, and only its starting point, set by WWW, different from one metal to another. Millikan’s line, in 1916, gave h≈6.57×10−34h \approx 6.57 \times 10^{-34}h≈6.57×10−34 J s, within about one per cent of today’s exact value.

Silicon. In a camera’s silicon the electron needn’t leave the material, only be freed from its place in the crystal, which takes the band gap, 1.121.121.12 eV. The longest wavelength that can do it is 1,240/1.12≈1,1001{,}240/1.12 \approx 1{,}1001,240/1.12≈1,100 nm, in the near infrared: beyond that the sensor is blind.

N=10−33.06×10−19≈3.3×1015N = \frac{10^{-3}}{3.06 \times 10^{-19}} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{15}N=3.06×10−1910−3​≈3.3×1015

Counting the lumps in a picture

John’s article on how astro cameras work has a worked example, with illustrative numbers: in a five-minute frame of a faint nebula, the nebula gives one pixel about 200 electrons. Each electron was freed by one lump of light, and not every lump that reaches a pixel is caught: the colour version of the sensor his article describes catches about 80 per cent at best. So the 200 electrons stand for about 250 lumps of light arriving in 300 seconds,

250300≈0.8 lumps a second:\frac{250}{300} \approx 0.8\ \text{lumps a second}:300250​≈0.8 lumps a second:

fewer than one a second, to each pixel.

Because the lumps arrive at random, the count in a pixel scatters from one frame to the next. For random, independent arrivals, the spread is the square root of the count: about 200≈14\sqrt{200} \approx 14200​≈14 electrons for 200. That scatter is the shot noise his article describes, which no camera can remove. It is the lumpiness of light, showing in the picture.
