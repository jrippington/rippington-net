Counting the waves in an oven

Take an oven shaped like a cube of side LLL, with walls that reflect light perfectly. A wave can keep going inside it only if it fits: across each direction a whole number of half-waves must fit between opposite walls. The waves that fit have wavevectors (arrows pointing along the wave, of length 2π/λ2\pi/\lambda2π/λ)

k=πL (nx,ny,nz),nx,ny,nz=1,2,3,…\mathbf{k} = \frac{\pi}{L}\,(n_x, n_y, n_z), \qquad n_x, n_y, n_z = 1, 2, 3, \dotsk=Lπ​(nx​,ny​,nz​),nx​,ny​,nz​=1,2,3,…

Each allowed wave is a point on a grid with spacing π/L\pi/Lπ/L, and each comes in two polarisations. The waves with wavevectors shorter than KKK are the grid points inside an eighth of a sphere of radius KKK (only positive nnn‘s count), so there are

N(K)=2×18×4π3(KLπ)3=VK33π2,N(K) = 2 \times \frac{1}{8} \times \frac{4\pi}{3} \left(\frac{K L}{\pi}\right)^3 = \frac{V K^3}{3\pi^2},N(K)=2×81​×34π​(πKL​)3=3π2VK3​,

where V=L3V = L^3V=L3 is the oven’s volume. In terms of wavelength, K=2π/λK = 2\pi/\lambdaK=2π/λ, the number of waves with wavelengths longer than λ\lambdaλ is

N(λ)=8πV3λ3.N(\lambda) = \frac{8\pi V}{3 \lambda^3}.N(λ)=3λ38πV​.

Halve λ\lambdaλ and NNN goes up eight times: twice as many fit in each of three directions. The number in a small range of wavelengths dλd\lambdadλ is

dN=8πVλ4 dλ,dN = \frac{8\pi V}{\lambda^4}\, d\lambda,dN=λ48πV​dλ,

which grows without limit as λ→0\lambda \to 0λ→0. (The result doesn’t depend on the oven’s shape, only on its volume, once the oven is many wavelengths across.) The figure’s top strip shows the count per step of log⁡λ\log\lambdalogλ, which goes as λ dN/dλ∝λ−3\lambda\,dN/d\lambda \propto \lambda^{-3}λdN/dλ∝λ−3: three powers of ten for every power of ten in wavelength.

The sharing rule. By equipartition, every independent way of holding energy gets, on average, 12kT\tfrac{1}{2}kT21​kT for each squared term in its energy, where k=1.380649×10−23k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}k=1.380649×10−23 J/K is Boltzmann’s constant and TTT the temperature in kelvin. A wave, like a pendulum, has two (its electric and magnetic energy), so each gets kTkTkT. The energy in the oven per unit volume, per unit wavelength, is then

uλ=8πλ4 kT,u_\lambda = \frac{8\pi}{\lambda^4}\, kT,uλ​=λ48π​kT,

and the brightness of the light coming out of a small hole, per unit wavelength (its spectral radiance), is Bλ=(c/4π) uλB_\lambda = (c/4\pi)\, u_\lambdaBλ​=(c/4π)uλ​:

BλRJ=2c kTλ4.B_\lambda^{\text{RJ}} = \frac{2 c\, k T}{\lambda^4}.BλRJ​=λ42ckT​.

This is Rayleigh and Jeans’s law. It fits the measurements at long wavelengths, and climbs without limit at short ones. The total energy is

∫0∞uλ dλ=8πkT∫0∞dλλ4=∞:\int_0^\infty u_\lambda \, d\lambda = 8\pi k T \int_0^\infty \frac{d\lambda}{\lambda^4} = \infty:∫0∞​uλ​dλ=8πkT∫0∞​λ4dλ​=∞:

the ultraviolet catastrophe. An oven obeying it could never come to any temperature, since the light inside could absorb unlimited energy. In an oven at 1,150 °C, the classical glow is already nearly 29 times the true peak’s height at the true peak’s wavelength, and it leaves the figure’s sheet at about 4,700 nanometres, still in the infrared.

The size of a lump

Planck’s lumps (quanta) have energy

ε=hf=hcλ,\varepsilon = h f = \frac{h c}{\lambda},ε=hf=λhc​,

with h=6.62607015×10−34h = 6.62607015 \times 10^{-34}h=6.62607015×10−34 J s, Planck’s constant (exact since 2019), and fff the frequency. For red light at 650 nm, f=c/λ=4.61×1014f = c/\lambda = 4.61 \times 10^{14}f=c/λ=4.61×1014 Hz and

ε=6.626×10−34×4.61×1014=3.06×10−19 J,\varepsilon = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 4.61 \times 10^{14} = 3.06 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J},ε=6.626×10−34×4.61×1014=3.06×10−19 J,

about a third of a billionth of a billionth of a joule. Physicists often use the electronvolt, 1 eV=1.602×10−191\ \text{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}1 eV=1.602×10−19 J, the energy an electron gains crossing one volt; the red lump is 1.911.911.91 eV. A violet lump (400 nm) is 3.103.103.10 eV, and an ultraviolet one at 254 nm 4.884.884.88 eV.

Against the jostle. The typical share of the heat is kTkTkT. At room temperature, 300 K,

kT=1.381×10−23×300=4.14×10−21 J=0.0259 eV,kT = 1.381 \times 10^{-23} \times 300 = 4.14 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J} = 0.0259\ \text{eV},kT=1.381×10−23×300=4.14×10−21 J=0.0259 eV,

so the red lump is 1.91/0.0259≈741.91/0.0259 \approx 741.91/0.0259≈74 times the typical share. The chance that a given red wave holds even one lump is about e−ε/kT=e−74≈10−32e^{-\varepsilon/kT} = e^{-74} \approx 10^{-32}e−ε/kT=e−74≈10−32, which is why nothing at room temperature glows red. In lava at 1,150 °C (T=1,423T = 1{,}423T=1,423 K), kT=0.123kT = 0.123kT=0.123 eV, nearly five times as much; a lump of green light (500 nm, 2.482.482.48 eV) is still about 20 times that, and e−20≈2×10−9e^{-20} \approx 2 \times 10^{-9}e−20≈2×10−9. That is small, but the lava has trillions upon trillions of atoms, and it glows.

Planck’s law

Suppose a wave of frequency fff can hold only whole lumps: energy 0,ε,2ε,3ε,…0, \varepsilon, 2\varepsilon, 3\varepsilon, \dots0,ε,2ε,3ε,…, with ε=hf\varepsilon = hfε=hf. In something at temperature TTT, Boltzmann’s rule gives the chance of energy EEE as in proportion to e−E/kTe^{-E/kT}e−E/kT. Write x=ε/kTx = \varepsilon / kTx=ε/kT. The chances add up to

Z=∑n=0∞e−nx=11−e−x,Z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-n x} = \frac{1}{1 - e^{-x}},Z=n=0∑∞​e−nx=1−e−x1​,

That fraction, x/(ex−1)x/(e^x - 1)x/(ex−1), is each wave’s share of the heat as a fraction of the classical kTkTkT: the figure’s middle strip. For long waves, x≪1x \ll 1x≪1, it is nearly 1, and the classical rule is recovered. For short waves, x≫1x \gg 1x≫1, it is about xe−xx e^{-x}xe−x, which dies away very fast: the short waves are starved.

Multiplying the number of waves by each one’s share gives Planck’s law:

Bλ(T)=2hc2λ5 1ehc/λkT−1.B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2 h c^2}{\lambda^5}\, \frac{1}{e^{h c / \lambda k T} - 1}.Bλ​(T)=λ52hc2​ehc/λkT−11​.

For long wavelengths, ehc/λkT−1≈hc/λkTe^{hc/\lambda kT} - 1 \approx hc/\lambda kTehc/λkT−1≈hc/λkT, and it becomes Rayleigh and Jeans’s law. Integrated over all wavelengths it is finite:

∫0∞Bλ dλ=2π4k415 c2h3 T4,\int_0^\infty B_\lambda\, d\lambda = \frac{2\pi^4 k^4}{15\, c^2 h^3}\, T^4,∫0∞​Bλ​dλ=15c2h32π4k4​T4,

so a hot body’s total glow, per square metre of its surface, is σT4\sigma T^4σT4 with σ=2π5k4/15c2h3=5.67×10−8\sigma = 2\pi^5 k^4/15 c^2 h^3 = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}σ=2π5k4/15c2h3=5.67×10−8 W m⁻² K⁻⁴ (Stefan and Boltzmann’s law). Double the temperature, and the glow is sixteen times as bright.

Why the peak moves with the temperature

The glow is brightest where dBλ/dλ=0dB_\lambda/d\lambda = 0dBλ​/dλ=0. With x=hc/λkTx = hc/\lambda kTx=hc/λkT, Bλ∝x5/(ex−1)B_\lambda \propto x^5/(e^x - 1)Bλ​∝x5/(ex−1) at a fixed temperature, so the peak is where

λpeak=hc4.965 kT=2.898 mm KT,\lambda_\text{peak} = \frac{h c}{4.965\, k T} = \frac{2.898\ \text{mm K}}{T},λpeak​=4.965kThc​=T2.898 mm K​,

The Sun’s peak is green, yet the Sun looks white, because its glow is spread right across the visible band: the eye sees the mixture, not the peak. (Spread the same glow out by frequency instead of wavelength and its peak lands somewhere else, at x=2.82x = 2.82x=2.82, about 880 nm for the Sun. “Where it’s brightest” depends on how you lay the spectrum out; the shape and the share in each band don’t.)

The doughnut’s visible share, three parts in a thousand billion billion, is a share of the power, and it isn’t quite nothing. A doughnut about nine centimetres across has a surface of about 0.02 m20.02\ \text{m}^20.02 m2, and at 60 °C its whole glow is about σT4×0.02≈14\sigma T^4 \times 0.02 \approx 14σT4×0.02≈14 watts, about 102110^{21}1021 lumps of infrared every second. Counting the lumps in the visible band the same way, it gives out about one lump of visible light, almost always deep red, every six seconds.
