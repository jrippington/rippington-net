Why hot things glow, and the lumps nobody wanted

Albert lights the fryer, the probe turns out to see in the infrared, and a fresh doughnut glows impeccably. Then a simple question, why a glow has a colour at all, leads by every rule physics had in 1900 to the conclusion that every coal fire should blaze with X-rays. The way out cost a careful man his peace of mind: energy, it seems, comes in lumps.

The fryer warms up

The grain of sugar was still there in the morning, under its upturned glass at the end of the counter, with its napkin propped against the glass.

DO NOT EAT

Albert was lighting the gas under the fryer. It was a deep steel tank of oil that took most of an hour to come up to temperature, and until it did, there would be no doughnuts. The batter waited beside it under a cloth. Outside, the sun had only just cleared the ridge, and the old lava of the plateau still had the night’s chill in it.

John and Cal sat down at their table by the window. Nobody mentioned the atoms, which by every rule of 1911 should have collapsed overnight and hadn’t. It seemed tactless.

“You said we’d start with the second cloud,” said John.

“I did.” Albert adjusted the flame. “Lord Kelvin’s second cloud: a rule for how heat shares itself out. It worked beautifully almost everywhere, and Kelvin was worried about the few places where it didn’t. He was right to worry. It failed far worse somewhere he hadn’t looked.” He nodded at the fryer. “Come and hold your hand beside this. Beside it, please. Not in it.”

John went behind the counter and held his palm a hand’s width from the side of the tank. The oil was nowhere near hot enough to fry anything yet. It wasn’t glowing, it wasn’t smoking, and it wasn’t making a sound. But his palm grew warm, and when he moved his hand away, the warmth went.

“It’s hot,” he said.

“Something is reaching your hand,” said Albert. “What?”

John thought about it. “Hot air?”

“Hot air rises. Your hand is beside the tank, not above it.”

“Then the heat’s coming across the gap.”

“It is. And it would come across the gap if there were no air at all.” Albert turned the flame up a little. “The Sun warms your face across a hundred and fifty million kilometres of empty space. The fryer is doing the same thing, on a smaller scale. It is shining on you.”

John looked at the dull grey side of the tank. It was not, by any ordinary meaning of the word, shining.

Light nobody can see

What reached John’s hand was light, of a kind no eye can see. It was found in 1800 by William Herschel, the astronomer who had discovered the planet Uranus. He passed sunlight through a glass prism, which spread it into a band of colours, red at one end and violet at the other, and he put thermometers in the different colours to see which warmed them most. Red warmed them more than violet. Then he put a thermometer just past the red end of the band, where there was no light at all to see, and it warmed most of all. Something invisible was coming out of the prism beyond the red. Herschel called it “calorific rays”, rays of heat. We call it infrared, “below the red”.

In Chasing a Beam of Light, John and Cal found that light is a wave of electric and magnetic forces, and that its colour is set by its wavelength, the distance from one crest of the wave to the next. Visible light’s waves are tiny. Red light’s are about 700 nanometres long (a nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre) and violet light’s about 400, with orange, yellow, green and blue in between. But the waves don’t stop where our eyes do. Longer than red are infrared waves, and longer still are microwaves and radio waves. Shorter than violet are ultraviolet waves, which give you sunburn, and shorter still are X-rays and gamma rays. They are all the same kind of wave, and they all travel at the same speed. Our eyes happen to be built for a narrow band of them in the middle.

The whole range, set out from the longest waves to the shortest, has a name, and so does the way any particular light is spread across it.

Spectrum

The range of light’s wavelengths, from radio waves, which can be metres long, through infrared, visible light and ultraviolet to X-rays and gamma rays, far smaller than an atom. The visible part is only a narrow band in the middle: a prism or a raindrop spreads it out as the colours of the rainbow. The word is also used for how one particular source’s light is spread across those wavelengths: the Sun’s spectrum, a lamp’s spectrum, the spectrum of the glow from the fryer.

“So the fryer’s glowing,” said Cal, “in a colour none of us can see.”

“So are you,” said Albert. “So is your coffee. So is everything in this café, and the mountain outside.”

“Prove it,” said Cal.

A doughnut, seen properly

Cal went out to The Van for the probe. It had lived in the cupboard behind the kettle since the first of all their journeys: a battered box about the size of a microwave oven, with a camera on every face, a blinking green light and a ruler painted along one side for reasons nobody could remember, and still the only piece of equipment on board that had never broken down. Cal brought it in from the car park, set it on the table and went through its settings, which he found in a handbook written, as far as anyone could tell, for a commercial dishwasher. On the third page, under a symbol nobody recognised, was a setting for one of its cameras that the handbook described only as “warm”.

The probe agreed, after some argument, to send its pictures to Cal’s phone.

The café changed completely. The camera saw infrared waves about fifteen times as long as red light’s, the kind warm things give out most of, and in its pictures everything shone by its own light, the warmest brightest. John’s face was bright, apart from the tip of his nose, which was cooler. Cal’s cup of coffee was a bright pool of light. The window, which by daylight was perfectly clear, was a dark, dull mirror: glass, which lets visible light straight through, stops these long waves almost entirely. The upturned glass at the end of the counter had become an opaque dome, and the grain of sugar under it had vanished. Albert looked pleased.

Then he lifted the first doughnut of the day out of the oil, rolled it in lemon sugar and put it on a plate in front of the probe.

On Cal’s phone it blazed: the brightest thing in the room, in its familiar shape. Its infrared emissions were, apparently, impeccable.

“That,” said John, “is a beautiful picture of a doughnut.”

“It’s a picture of how warm the doughnut is,” said Cal. He held the phone up to the room. “And of how warm everything else is. The table’s glowing. The chairs are glowing. You’re glowing.”

“Everything is,” said Albert. “Anything with any warmth in it at all. The colder it is, the fainter and longer its waves, but there is nothing in this room, or on the mountain, that is not shining.”

Thermal radiation

The light that anything gives out simply because it is warm. The atoms in everything are always jostling, and the warmer it is, the harder they jostle; jostling electric charges give out light. Everything warmer than absolute zero, the coldest anything can be (about −273 °C), gives out thermal radiation. At everyday temperatures nearly all of it is infrared, which our eyes can’t see; heat something to about 500 °C and the first of it reaches the visible, as a dull red glow. Hotter things give out far more of it, and at shorter wavelengths.

Why should warm things give out light at all? The answer goes back to the first journey. Everything is made of atoms, and atoms contain electric charges: electrons, light and negative, round a heavy, positive nucleus. In anything warm, the atoms are never still. In a solid they jiggle about their places, and in a gas they fly about and collide; the warmer it is, the harder they jiggle. And in the first journey John and Cal found that a charge which changes its motion sends out electromagnetic waves, which is how a radio mast works and why the planet-style atom couldn’t last. Jiggling charges are always changing their motion, so everything warm is a crowd of tiny, jostling radio masts, sending out light. The harder they jiggle, the more light they send, and the shorter its waves.

Red hot, white hot

Heat a poker in a fire, and you can watch that happen. For a long time nothing shows, though you can feel the warmth from it at a distance, as John felt the fryer. Then, at about 500 °C, it begins to glow a dull, sullen red, faint enough that you’d only notice it in a dim room. In 1847 the chemist John William Draper heated all sorts of things, metals, stone and brick among them, and found that every one of them began to glow at almost the same temperature, about 525 °C, whatever it was made of. Hotter, the red brightens to cherry, then to orange, then yellow. Hotter still, it glows white: a lamp’s filament, at about 2,500 °C, is white-hot. For centuries, long before there were thermometers that could stand the heat, blacksmiths and potters judged their fires by exactly these colours.

What’s glowing hardly matters. A lump of iron, a brick and a pot, heated to the same temperature, glow almost exactly the same colour. Inside a closed oven it matters not at all. If you could look in through a small hole in the door of an oven at full heat, everything inside, the walls, the shelves and whatever was on them, would glow with the same light, so evenly that it would be hard to make out where one thing ended and the next began. In 1859 the German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff showed why that must be so: inside a closed oven, once everything has settled to the same temperature, the light depends on the temperature and on nothing else. Not the walls’ material, not their shape, not their colour by daylight. Light coming out through a small hole in a hot oven is the purest possible example of a glow. It’s sometimes called “black-body radiation”, for a reason that’s easy to see: a small hole in an oven looks perfectly black when the oven is cold, because any light going in gets lost inside and never comes out.

So the colour of a glow is a thermometer. It tells you how hot something is, whatever it’s made of.

The lava under the café

The café stands on something that once glowed. The plateau round it is lava, flow upon flow of rock that came out of the ground molten and glowing, spread across the landscape, and set.

The island’s ordinary lava is basalt, the dark, runny rock of most volcanoes, and it erupts at about 1,100 to 1,200 °C, glowing orange-yellow where it flows in the open and dull red where its surface begins to crust. Teide’s own lava was different: thick, sticky stuff called phonolite, cooler when it erupted, at about 900 °C, and redder. High on the mountain’s flank, the black tongues of rock called the Lavas Negras look as though they set last week. They’re about 1,150 years old, and they’re the last lava Teide’s summit has given out. The mountain hasn’t erupted in all the time anyone has written things down, but the island round it has, many times. Columbus is said to have logged an eruption as he sailed past in 1492, on his way to the Americas; in 1706 a lava flow buried the old harbour of Garachico; in 1798 the flank of Pico Viejo, Teide’s neighbour, split open; and the most recent, Chinyero, erupted in 1909.

And across the sea, La Palma erupted only a few years ago. On a clear day, from parts of the plateau, it shows as a long grey shape on the western horizon, and John wanted to see it.

So, after the second doughnut, they took The Van across the plateau, with the probe on the dashboard. The road climbed, and The Van, which had been running the kettle at the same time, grew hotter and hotter, and somewhere on the long climb a warning came up on the dashboard.

ENGINE GLOWING. THIS IS NOT A FEATURE

They pulled over. Cal pointed the probe at the bonnet. On his phone the engine was a furnace, brighter than anything else in the landscape, and had been for some time; it had been glowing in the infrared long before it was hot enough to glow in any way they could see. John lifted the bonnet and peered into the shadow underneath, and there, deep in the engine, something was glowing a dull, sullen red.

“Five hundred degrees,” said Cal. “At least.”

“It’s a camper van,” said John. “Not a kiln.”

The Van said nothing, which was unlike it, and ticked as it cooled.

While they waited, John asked the simulator to show them La Palma’s eruption. The simulator, which could rebuild anything that had ever been measured, rebuilt the night of 3 October 2021, two weeks into it, and set them, for their own safety, on a hillside on the far side of the valley.

The eruption had begun on 19 September 2021, when a crack opened in the Cumbre Vieja ridge in the south of the island and a new volcano began to build itself, a cone the islanders came to call Tajogaite. It erupted for eighty-five days, until 13 December. Its lava came out at about 1,140 °C, and flowed down through farms and villages to the sea, which it reached on 28 September. By the end it had destroyed nearly three thousand buildings and buried nearly a thousand hectares of farmland, and about seven thousand people had been moved from their homes.

On the screen, rivers of lava ran down the mountainside in the dark, orange-yellow where they flowed fast in the open and red where their skins were cooling, and at the vent the fountains of molten rock were a fierce, dazzling yellow. Then Cal switched to the probe’s warm camera, and the whole hillside lit up. The lava’s dark crust, which by eye was barely there, blazed. Ash and rock that had set hours before, black to the eye, still glowed in the infrared. The ground for a long way round them, warmed by them, glowed faintly too.

“There’s far more glow than colour,” said John.

“Most of it’s the bit we can’t see,” said Cal. “Again.”

When The Van had cooled, they drove on to the place where the road looked out to the west. La Palma was there on the horizon, long and grey and quite still.

Turn up the heat

The stars are glowing the same way, and you can see their temperatures from the plateau on any clear night. In winter, look at Orion. At its top left is Betelgeuse, distinctly orange, with a surface at about 3,300 °C. At its bottom right is Rigel, blue-white, at about 12,000 °C. The Sun, at about 5,500 °C at its surface, sits between them: white, if you could see it from space, though the air makes it look a little yellow from the ground. The colour of a star is a thermometer reading from hundreds of light-years away.

Put all of this together and the pattern is plain. The hotter something is, the more light it gives out, and the further its glow moves from the infrared, through red, orange and yellow, to white and then blue. The figure below lets you watch it. At the top is an oven, seen through a small hole in its door, with the hole glowing as your eye would see it, and beside it a scale of temperatures, with some familiar things marked on it: a warm doughnut, a hob, the island’s lava, a lamp’s filament, the Sun, and the two stars of Orion. Underneath is the glow itself, spread out by wavelength, from the ultraviolet on the left, through the narrow band of colours our eyes can see, to the far infrared on the right. The curve shows how much of the glow comes out at each wavelength, and the part of it that falls inside the visible band is filled in with its colours.

Play it, or slide the temperature, and watch three things. The whole curve slides to the left as the temperature rises, towards shorter waves: the dashed line marks its brightest wavelength. The hole goes from dark, to dull red, to orange, to white, to blue-white. And the table on the sheet shows how much of the glow is light you can actually see.

The numbers are startling. A warm doughnut’s glow is all infrared: its brightest waves are about nine thousand nanometres long, and the share of it you could see doesn’t bear writing down. The island’s lava, glowing a fierce orange-yellow, gives out less than a thousandth of its glow as visible light; the rest, more than 99.9 per cent, is infrared, which is why the probe’s camera found so much more of it than their eyes did. Even a lamp’s white-hot filament gives out only about 8 per cent of its glow as light; the other 92 per cent is heat, which is why old-fashioned light bulbs made such good hand-warmers and such poor lamps. The Sun, much hotter, gives out a little under half its light as visible, about as much again as infrared, and a tenth as ultraviolet. Most of Rigel’s glow, nearly three fifths of it, is ultraviolet.

Why does a glow have a colour?

They were back at the café by lunchtime, and the fryer was at full heat. John had been thinking.

“The hotter it is, the harder the atoms jiggle, and the more light they give out,” he said. “That makes sense. But why does the colour change? And why is there a colour at all? Why doesn’t a hot thing just glow a bit of everything, more and more of it as it gets hotter?”

“Now you have reached Lord Kelvin’s cloud,” said Albert.

He sat down with them, which he rarely did at lunchtime, and took out a napkin.

“In the nineteenth century,” he said, “physicists worked out a rule for how heat shares itself out. It was very beautiful, and it was very well tested. Then they applied it to light in an oven, and it gave an answer so wrong that it could not be repaired.”

The rule for sharing heat

Pour hot water into cold, and it soon settles to one temperature in between. Put a cold spoon in hot tea, and the spoon warms and the tea cools until they’re the same. Heat always spreads out like this, from hotter things to colder ones, until it’s evenly shared. The nineteenth century’s great discovery was what that sharing looks like among the atoms themselves.

Think of a box of air. Its molecules fly about at random, colliding with each other and with the walls, billions of times a second. Fast molecules hit slow ones and share some of their speed; slow ones are knocked faster. Like coins changing hands in a market crowd, the energy is always being passed about, and however it starts, it ends up shared out, on average, equally. And not only equally among the molecules, but equally among every separate way each molecule can move: flying left and right, up and down, forwards and back, spinning, and so on. Every one of those ways of moving gets, on average, the same share of the heat, and the share is set by the temperature. Double the temperature (counted from absolute zero) and every share doubles.

That is the rule for sharing heat. In the 1860s and 1870s James Clerk Maxwell and Ludwig Boltzmann worked it out, and it explained a great deal: how hard a gas pushes on its container, how fast sound travels through it, how much heat it takes to warm it up. In 1900, when Lord Kelvin gave his lecture on the two clouds, it was called the Maxwell-Boltzmann doctrine of the partition of energy: “partition” meaning sharing out. Kelvin’s own worry was about gases. Some of them took less heat to warm up than the rule said they should, as though some of their molecules’ ways of moving were refusing their share, and nobody could see why. But its worst failure was with light.

Sharing heat with light

Inside a hot oven, the walls’ atoms are jiggling, and they send out light; the light bounces about between the walls and gets absorbed again, and the atoms absorbing it jiggle harder. Energy is always being passed back and forth between the walls and the light inside, just as it’s passed between molecules in a gas. Leave the oven long enough, and it settles down: the walls and the light share the heat, and by the rule, every separate way the light can carry energy should get its equal share.

What are the light’s separate ways of carrying energy? Waves. The light inside an oven is a mixture of waves of every wavelength, bouncing back and forth between the walls, and each different wave that can live in the oven is a separate way for the light to hold energy. So, by the rule, each kind of wave should get the same share of the heat.

How many kinds of wave are there? Only waves that fit neatly between the walls can keep going in a closed oven. (A guitar string can play only certain notes for a related reason, and that idea comes back, with great consequences, in the eighth journey.) So you can count them, at least in principle. Long waves can fit into an oven only a few ways. Shorter waves can fit far more: halve the wavelength, and twice as many fit across the oven, twice as many from top to bottom and twice as many from front to back, so eight times as many altogether. Halve it again, and there are sixty-four times as many. There is no end to it. The shorter the waves, the more of them there are, without any limit at all.

For the mathematically inclinedCounting the waves in an oven

Now put the two together, as Albert did on his napkin. Every kind of wave gets an equal share of the heat. There are more and more kinds of wave the shorter they are, without end. So nearly all the heat must go into the very shortest waves: into the ultraviolet, then X-rays, then shorter still, without limit.

Follow that through, and it is a disaster. A hot oven would pour all its heat into ever shorter waves and never finish, so it could never get hot at all. Open the fryer, and you’d be blasted not with warmth but with ultraviolet light and X-rays. Every coal fire, every candle, every warm doughnut, and John and Cal themselves, would be blazing with them. In 1900 the British physicist Lord Rayleigh applied the sharing rule to the light in an oven, and in 1905 he and James Jeans worked out exactly what it predicted: a glow that climbs higher and higher towards the short waves, for ever, and holds unlimited energy. The prediction fitted the measurements beautifully for long waves, in the far infrared, and went wildly wrong everywhere else. In 1911 Paul Ehrenfest gave the trouble the name it still has: the ultraviolet catastrophe.

“So the rule’s wrong,” said Cal.

“Or the counting is,” said John.

“The counting is only geometry,” said Albert. “How many waves fit in a box. It is the same counting that tells you how many notes an organ pipe can play. It is not wrong.”

“Then maybe the walls can’t make the short waves.”

“Make them hot enough and they make plenty,” said Albert. “Rigel’s glow is mostly ultraviolet. And the rule does not care how the waves are made. Leave an oven long enough and the sharing happens.”

There was no way out. The counting was solid, and so was the logic. The only thing left was the rule itself: something about the sharing of heat went wrong for short waves, and nobody in 1900 knew what.

Albert’s till

The measurements, at least, were excellent. In Berlin, at the national institute for physics and standards, the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt, physicists had built ovens with small holes in them, exactly the kind of oven Kirchhoff had imagined, and heated them electrically to as much as 1,600 °C. Through the holes they measured the glow, wavelength by wavelength, from the visible far into the infrared, more carefully than anyone ever had. Otto Lummer and Ernst Pringsheim measured the shorter waves; Heinrich Rubens and Ferdinand Kurlbaum the longest.

Max Planck was a professor of physics in Berlin, a careful, conservative man who had spent years on the theory of heat. On Sunday 7 October 1900, the Rubenses came to lunch, and Rubens told Planck about his newest measurements in the far infrared. That evening, by the usual account, Planck found a formula that fitted them, and on 19 October he presented it to the German Physical Society. It fitted every measurement, at every wavelength and every temperature. Nobody knew why.

Planck spent the next two months finding out. The only way he could make his formula come out of the physics of heat was by assuming something he found deeply unnatural: that energy could be handed to light, or taken from it, only in whole lumps, and that the size of a lump depended on the light’s frequency (the number of its waves passing each second): the higher the frequency, the bigger the lump. On 14 December 1900 he presented the derivation to the German Physical Society. Years later he wrote, in a letter, that the whole thing had been an act of desperation: he had to find an explanation, whatever it cost.

“But why do lumps help?” said John.

Albert pointed at a small card on the till, which neither of them had ever noticed. It had evidently been there for some time.

WHOLE DOUGHNUTS ONLY. NO SLICES. NO CLUBBING TOGETHER

“My till,” he said, “works like Planck’s oven.”

The rule at the till

Think of the jiggling atoms in the oven’s walls as customers in the café, and the energy each one happens to have at a given moment as the money in their pocket. Because the atoms jostle at random, the amounts are random too: most customers have a few coins, some have a little more, and very occasionally one has a large note. The hotter the oven, the more money there is in everybody’s pockets.

The different kinds of wave are the things on sale. Long infrared waves are cheap, like a cup of coffee, which costs one small coin. Red light costs more. Ultraviolet costs a big note, as Albert’s doughnuts do. Each wave’s price is the size of its lump of energy, and the shorter the wave, the higher its frequency and the higher its price.

The old rule, the one Rayleigh and Jeans used, is a café that sells everything by the slice, in any amount at all. Every customer can buy a sliver of anything with whatever’s in their pocket. So the money gets spread over everything on the menu, an equal share for every item. And the menu has more and more items the dearer they get, without end. So nearly all the money goes on the dearest end of the menu, a crumb at a time: the ultraviolet catastrophe.

Albert’s till sells only whole things, and each customer pays alone, from their own pocket. A crowd of customers with a few coins each buys a great deal of coffee and no doughnuts at all, because no single one of them has a big note, and they aren’t allowed to club together. Now and then someone turns out to have a large note in their pocket, and buys a doughnut, but the bigger the note, the rarer that is, and it gets rarer very fast. So the short waves, which cost big notes, almost never get bought. They’re starved. The shop’s takings pile up in the middle of the menu, where the prices are high enough to add up and low enough that people can still afford them. That pile is the peak of the glow.

Now turn up the heat. Everyone’s pockets fill up. Dearer things start selling, and the pile of takings moves up the menu: the peak of the glow moves to shorter waves, from infrared towards red, orange, white and blue. Double the temperature, and the peak moves to half the wavelength. That was known before Planck, from Wilhelm Wien’s work in 1893, and Planck’s lumps explain it.

Like every analogy, the till has its limits. Atoms don’t carry pockets, energy flows back from the light to the walls as well as into it, and nobody hands over change. But it keeps the two things that matter. Energy goes into each wave in whole lumps, never in slivers. And each lump has to be paid in one go, by one atom’s jiggling, from what it happens to have at that moment.

The same rule answers Kelvin’s own worry about gases. A molecule’s way of moving whose lumps cost more than the heat can afford never gets its share, so the gas takes less heat to warm up than the old rule said. Both of the sharing rule’s troubles had the same cure.

Quantum

A lump of energy: the smallest amount of energy that can be given to, or taken from, something that vibrates at a given frequency, such as a wave of light. (The plural is quanta.) Its size is in proportion to the frequency: double the frequency and the lump doubles. For everyday things the lumps are far too small to notice, which is why energy seems to flow smoothly. The idea was Max Planck’s, in 1900, and it gave quantum physics its name.

How big are the lumps? Planck’s formula needed one new number to say, and the measurements fixed it. It is now one of the most fundamental numbers in physics.

Planck’s constant

The number that sets the size of nature’s lumps of energy: a lump for something vibrating at a given frequency is Planck’s constant times that frequency. It is extremely small, which is why the lumps went unnoticed for so long: a lump of red light is about a third of a billionth of a billionth of a joule (lifting an apple one metre takes about one joule). Planck worked out its value from the Berlin measurements in 1900. Since 2019 it has been fixed exactly, and used to define the kilogram.

So a lump of red light is tiny, and yet it is still enormous compared with the jiggle of an atom at room temperature: about seventy-five times as much as an atom’s typical share of the heat. That is why nothing at room temperature ever glows red. A wall at room temperature is a crowd of customers with a few coins each, and a red lump costs a note none of them will ever have. In lava, at 1,150 °C, the typical share is nearly five times bigger. A lump of green light still costs twenty times that, but now, in a crowd of trillions of atoms, a few customers are lucky enough, and the lava glows.

Lumps make it inescapable

The argument comes down to this. The colour of a glow follows its temperature: dull red, then orange, then white, then blue, the same for everything that glows. For a glow to have a colour at all, and a peak, short waves must be harder to make than long ones, and harder the shorter they are. If heat could be handed to light in any amount, however small, nothing would make short waves hard: every kind of wave would get its equal share, the countless short waves would take nearly all of it, and everything warm would blaze violet and beyond. They don’t. So energy can’t be handed to light in any amount. It goes in lumps, bigger for shorter waves, and the jostling of heat can rarely afford the big ones.

The figure below shows it happening in an oven as hot as the island’s lava. Its three strips share the same scale of wavelengths, from the ultraviolet on the left to the far infrared on the right, with the visible band shaded through all three. The top strip counts how many kinds of wave fit in the oven: more and more the shorter they are, eight times as many each time the wavelength halves, so many that it’s drawn on a scale where each line up is a thousand times the one below. The middle strip shows each kind of wave’s share of the heat. The bottom strip multiplies the two: how many waves there are, times how much each one gets, is the glow. Choose “Share it out”, the old rule, and every wave gets an equal share: the glow follows the number of waves, and shoots off the top of the sheet in the infrared and keeps climbing. Choose “Sell it in lumps”, Planck’s rule, and the short waves are starved: the glow turns over, with its peak in the infrared and a little of it in the visible, the oven’s orange glow. Then slide the size of the lumps between the two, and watch what happens as they shrink.

As the lumps shrink, the short waves become affordable, the peak slides towards the ultraviolet, and the glow grows: with lumps half the size of Planck’s, the oven’s light would hold eight times as much energy; a tenth the size, a thousand times. With no lumps at all, there’s no limit. Only lumps of exactly Planck’s size give the glow ovens, lava and stars actually have.

Planck himself, as it happens, wasn’t trying to rescue physics from the ultraviolet catastrophe, which hadn’t yet been spelled out in full or named. He was trying to explain a set of measurements, and the lumps were the price of explaining them. Only afterwards did it become clear that the lumps were the only way out of the catastrophe as well.

By evening the coaches had gone, and Albert had turned off the gas under the fryer. The oil would take hours to cool. “So Planck solved it,” said John.
