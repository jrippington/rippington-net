The Van and the wall

Cal was already in the car park when John came out with the coffee, sitting in The Van’s driving seat with the door open and the dashboard screen lit.

He had gone home the night before with two questions nobody had answered. The atom survives, they had found, because its electron is a wave held in by the nucleus, and a wave held in can only take certain shapes. But what holds the nucleus itself together, a huddle of positive charges that ought to fly apart? And how does the Sun manage to shine? The only reply, from Albert as they paid at the till, had been a single sentence, and it had kept him awake: a wave can’t stop dead at a wall.

“I’ve been thinking about walls,” he said.

The Van was parked where it always was, nose to the low wall that ran along the edge of the car park. It was an old wall, built without mortar from blocks of the plateau’s dark lava, about knee high and a foot thick, and beyond it the old lava stretched away towards the mountain.

Cal had the simulator running. On the screen was a drawing of the car park, the wall, and The Van, parked against it, just as it was outside the windscreen. He reached for the switch for the simulator’s quantum mode, on the dashboard since the seventh journey. It turns Planck’s constant up, the number setting the size of the quantum world’s lumps, until a camper van behaves like an electron.

PLANCK’S CONSTANT: INCREASED. PLEASE DRIVE CAREFULLY

On the screen, The Van crept forward and nudged the wall. It bounced back a little and stopped, as a van would. Cal let it creep forward again. Nudge, and back.

“If it’s going to hit the wall,” said John, “I’d rather it did it in the simulator.”

The third time, there was no nudge. One moment The Van on the screen was on the near side of the wall; the next it was on the far side, out on the lava, facing the mountain. The wall stood exactly as it had, every block in place.

VEHICLE LOCATION: OTHER SIDE OF WALL

John looked out through the windscreen at the real wall, and the real lava beyond it, and then at the screen.

“It didn’t climb over,” he said. “And it didn’t knock the wall down.”

“It went through,” said Cal.

“Then could this one?” John patted the dashboard. “The real one?”

Cal turned Planck’s constant back down to its real size, put The Van against the wall on the screen again, and asked the simulator for the chance of the real Van doing what the simulator’s Van had just done. It thought for longer than usual.

Over the hill, or back

Start with the ball, because the ball is what everyday life has taught us to expect.

At the entrance to the car park there’s a hump in the tarmac, put there to slow the coaches. Roll a ball at it gently, and the ball rolls up the slope, slowing as it climbs, stops, and rolls back down the way it came. Roll it harder, and it climbs higher before it stops. Roll it hard enough, and it gets to the top with a little speed to spare and rolls down the far side.

What decides which happens is energy. A rolling ball has energy because it’s moving, and to climb it has to turn some of that into the energy of being higher up. If it has less energy than it would take to reach the top, it runs out on the way up, stops, and comes back. There’s nothing chancy about it. Roll the same ball at the same speed a thousand times and it comes back a thousand times. Energy is never created or destroyed, as The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe put it, only passed on or converted, and a ball can’t spend energy it hasn’t got.

Physicists use the same picture for anything that stands in a particle’s way, whether it’s a hill, a wall or something stranger. Draw the obstacle as a hill whose height is the energy it would take to get past, and draw the particle’s own energy as a level line. Wherever the line is below the hill, the particle can’t go: it would need to be moving with less than no energy of motion at all. So by ordinary physics there are only two possible endings. A particle with less energy than the top always comes back, and a particle with more always gets over.

The Van at the car park wall is the same story, only cruder. Driven gently into the wall, it doesn’t have the energy to climb it, let alone to knock it down. It stops, and if anything it bounces back. Nobody has ever parked a van against a wall and found it on the other side the next morning.

But an electron isn’t a ball.

Why a wave can’t stop dead

In the fourth journey, John and Cal found that a single electron goes through two slits as a wave, and in the fifth, that the chance of finding it at any place is set by the size of its wave there: a big wave, a big chance; no wave, no chance. So the question for an electron at a wall isn’t whether it can climb. It’s what its wave does at the edge of a place where it hasn’t the energy to be.

Inside the wall, the electron’s energy is below the top, so it would have less than no energy of motion. Its wave can’t wave there: waving, with a wavelength, is what motion looks like in a wave. So you might expect the wave simply to stop at the wall’s face, cut off as cleanly as the ball turned back.

It can’t. A wave that stopped dead would have a sharp corner at the wall’s face: full height on one side and nothing at all on the other, with no distance between. And the seventh journey found out what it takes to squeeze a wave into a small space. You have to build it out of many waves of different wavelengths, and the smaller the space, the shorter the wavelengths you need; and for an electron, a shorter wavelength means faster motion and more energy. A corner with no width at all is a squeeze into no space at all. It would need waves of every wavelength down to nothing, and so an electron with unlimited energy. This electron has a perfectly ordinary amount.

So the wave has to round off. It carries on into the wall, not waving, but getting smaller and smaller with every step it goes in, fading instead of stopping. That’s the sentence Cal had been turning over all night. A wave can’t stop dead at a wall, because stopping dead would cost more energy than it has.

How fast does it fade? Fast, and in a particular way. Every extra step into the wall shrinks what’s left by the same fraction. It’s like money in a bank that charges interest instead of paying it: each year takes the same share of what’s left, so the sum doesn’t dwindle steadily to nothing but halves and halves and halves again. Suppose a tenth of a nanometre of wall halves the electron’s chance of being found beyond it. (A nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre; a tenth of one is about the width of the smallest atom, hydrogen.) Then two tenths leave a quarter, three tenths an eighth, and ten tenths, a whole nanometre, leave about a thousandth. Doubling the wall’s thickness doesn’t halve the chance. It squares it: a chance of one in ten becomes one in a hundred.

Two things set how fast the wave fades. One is how far short of the top the particle’s energy is: the further short, the faster. The other is the particle’s mass: the heavier it is, the faster its wave dies away inside the wall. A proton, about 1,800 times heavier than an electron, fades about forty times faster in the same wall. Remember The Van.

Now the last step. If the wall is thin enough that the wave hasn’t faded to nothing by the time it reaches the far side, some of it is still there when it comes out. And on the far side, where the electron has energy to move again, the wave can wave again. It carries on, with the same wavelength as before, only smaller. Since the size of the wave sets the chance of finding the electron, there’s a chance of finding it beyond the wall. Not on top of it, and not through a hole in it: on the far side of a wall it hasn’t the energy to climb.

Is there a way out? You’d have to say that the wave really does stop dead at the wall’s face, and that costs unlimited energy. Or you’d have to say that the wave’s size doesn’t set the chance of finding the particle, and that’s the rule the stripes of the fourth journey were built by, dot after dot. Neither way out is open. Thin walls leak.

Quantum tunnelling

The way a particle can turn up on the far side of a barrier it hasn’t the energy to climb. The particle is a wave, and a wave can’t stop dead at a barrier’s edge: it fades inside it, and if the barrier is thin enough, some of the wave is left on the far side, which means a chance of finding the particle there. The chance falls off very steeply as the barrier gets thicker or higher, or the particle heavier. It is common for electrons crossing gaps a few atoms wide, and unheard of for anything big.

The light in a glass of water

There’s a cousin of tunnelling you can see for yourself, with nothing more than a glass of water.

Light going from glass or water into air bends as it crosses the surface, away from the straight-on direction. The more slanted it comes, the more it bends, until, at a slant of about forty degrees from straight on for glass, it would have to bend so far that it would skim along the surface. Slant it any more, and there’s no direction left in the air for it to go, so none of it gets out: all of it is turned back into the glass, as if the surface were a perfect mirror. That’s why the underside of the water in a swimming pool or a fish tank looks silver from below.

But a wave can’t stop dead at an edge, and light is a wave. At the surface it doesn’t simply vanish: it reaches a little way out into the air, fading as it goes, over about a ten-thousandth of a millimetre, a fraction of its own wavelength. Nothing usually comes within that distance, so nothing happens, and the mirror is perfect. Bring another piece of glass close enough, though, and the fading light reaches it before it has died away, and carries on into it as light. The mirror leaks, across a gap of air it supposedly can’t cross. Isaac Newton noticed it more than three hundred years ago, with light getting from one glass prism into another across a very thin gap.

Try it

Fill a clear, straight-sided glass with water and hold it in your hand, with your fingertips pressed firmly against its side. Now look down into the water from above, at the side of the glass where your fingers are, at a steep slant, so that you’re looking almost along the glass wall. The wall looks silvery, like a mirror. And on it, where your fingertips press, you’ll see the ridges of your fingerprints, dark, sharp and astonishingly clear.

The mirror is the light inside the glass, meeting the outer surface too slanted to get out. In the valleys of your skin, there’s air between your finger and the glass, a gap hundreds of times wider than the light’s tiny reach, so the mirror holds and the valleys look silver. Where a ridge of skin is pressed against the glass, or comes within that ten-thousandth of a millimetre of it, the light gets out into your finger instead of coming back, and there the mirror goes dark. (Most of the dark, in fact, is where damp skin is pressed right against the glass and spoils the mirror outright. The leak across gaps too narrow for the light’s reach to miss is light’s own tunnelling.) Some fingerprint scanners work in exactly this way, with a glass prism lit from inside.

An electron at a wall

The electron’s wall can be just as thin. Electrons in a piece of metal are held in by the metal: to leave it, an electron needs a few electronvolts of energy, an electronvolt being the tiny unit of energy that suits single particles. Put two pieces of metal a nanometre apart, with nothing between them but a vacuum or a thin layer of insulating oxide, and the gap is a wall exactly like the hill in the car park: a barrier of a certain height, a nanometre thick, that the electrons haven’t the energy to climb.

The figure below sends an electron at such a wall, again and again. It’s drawn as physicists draw these things, on an energy diagram. The wall stands as high as the energy it would take to climb it, and as thick as it is, and the dashed line is the electron’s own energy. The electron comes in from the left as a wave, drawn as a line swinging up and down along its energy line, inside a fainter outline that shows the wave’s size. The wave is the real thing, worked out step by step by the rules of quantum mechanics.

Play it and watch the wave arrive at the wall. Most of it is turned back, and for a moment the incoming wave and the reflected one overlap and make ripples. Inside the wall you can see it fade. And on the far side, a smaller wave carries on. At the end of each run the electron is found, once, as a single particle, either back on the left or through on the right, with the chance the wave gives; the table keeps count. Notice that the wave on the far side looks bigger than its chance: a wave half as tall as the one that arrived means a chance only a quarter as big, because the chance goes with the wave’s size multiplied by itself. Then make the wall thicker, a little at a time, and watch the wave on the far side shrink much faster than the wall grows, until at a nanometre and a half there’s almost nothing left to see. Give the electron more energy and it fades more slowly. Give it more than the wall’s height, and something else strange happens: part of the wave is still turned back, though a ball would have sailed over. Finally, switch to the ball, which does what everyday life says it should, and comes back every time.

The Van’s odds

So what was the number too large for The Van’s display?

The Van is heavy, about two and a half tonnes, and the wall is a foot thick, about thirty centimetres. Give The Van every advantage: suppose it were short of the energy it needed to get over the wall by only a single joule, about what it takes to lift an apple a metre. Then The Van’s wave would fade inside the wall at a rate set by its two and a half tonnes, and by the end of thirty centimetres it would have faded by a factor almost impossible to write down.

The chance comes out as a decimal point followed by a string of noughts, and then, at last, some other figure. The number of noughts is nearly two followed by thirty-five noughts of its own. Written out, one nought to a millimetre, the string would reach about four hundred thousand times further than the edge of the observable universe, which is a long way to go before the first digit that isn’t nought. The display font had a point.

That’s how far the real world is from the simulator’s quantum mode. To give The Van a decent chance, about one in three, of turning up on the other side of the wall, the simulator had to turn Planck’s constant up about four hundred million billion billion billion times. (That’s a few hundred times less than for the drive at seventeen miles an hour in the seventh journey. At that setting The Van would have gone through the wall nearly every time, so Cal had turned it down for the wall.) Then, and only then, does a camper van behave like an electron at a wall. In the real world, Planck’s constant is what it is, The Van weighs what it does, and it will be parked on this side of the wall every morning for the rest of time.

“So nothing big ever tunnels,” said John.

“Nothing big,” said Cal. “Albert’s doughnuts aren’t going to come through the glass of the display case.”

“If they could,” said Albert, “I would have noticed by now.”

A crater with a rim

For an electron at a gap a few atoms wide, the chances are good. That alone would make tunnelling a curiosity of the laboratory. What made it famous, in 1928, was that it answered a question about radioactivity nobody else could.

In the first journey, Rutherford’s laboratory fired alpha particles at gold leaf, and they came from a speck of radium in a lead box. Radium, like uranium and a few dozen other kinds of atom, is radioactive: every so often, one of its nuclei throws out an alpha particle, at about a twentieth of the speed of light, and becomes the nucleus of a different element. An alpha particle, as Rutherford had shown, is a helium nucleus: two protons and two neutrons stuck together. (Neutrons are particles about as heavy as protons, with no electric charge, which live with the protons in every nucleus but hydrogen’s.)

Where did the alpha particle come from, and why did it leave when it did?

A nucleus is a tiny huddle of protons and neutrons, and the protons, all positive, push each other apart, as fiercely as anything does at such distances. Something stronger holds them together: a pull that acts only when particles are almost touching, and fades to nothing a little further off. (What that pull is, the last journey of this series comes back to.) So an alpha particle inside a nucleus is like a ball in a crater. Inside, the short-range pull holds it in. But once it’s out past the rim, only the electric push is left, and that flings it away, faster and faster, until it leaves at the speed Rutherford’s alpha particles had.

Here was the puzzle. The rim of the crater is the top of the electric push, just outside the nucleus, and it’s high. In 1927 Rutherford fired alpha particles with about twice the energy of uranium’s own at uranium nuclei, and they bounced off exactly as an electric push alone would turn them: none got close enough to feel anything else. So the rim is at least twice as high as the energy uranium’s alpha particles come out with, and today’s measurements of the nucleus’s size put it six or seven times as high. How can an alpha particle get out of a crater whose rim is far higher than its energy? If it had climbed out over the rim, it would roll down the far side and leave with at least the rim’s energy. It leaves with much less.

In the summer of 1928, a young physicist from Leningrad visiting Göttingen, George Gamow, aged twenty-four, worked out the answer, and at Princeton Ronald Gurney and Edward Condon found it at the same time: the alpha particle doesn’t climb out. Its wave fades through the crater’s wall and leaks out partway down the far side, where the electric push takes over. It’s tunnelling, and it explained at once a pattern that had been sitting in the measurements for years.

“So the alpha particle is a wave sloshing about in the crater,” said Cal.

“Knocking on the wall,” said Albert, “about a billion trillion times a second. And each time, with a very small chance, it gets out.”

“And there’s no telling which knock?”

“None,” said Albert. “It is pure chance, every time.” He looked out of the window at the mountain. “I have told you before. I have never liked games of chance.”

This is why radioactivity runs by chance and an old nucleus is no more likely to go off than a young one. Every knock is a fresh try with the same small chance, so a nucleus that has been knocking for a billion years has exactly the same chance of getting out in the next second as one that has just been made. Take a great many such nuclei, and in any given time the same share of them goes off. That gives every radioactive kind of atom a time of its own.

Half-life

The time it takes for half the atoms in a sample of a radioactive element to decay. Each nucleus has the same small chance of decaying in every second, however long it has existed, so after one half-life half the atoms are left, after two a quarter, after three an eighth, and so on. Nobody can say when any one atom will go; the half-life says how many will, on average. Uranium-238’s half-life is about 4.5 billion years, roughly the age of the Earth; radium-226’s is 1,600 years.

Half-lives come in an astonishing range. Polonium-212 throws out its alpha particles with a little over twice the energy of thorium-232’s. Its half-life is a third of a millionth of a second. Thorium-232’s is 14 billion years, longer than the universe has existed. A little over twice the energy, and a million billion billion times as long a wait.

Only tunnelling makes sense of that. A faster alpha particle comes out further up the crater’s outer slope, where the wall it has to fade through is thinner, and with the chance falling off so steeply with thickness, a slightly thinner wall makes an enormous difference. Gamow’s simple picture, a wave knocking on a wall, gets the half-lives of polonium, radium, uranium and thorium right within a factor of five, across twenty-four powers of ten. And it explains Rutherford’s speck of radium: with a half-life of 1,600 years, a speck of radium gives out a steady stream of alpha particles for as long as anyone cares to watch.

Why the Sun can shine

Cal’s other question was the Sun.

The Sun shines by fusing hydrogen into helium. Deep in its core, hydrogen’s nuclei, single protons, join together, and through a few steps four of them become one helium nucleus. The helium weighs a little less than the four protons it was made from, about seven tenths of one per cent less, and the difference comes out as energy, by E = mc². The Sun turns about four million tonnes of itself into sunlight every second, as John and Cal found in The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe.

Nuclear fusion

Joining light nuclei together into a heavier one. Every nucleus carries positive charge, so two nuclei push each other apart, and they must get very close, within a few millionths of a billionth of a metre, before a far stronger, short-range pull takes over and holds them together. When light nuclei fuse, the new nucleus weighs a little less than its parts, and the difference comes out as energy. The Sun shines by fusing hydrogen into helium, which turns about seven tenths of one per cent of the hydrogen’s mass into energy.

To fuse, two protons have to touch, or near enough: get within about two millionths of a billionth of a metre of each other, two femtometres, where the short-range pull can take hold. And two protons are both positive, so they push each other apart, harder and harder the closer they get. Between them, as between the alpha particle and the outside world, stands an electric hill.

The core of the Sun is about fifteen million degrees, and it’s squeezed by the weight of the whole Sun above it to about 150 times the density of water. Its protons rush about at hundreds of kilometres a second, crashing into one another all the time. Surely, at fifteen million degrees, they hit each other hard enough?

Not nearly. For a typical pair of protons in the core, coming at each other head on, the top of the hill is about 370 times higher than their energy. Such a pair climbs as far as its energy allows, stops, and turns back while the two are still about 740 femtometres apart, hundreds of times too far away to touch. Some pairs are faster than the typical one, of course; in a hot gas there’s always a lucky few. But to have even the hill’s energy, a pair would need to be so outrageously lucky that, by ordinary physics, it would happen to one head-on approach in a number with 239 noughts. The Sun has a great many protons, and they crash into each other unimaginably often, but not that often: by ordinary physics, at fifteen million degrees, no two protons in the whole Sun would have touched in the whole history of the universe. For an ordinary pair to get over the top, the core would have to be hundreds of times hotter than it is, billions of degrees.

And yet the Sun shines. With tunnelling, a pair doesn’t have to get over the hill. It has to get through it, from where it turns back to where the short-range pull takes over, and its wave fades through the hill on the way. For a typical pair, the hill is far too thick. But a pair with a few times the typical energy, still far below the top, turns back closer in, where the hill is thinner, and the fading wave has a real chance of getting through. At fifteen million degrees, about one head-on approach in thirteen thousand ends with the two protons touching. For ordinary physics to let as many through, the core would have to be about fifty times hotter than it is, around 700 million degrees.

The figure below is the hill between two protons, drawn to scale in distance, with their energy going up the page. The hill rises off the top of the sheet: at two femtometres, where they’d touch, it’s forty times higher than the sheet. The thin amber line at the far left is touching. The pairs coming in from the right are pairs from the core, with the energies its heat gives them, each climbing as far as it can and going back. Play it, and the table counts every approach, two thousand a second (it draws only a few). Wait, and now and then one, nearly always one of the faster pairs, gets through the hill and touches: watch the far left for an amber flash. Switch to ordinary physics, and nothing ever gets through. Then try the core’s temperature: cooler, and the touches become far rarer; hotter, and far commoner. The last line of the table says how hot the core would have to be for ordinary physics to manage as many.

So the Sun shines because protons tunnel. So does every star you’ve ever seen. Without tunnelling there’d be no starlight, no sunlight, and nobody on the plateau to sit in it.

Even with tunnelling, the Sun burns its hydrogen remarkably slowly, and that slowness is a different story. Once two protons touch, one of them has to turn into a neutron in the brief moment they’re together, and that almost never happens: it’s the work of a force called the weak force, and a later series comes back to it. It’s why a proton in the Sun’s core waits, on average, billions of years before it fuses, and why the Sun will go on shining for billions of years more.

Feeling atoms one at a time

The first journey made a promise. Light can never show you a single atom, because its waves are thousands of times too long, but there are microscopes that make pictures of single atoms by feeling their way across a surface. They feel with tunnelling.

A scanning tunnelling microscope has a needle of metal, sharpened until, with luck, a single atom sits at its point. It’s brought down towards a surface until the gap between them is less than a nanometre, and a small voltage is put across the gap. Electrons can’t jump the gap by ordinary physics; they haven’t the energy. But they tunnel across it, and a tiny current flows, a few billionths of an amp.

The current is the clever part. Because the chance of tunnelling falls off so steeply with the gap, the current changes enormously with the needle’s height: for every tenth of a nanometre further from the surface, less than the width of most atoms, it falls to about a tenth. So the needle can feel a bump on the surface a fraction of an atom high. As it’s moved slowly across the surface, line by line, a control circuit keeps nudging it up and down to keep the current steady, and the record of those nudges is a map of the surface’s hills and valleys, atom by atom.

Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer built the first one at IBM’s laboratory near Zurich in 1981, and shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for it in 1986 with Ernst Ruska, who had built the first electron microscope. Within a few years, the needles weren’t only feeling atoms but moving them, pushing them one by one across a cold surface into place. At the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, a machine arranged cobalt atoms into an oval ring on a sheet of copper, and the microscope’s picture of it shows each atom as a small bump. Inside the ring, the copper’s surface is covered in ripples. They’re the waves of the copper’s electrons, held in by the ring, playing the notes a held-in wave can play, exactly as the eighth journey’s atom does.

“So those really are atoms,” said John.

“Drawn by electrons that went where they had no business going,” said Cal.

The memory in your pocket

There’s very probably a tunnelling machine in your pocket.

The memory in a phone, a memory stick or a camera’s card keeps its data when the power is off, for years, and it does it with walls. Each of its tiny cells has a little island where electrons can be kept, completely surrounded by a layer of glass-like insulating oxide, about ten nanometres thick in the first designs and a little thinner since. Electrons put on the island are trapped there. Whether a cell’s island is holding electrons or not is the difference between a one and a nought, and billions of such cells hold a photograph.

Why don’t the trapped electrons tunnel out? Because ten nanometres is, for an electron, a very thick wall. With the chance falling off as steeply as it does, the electrons on the island would have to knock on the wall for longer than anyone will ever keep a phone. So they stay, power or no power.

To write a new photograph, though, the electrons have to cross that same wall on purpose, and they do it by tunnelling. A strong voltage across the wall tilts it: instead of a flat-topped barrier, the top slopes down steeply towards the far side. An electron at the bottom now only has to tunnel through the thin part of the wall below where the slope crosses its energy level, and with the chance so sensitive to thickness, electrons pour through. Take the voltage away and the wall is thick again, and they’re trapped. Ralph Fowler and Lothar Nordheim worked out the rule for electrons pulled out of a metal in this way in 1928, the same year as Gamow’s alpha particles, and Fujio Masuoka’s team at Toshiba built the kind of memory that uses it, flash memory, in the 1980s.

So every time a phone saves a picture, electrons are being driven through a wall they haven’t the energy to climb, by the same rule that empties a nucleus and lights the Sun. And every time it keeps one, it’s relying on that same rule, the other way round.

Back to Teide

By the evening the coaches had gone, and the sun was low over the western ridge, throwing long shadows across the plateau. The light came in sideways through the café’s windows and glared off the varnished tops of the tables. In the display case the last of the day’s doughnuts sat, entirely on the right side of the glass.

John stood at the window. A hundred and fifty million kilometres away, in the core of that low sun, protons were crashing into each other in their trillions of trillions, and nearly all of them were bouncing off. The few that tunnelled were enough. Every bit of warmth in the plateau’s old rock had come from them.

Out in the car park, The Van sat against its wall, on the near side, where it would stay. On the dashboard, the simulator’s Van was still out on the lava, facing the mountain.

“You’ve left it in quantum mode,” said John.

“I’ll fix it in the morning,” said Cal. He put on his sunglasses against the glare from the table. Then he tilted his head to one side, and frowned, and tilted it back again.

“That’s odd,” he said. “When I tilt my head, the glare comes back.”

Albert, wiping down the counter, didn’t look up.

“Come back in the morning,” he said. “And bring another pair of sunglasses.”
