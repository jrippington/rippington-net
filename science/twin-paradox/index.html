Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 6 of 14

The apparently perfect paradox

“There’s a contradiction,” said Cal. He had been thinking about Albert’s twins all morning.

They were sitting in The Van in the café car park, with its doors open, because John wanted to look at the speedometer while he thought. He was trying to revive it by tapping the glass with a teaspoon. It had been reading 17 miles an hour since breakfast, and The Van had been parked since breakfast.

“You’ll need to narrow that down,” said John.

“The light clock,” said Cal. “At The Station. We agreed that when two things move steadily past each other, each of them measures the other’s clock running slow.”

“Yes.”

“So suppose we go somewhere, fast, and come back. All the way out and all the way back, we’re moving relative to Albert, so by our reckoning his clock runs slow. And by his reckoning, ours does. When we get back, we’ll be standing next to each other with our clocks side by side.” Cal put his coffee down. “They can’t both be behind.”

John put down the spoon. “Ah.”

It was, John had to admit, a very good contradiction. In the third journey, The Station measured The Van’s clock running slow, and The Van, with equal right, measured The Station’s running slow. That was fine while The Van and The Station were rushing apart, because, as the fourth journey found, comparing clocks in different places depends on whose clocks you trust to be in step, and the two frames disagree about that. Each side’s account was consistent, and neither could be proved wrong, because the two clocks were never side by side again.

But bring them back together, and there’s no more room for argument. Two clocks side by side, at the same place at the same moment, can be read by anyone, and everyone will read the same numbers. If one of them is behind, everyone will agree which.

Albert emerged from the café with two coffees and a small card, the size of a railway ticket. On one side it said:

RETURN TICKET

He turned it over. On the back, in his tidy capitals, he had written:

DATE NOT GUARANTEED

“An excellent paradox,” he said, putting it on the table between them. “It will disappear when you draw the journeys.”

Cal turned the ticket over and back again. “Why do you keep these things?”

“Because,” said Albert, “most people leave the return journey out of their arguments.”

Twins

Cal’s puzzle is old, famous and usually told about twins.

The twin paradox

The puzzle of two twins, one of whom stays at home while the other makes a fast round trip. Each twin, while they are moving apart or together at a steady speed, measures the other’s clock running slow; so which of them is younger when they meet again? Relativity’s answer is definite: the twin who travelled is younger. It is called a paradox because the situation looks symmetrical, but it isn’t: only one of the twins turns round.

The idea is nearly as old as relativity. Einstein noticed it in his paper of 1905: two clocks set going together, one of which is taken on a journey round a closed path and brought back, will disagree when they meet again, the travelling clock behind. He called it a peculiar consequence of the theory. In 1911 the French physicist Paul Langevin made it vivid at a philosophy congress in Bologna, with a traveller fired off in a projectile at very nearly the speed of light, who spends one year of their own time going out and one year coming back, and steps out two years older to find that two centuries have passed on Earth. The German physicist Max von Laue took it up the same year, and by 1913 he had shown, with the geometry of spacetime, where its answer lies. The twins arrived in 1918, in a book by the mathematician Hermann Weyl, and they have been arguing ever since.

Two routes between the same events

A ten-year expedition would make a splendid demonstration and a dreadful commitment, so John and Cal used The Van’s simulator, which had been behaving itself since Cal hit it.

Setting up the simulation

The simulator needed a home, and John gave it an idealised one: a station floating in deep space, far from any planet, standing in for the café, with a clock on it. They called it Albert’s clock. The home station never fires an engine and never turns. It is an inertial frame, from start to finish.

For the trip, John chose a destination four light-years away from home, in the home station’s frame, and a speed of eight-tenths of the speed of light. To keep the comparison as clean as possible, he had the simulated Van pass the home station already moving at full speed, so that at the moment it flashed past, The Van’s clock and Albert’s clock were side by side for an instant and could be set to agree: both read zero. The Van coasted out to the destination. There it turned round, and the simulator, to begin with, made the turn instantaneous: in one moment The Van was going out at eight-tenths of the speed of light, and in the next it was coming back at the same speed. (No real van could survive that, and we’ll round off the corner later.) Then The Van coasted home and flashed past the home station again, and at that moment the two clocks were side by side once more and could be compared.

Two events, then, that both clocks attend: The Van passing home on the way out (call it the departure), and The Van passing home on the way back (the reunion). Albert’s clock gets from one to the other by staying put. The Van’s clock gets there by going out four light-years and back.

Home’s account

The home station’s frame is the simplest place to do the sums, because it’s inertial throughout. At eight-tenths of the speed of light, four light-years takes five years. The Van takes five years to get out and five years to get back: Albert’s clock reads ten years at the reunion.

And on the way, The Van’s clock is a moving clock, running slow by the Lorentz factor, which at eight-tenths of the speed of light is 5/3. So in each five-year leg, The Van’s clock advances five years divided by 5/3: three years. At the reunion, The Van’s clock reads six years.

The simulator showed it plainly:

HOME: 10 YEARS. THE VAN: 6 YEARS.

Cal leaned forward. “But if we’re entitled to say the home station is the one moving, why can’t it be younger? On the way out, we see its clock running slow. On the way back, the same. So by our reckoning, Albert’s clock should only have done six years while ours did ten.”

“During one leg, you can say that,” said John. “The journey as a whole is different.”

Who turned round?

The principle of relativity, from the first journey, says that every inertial frame is as good as every other. It doesn’t say that every frame is as good as every other. And there is a simple experiment that tells the home station and The Van apart.

Put an accelerometer in each, like the spring-mounted instrument in The Van’s cupboard: a weight held between two springs, which swings over when the instrument’s motion changes, and sits at zero while it moves steadily. The home station’s accelerometer reads zero for the whole ten years. The Van’s reads zero on the way out, and zero on the way back, but at the turn, when The Van’s velocity changes from out to back, its needle swings hard over. In the simulator’s idealised instant, it swings infinitely hard. Anyone aboard would know about it.

So the two situations are different. The home station sits in one inertial frame the whole time. The Van is in one inertial frame on the way out, and in a different inertial frame on the way back, and the change from one to the other is something its passengers can feel. Cal’s argument, that The Van can treat itself as at rest and the home station as moving, works perfectly well for either leg on its own. What it can’t do is treat the whole trip as if The Van were at rest throughout, in one frame, because there is no single inertial frame in which The Van is at rest throughout.

That tells us that the two clocks can disagree, and which one is special. To see why the traveller’s clock is the one that is behind, and by how much, it helps to think of a clock as measuring something, the way a car’s mileometer does.

Clocks measure the length of a path

Two cars and two roads

Two cars leave the same car park at the bottom of the mountain and drive to the café on the plateau, one by the main road and one by a long, winding back road. Each car has a mileometer, the counter that adds up the distance it has driven. When they meet in the café car park, the two mileometers disagree: the car that took the winding road has done more miles.

Nobody finds this strange, or thinks that the winding road made a mileometer run fast, or that one of them is broken. A mileometer measures the length of the path its car actually drove, and the two cars drove different paths between the same two places, so the mileometers disagree. Asking which of them is right makes no sense: both are, each about its own path.

Clocks and worldlines

A clock is the same kind of thing, in spacetime. It measures its proper time, the time it experiences, along the path it actually takes through spacetime: its worldline, in the language of the fourth journey. Albert’s clock and The Van’s clock both go from the departure event to the reunion event, but by different worldlines. On a spacetime diagram in the home station’s frame, Albert’s worldline goes straight up the page. The Van’s goes up and to the right to the turn, then up and back to the left to the reunion: a V on its side, like a pointed bracket.

Proper time along a worldline

The time a clock measures as it travels along its worldline from one event to another. Different worldlines between the same two events can have different proper times, just as different roads between two towns have different lengths, and when two clocks meet again after taking different worldlines, they can read differently. In spacetime, unlike on a map, the straight worldline between two events, the one that coasts without ever accelerating, has the longest proper time of any path between them. Any detour makes the time shorter.

On a map, the straight road between two places is the shortest. In spacetime, the straight path between two events is the longest, and that answers Cal’s paradox: the traveller’s bent path, out and back, collects less time than the home station’s straight one.

Why straight is longest

Why should a straight line be the longest? Because of the minus sign in spacetime’s geometry, which the fourth journey found.

On a map, if you go 5 kilometres north and 4 kilometres east, Pythagoras says your straight-line distance is the square root of 5 squared plus 4 squared: the squares add, and the slanting path is longer than either side. In spacetime, with time measured in years and distance in light-years so that light goes at 45 degrees, a clock’s proper time along a straight stretch is the square root of the time squared minus the distance squared. The squares subtract.

So take The Van’s outward leg in the home station’s frame: five years up the page, four light-years across. On a map, that slanting line would be the square root of 25 plus 16, about 6.4: longer than the straight-up five. In spacetime it’s the square root of 25 minus 16, which is the square root of 9: three years. The slant makes it shorter. The return leg is the same: another three. Six years for The Van, against ten for the straight path straight up the page. Exactly what the simulator said, from a completely different-looking calculation.

The more a worldline slants, the closer it gets to the 45-degree line of light, and the less proper time it collects. Light itself, at exactly 45 degrees, collects none at all: time squared minus distance squared is zero. And a worldline can’t slant further than light. So among all the paths from departure to reunion, the one that goes straight up, never moving, never accelerating, collects the most time. Any path that goes somewhere and comes back collects less.

Von Laue showed this in 1913, and physicists still think of the twin paradox this way, as a fact about the geometry of the two paths: the two clocks took different routes through spacetime, and the routes have different lengths. Nothing damaged the traveller’s clock or interfered with its works.

Does the turn itself change the clock?

The travelling-twin story is often explained by saying, “One twin accelerates, so the symmetry is broken.” That’s true as far as it goes. The accelerometer is what tells us which twin is which. But it can leave you imagining that the acceleration itself does something to the clock: that in the brief moment of turning, the traveller’s clock is somehow wound back by four years.

Two things show otherwise. The first is in the figure: round the turn, and while The Van slows, stops and speeds up again, its light hardly zigzags at all, and its clock ticks almost as fast as home’s. In numbers, at eight-tenths of the speed of light and four light-years, the turn takes a little over two years of The Van’s time. The final difference is a little under four years, but less than half a year of it builds up during the turn, counting in the home station’s frame. The rest, well over three years, builds up while The Van is coasting steadily, with its accelerometer reading zero. How much time passes depends on the whole route, not on how hard the engine pushes at the corner. Make the turn sharper and sharper, and the corner contributes less and less, while the difference grows towards the sharp turn’s four years.

The second is an experiment you’ve already met. In the third journey, muons were sent round a ring at CERN at 99.94 per cent of the speed of light, out and back, round and round, which is a twin’s journey many times over. To go round in a circle, they had to be accelerated continuously, at more than a million million million g. They lived just as long as their speed alone predicted: the Lorentz factor slowed their clocks, and that colossal acceleration added nothing. Physicists call this the clock hypothesis: an ideal clock’s rate depends only on its speed at each moment, not on its acceleration.

So acceleration is the sign that the traveller changed frames, and the thing that makes the journey a round trip. In the real universe, of course, a turn takes time, force and energy, and a real journey would have to include them. The clean V-shaped graph is a way to see the principle before worrying about the hardware.

What happens to “now” at the turnaround?

That still leaves Cal’s argument, which is uncomfortably convincing. On the way out, The Van measures Albert’s clock running slow. On the way back, it measures Albert’s clock running slow again. How can Albert’s clock end up ahead?

The answer is in the fourth journey: comparing clocks that are far apart depends on which events you count as happening at the same time, and that depends on your frame.

The Van’s bookkeeping

On the way out, coasting at eight-tenths of the speed of light, The Van is in one inertial frame, with its own lines of “now”. Just before the turn, The Van’s clock reads three years. The question “what does Albert’s clock read now, in The Van’s frame?” is answered by following The Van’s line of “now” back across the four light-years to the home station’s worldline. On the spacetime diagram drawn in the home station’s frame, that line is tilted, upwards towards The Van, as the fourth journey found, so it reaches home’s worldline low down: at 1.8 years. So, by The Van’s outward reckoning, when The Van’s clock reads three years, Albert’s reads 1.8. Albert’s clock is running slow, by the same factor of 5/3, exactly as Cal said.

Then The Van turns round. Now it is in a different inertial frame, moving the other way, and its lines of “now” tilt the other way. Just after the turn, still with three years on its own clock, The Van’s new line of “now” reaches home’s worldline high up: at 8.2 years.

So in the instant of the turn, the time that The Van’s frame assigns to Albert’s distant clock jumps from 1.8 years to 8.2: 6.4 years of home’s history that never lay on any of The Van’s lines of “now” while it was coasting. On the way home, The Van again measures Albert’s clock running slow, from 8.2 years to 10, which is 1.8 years of home time while The Van’s clock does three. Add it up, by The Van’s bookkeeping: 1.8 on the way out, 6.4 at the turn, 1.8 on the way back. Ten years. Cal’s argument counted the two coasting legs and left out the turn, and the turn is where most of home’s ten years went.

Does Albert’s clock jump?

Nothing happens to Albert’s clock. It goes on ticking at its steady rate, one year per year, the whole time. Nobody at home notices anything when The Van turns round, four light-years away. No signal travels from The Van to Albert, or back, in the moment of the turn. What jumps is The Van’s assignment of a time to a distant event: which of home’s moments The Van’s frame calls “now”. Change frame, and you change which distant events count as simultaneous with you, and four light-years away the change is large. It’s the Andromeda paradox of the fourth journey again, on a smaller scale, with the walk replaced by a rocket.

What they would actually see

There is something The Van’s crew can actually watch, and it never jumps. Suppose Albert sends a flash of light every year, on his birthday, so to speak. Light from home always travels at the speed of light, so the flashes arrive at The Van at definite moments, which everybody agrees on.

On the way out, The Van is running away from the flashes, so they arrive spread out: the Doppler effect of the second journey. At eight-tenths of the speed of light, The Van receives one of home’s yearly flashes only every three years. By the turn, after three years on The Van’s clock, its crew have seen exactly one birthday, and the home they see through the window is one year older than when they left. On the way back, The Van is rushing towards the flashes, and they arrive three times as often as they were sent: in the three years of the return, the crew see the other nine birthdays, home racing through nine years in the window. One plus nine: ten. Seen through the window, Albert’s clock runs slow on the way out and fast on the way back, steadily, and arrives at exactly the right total.

So there are two different things one might mean by “what Albert’s clock is doing now”: the time The Van’s frame assigns to it, which jumps at the turn, and the time The Van actually sees on it, which doesn’t. Both end at ten years, because at the reunion the two clocks are side by side, where anyone can read them.

Both appear together below, as a scene like the light clocks above: The Van going out and back, with Albert’s birthday flashes travelling out from home at the speed of light. Underneath, two rows of tally marks count The Van’s own years and the birthdays its crew have seen. The bar at the bottom is home’s clock as The Van’s “now” reckons it.

Play it. On the way out, the flashes chase The Van and only one catches it, right at the turn. Coming back, The Van meets the other nine head-on. Meanwhile the bar creeps up to 1.8 years, jumps over the middle of home’s ten years at the turn, and fills up to ten on the way back. Then press Round the turn: the jump becomes a quick sweep.

The experiment, scaled down

A ten-year simulation is one thing. John and Cal wanted to do it for real, and The Van was capable of a short trip at a good fraction of the speed of light, as long as nobody asked how.

So they did the twins’ experiment at a scale that wouldn’t cost them any birthdays. Albert took two of the little clocks from his shoebox, set them going together side by side on the café counter, where there could be no argument about it, and handed one to Cal. The other stayed on the counter, beside the coffee machine. Then The Van took off, climbed out of the atmosphere, ran out to The Station at six-tenths of the speed of light, turned round there, ran back, and came down into the car park, rather heavily.

The whole trip took a few minutes by the café’s clocks. Most of that was the climb and the descent, at speeds where the clocks hardly drift apart at all; the part at six-tenths of the speed of light, out to The Station a little beyond the Moon and back, came to about four and a half seconds. At six-tenths of the speed of light the Lorentz factor is 1.25, so during those seconds The Van’s clock ran at four-fifths of the counter clock’s rate.

Cal walked into the café and put his clock down beside the one on the counter. They were side by side, at the same place and the same moment. Nobody’s frame came into it.

The Van’s clock was nine-tenths of a second behind.

“You are younger than you would have been,” said Albert, examining the two clocks over his glasses. “Very slightly. Don’t let it go to your heads.”

The Van’s own time display, which might have been expected to take an interest, was busy asking for a firmware update for the dishwasher.

Real twins

The Van is fiction; the principle has been tested with real clocks. Three of the experiments in the third journey were twin experiments, clocks sent on different journeys and compared face to face when they met again: Hafele and Keating’s atomic clocks, flown round the world on airliners and brought back to the ones left at the United States Naval Observatory (the authors called their result an unambiguous resolution of the clock paradox with real clocks); CERN’s muons, going round and round their ring; and the aluminium ion at the National Institute of Standards and Technology, moving back and forth at the speed of a bicycle. All of them came out as relativity predicts. (Clocks on aircraft also run at different rates because of their height in the Earth’s gravity, which the ninth journey explains, so an honest comparison has to include both effects.)

Back to Teide

They had come back to the café a little later than intended. By the standards of relativistic travel the delay was insignificant. By the standards of Albert’s kitchen, it was just long enough for the doughnuts to sell out.

Cal put the return ticket on the table beside the two clocks.

“So we can choose who stays home and who travels,” he said, “but we can’t make both routes collect the same amount of time.”

“Quite,” said Albert.

John looked out across the plateau. The same mountain, the same dark slopes of old lava, the same place they kept leaving and coming back to. On a spacetime diagram, though, coming back to a place never meant coming back to the same event. The café was where it had always been, but the afternoon had moved on. And every path that went away from it and came back would be a little shorter, in time, than the path of the café itself, which had stayed put.

“Every journey,” he said quietly, “is also a journey into the future.”

“And the farther and faster we go,” said Cal, “the less that future belongs to the people we left.”

Albert let them sit with that for a while. Then he pushed the bill across the table.

“Now,” he said, “about the cost of your engine.”
