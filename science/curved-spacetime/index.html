Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 10 of 14

The apple and the argument

The next afternoon, Albert set an apple on the café counter.

John watched him suspiciously. “Are you going to drop it?”

“Yes.”

“And then tell us that gravity didn’t pull it down?”

“Something like that.”

Cal’s coffee was already well out of range. He had learned.

Albert held the apple out at arm’s length, a little above the height of the counter, and let go. It fell, quite quickly, and struck the floor with a modest thump.

“Well,” said John. “Downwards.”

“Indeed,” said Albert.

“Speeding up all the way, the way things do when they’re pulled. It’s not exactly a mystery.”

“And yet you have just found out that clocks tick faster up here than at the coast, and before that, that in a closed room nobody can tell gravity from a floor pushing them along. Which of those explains the apple?”

John opened his mouth, and closed it again.

“The apple,” said Albert, retrieving it, “was falling freely. Nothing was touching it. In the way of thinking we are coming to, that means it was simply following its natural path.”

John looked at the apple, then at Albert. “You’re going to need a better explanation than ‘natural’.”

Albert smiled, reached under the counter, and unrolled an enormous sheet of graph paper.

Drawing a fall on graph paper

Albert drew two lines on the sheet, one along the bottom and one up the side, and labelled them.

Along the bottom, time. Up the side, height. It was the kind of graph most people meet at school, distance against time, except that here the distance was height, so that up the page really did mean up.

First he drew the floor. The floor stays at the same height as time passes, so its line ran straight across the page, along the bottom.

Then he drew the apple. While he was holding it, it stayed at the same height too, about a metre and a bit above the floor: another straight line across the page, higher up. Then he let go. For the first instant the apple’s line still ran almost level, but it soon began to bend downwards, more and more steeply, until it met the floor’s line, about half a second after he had let go. A curve, the shape called a parabola.

A line on a graph like this, the record of where something is at every moment, is a worldline: the same kind of picture as in When Exactly Is Now?, except that there time ran up the page, and here it runs across, because here height means up.

“There,” said John. “The floor’s line is straight, because the floor isn’t moving. The apple’s line is curved, because the apple is speeding up. Things that speed up have curved lines on graphs like this. The apple is the one that’s accelerating.”

It’s what almost everyone would say, and it’s what the graph seems to show. The apple’s line bends; the floor’s doesn’t. Something is pulling the apple downwards, making it go faster and faster. What else could the curve mean?

“Ask them,” said Albert.

Asking the apple

Cal knew what Albert meant. He went out to The Van and came back with two things from the cupboard behind the kettle. One was the accelerometer, the little instrument labelled ACCELERATION, which in the first journey had shown them when The Van’s motion changed, and in Einstein’s Happiest Thought had shown them which of their rooms was being pushed. Stood on its end, it measures the push of whatever is holding it up. The other was the battered probe from the first journey, with the ruler painted down its side. Albert produced a second accelerometer, identical to the first, from under the counter, without explaining why he had one.

Cal stood one on its end on the floor beside the counter. Its weight sagged in its springs, and the needle read 1 g: one unit of the Earth’s gravity, about 9.8 metres per second per second. That was the floor, pushing up on the instrument, just as they had learned to expect.

The other he taped to the apple. Albert held it out at arm’s length again, and its needle read 1 g too: Albert’s hand was holding it up.

Then Albert let go.

The moment the apple left his fingers, the needle on the apple swung to zero, and it stayed at zero all the way down. The needle on the floor didn’t move at all: 1 g, before, during and after. When the apple hit the floor there was a brief, violent jolt on its instrument, the only moment in the whole fall when anything happened to it. Then, lying on the floor, it read 1 g, like the floor.

What the accelerometers say

An accelerometer answers one question, whatever anybody thinks about which object is “really” moving: is something pushing on me? And both of them answered clearly.

The falling apple wasn’t being pushed. Nothing touched it, and its accelerometer felt nothing, exactly as if it were floating in The Van with the engine off, in the empty space of the first journey. The floor, on the other hand, was being pushed all the time, up from below, by the rock of the mountain, and its accelerometer felt every bit of that push: one g, the same reading the accelerometer had given in The Van in deep space, with the engine pushing.

Which one is really accelerating?

Now set those readings beside everything you have ever felt. When you drop something, it falls: it speeds up, faster and faster, until it hits the ground. The ground just stays where it is, and so do you, standing on it. That is what John drew on the graph paper, and it is what you have felt every day of your life.

The accelerometers say the opposite. The object with nothing acting on it, floating along like the screw in The Van with its engine off, was the apple. The object being pushed, constantly, at one g, was the floor.

The next figure sets four instruments side by side: the apple’s and the floor’s at the café, and two in The Van in deep space, one coasting with its engine off and one with its engine pushing at one g, as in Einstein’s Happiest Thought.

The eighth journey ended with two pictures of the café that no experiment in a closed room could choose between. In the first, gravity pulls you down and the floor holds you up against it. In the second, the floor is pushing you because it is accelerating, as The Van’s floor did in deep space. The first picture can still explain the readings. Gravity pulls on every part of the falling apple alike, so the springs in its accelerometer feel nothing, though the apple is speeding up; and the floor’s accelerometer reads one g because the floor holds it up against gravity, though nothing is moving. But to explain them that way, you need a force that no accelerometer ever registers, falling or standing still; as the three rooms showed, it vanishes completely, in a small room, for anyone who falls with it.

Einstein took the accelerometers at their word, and the result turns John’s reading of the graph upside down.

The apple, falling, has nothing acting on it, and it is doing what anything left alone does: carrying straight on, along its natural path, as Albert said. Its acceleration is zero. So a freely falling room, over a small region and a short time, really is an inertial reference frame, the kind of room in which a thing left alone carries straight on. The falling Van was one. The café, like The Van with its engine running, is a room being pushed.

The floor, reading one g, really is accelerating. It is being pushed, all the time, off the natural path the apple follows, by the rock beneath it, which is pushed by the rock beneath that, all the way down into the Earth. That is why physicists call the accelerometer’s reading on the ground a proper acceleration: it is as real an acceleration as The Van’s, with its engine running in deep space.

So the screw John dropped in the car park, with the blinds down, never fell to the floor. The floor came up to meet it, just as The Van’s floor came up to meet the screw in deep space. And you, sitting or standing as you read this, are being accelerated upwards at one g, now, and have been all your life. The weight you feel isn’t gravity pulling you down. It is the floor, and the Earth beneath it, accelerating up to meet you.

John put a hand flat on the counter, as if to make sure of it.

“But it isn’t going anywhere,” he said. “Nothing here is. This café has been on the same bit of mountain for years.”

“Yes,” said Albert. “That is the interesting part.”

It is a fair objection, and a strong one. Anything accelerating upwards at one g, about ten metres a second faster every second, should be nearly eighteen kilometres up within a minute, and the café hasn’t moved. In the everyday picture of space and time, there is no answer to that. Albert had one, but it would take him the rest of the afternoon.

The apple’s path, meanwhile, has a name, because in Einstein’s picture of gravity it is the spacetime version of a straight line.

Geodesic

The straightest possible path. On a flat sheet of paper, a geodesic is an ordinary straight line. On a globe, it’s a great circle, like the route an aircraft flies between two distant cities: as straight as the curved surface allows. In spacetime, a geodesic is the worldline of anything moving freely, with nothing but gravity acting on it: a falling apple, an orbiting space station, a coasting spaceship. An accelerometer carried along a geodesic reads zero. Over any small stretch, a geodesic looks exactly like the straight, steady motion of inertia; only over longer stretches can it look curved.

Two graphs of one fall

“All right,” said John. “The apple’s accelerometer says zero. But the apple’s line on the graph is still curved. I can see it.”

“Whose graph?” asked Cal.

John looked at the paper. It was, in fact, the floor’s graph: the heights were measured from the floor, and the floor’s line was the one drawn straight along the bottom.

This is exactly the lesson of the first journey, in a new setting. There, a doughnut thrown across The Van went straight across relative to The Van, and along a long diagonal relative to the probe drifting outside. Neither path was more true than the other; they were the same throw, measured relative to different things. The shape of a path on a graph depends on what you measure it against.

So try measuring the fall against something falling with the apple instead. That’s what the probe was for. Cal switched off its thruster, held it out beside the apple, and when Albert let go of the apple, Cal let go of the probe. The two fell side by side, as everything does, whatever its weight, and the probe’s cameras watched the whole way down.

The probe’s pictures were strange. Relative to the probe, the apple hung perfectly still beside it, level with the same mark on the probe’s ruler all the way down. Its worldline, measured against the probe, was a straight line across the page. It was the floor that moved. In the probe’s pictures, the floor rushed up towards the apple, faster and faster, its worldline curving upwards, until it met the apple and the probe together. (The probe survived. It always does.)

So relative to the floor, the apple’s line curves and the floor’s is straight; relative to the probe falling with the apple, the floor’s line curves and the apple’s is straight. Which curve is real?

Here, unlike in the first journey, there’s a way to decide, and it’s the accelerometers. They read the same whichever graph you draw: zero on the apple, 1 g on the floor. The curve on a graph depends on the graph; the accelerometer’s reading depends only on the push.

Play the fall, or drag through it with the time slider. Watch the apple’s curve in the top graph and the floor’s curve in the bottom one, and notice that they are the same curve, turned over. Then watch the accelerometers, which agree in both: on the apple, 1 g while Albert holds it, zero all the way down, a jolt as it lands, and 1 g again once the floor is holding it up; on the floor, 1 g throughout. Change the height Albert drops it from: a higher drop takes longer, but the shapes and the readings are the same. And switch the graphs to “time upwards” to see the same worldlines drawn the way When Exactly Is Now? drew them.

Why doesn’t the Earth get bigger?

Cal had been quiet, turning the instrument on the floor in his fingers.

“Never mind the café,” he said. “If the floor’s the thing that’s accelerating, upwards, all the time, then so is the ground on the other side of the Earth. That’s accelerating upwards too, which from here is downwards. Every bit of the Earth’s surface is accelerating outwards, all the time, and has been for billions of years. So why isn’t the Earth getting bigger?”

It’s a very good question, and neither John’s graph nor the probe’s can answer it. Both of them are pictures of one small place, the café floor and the air above it, for a moment or two. To see why the ground can be pushed off its natural path for ever without going anywhere, we need a picture of something more: of what gravity does to time.

Albert rolled up the graph paper.

Rolling the graph into a tube

He didn’t put it away. He rolled it into a tube, with the time axis running round the tube and the height axis running along it, and stood it on the counter, height upwards.

What going round the tube means

One warning first: rolling the paper doesn’t mean that time goes round in circles. Time never comes back to where it started, and nothing in this picture says it does. Rolling the paper is just a way of giving it a shape that we can then change, and it works like this. Each time round the tube stands for one second, or one tick, of the clocks standing still at that height. The faint lines running up the tube mark the tenths of that second, the same reading on every clock. Going round once is one tick; going round again is the next tick, and so on, for as long as you like.

On a plain tube, nothing much has changed. The floor’s worldline, which ran straight along the bottom of the flat sheet, now runs round the bottom of the tube in a ring. The apple’s worldline while Albert held it is another ring, higher up. And a straight line drawn on the flat paper is still straight on the tube: you can roll and unroll the paper as often as you like without stretching it. If the apple were let go in empty space, its worldline would set off running round the tube at its own height, and it would carry on doing so, round and round, for ever. Nothing falls in empty space. A plain tube is a picture of no gravity at all.

In the next figure, John’s graph rolls up into a tube and flat again. Rolled, each lap is one tick, and an apple let go in empty space goes round and round at its own height.

From a tube to a cone

But Time Runs Faster Upstairs found something that a plain tube can’t show. Near the Earth, clocks at different heights don’t tick at the same rate. A clock lower down runs slower than a clock higher up: each of its seconds lasts slightly longer, compared with the clocks above it.

So Albert made a new tube. He cut a fresh sheet into a gently curved fan, the shape you’d cut to make a lampshade, and rolled that instead. Going round it once still meant one tick of the clocks at that height. But now the distance round, how long each tick lasts compared with the others, was different at different heights: longer near the bottom, where the clocks are slower and their seconds last longer, and shorter near the top. The tube had become a cone, wider at the bottom than at the top.

In the next figure, three clocks stand at different heights, and beside each a dot goes round the paper, all three together, each lap one tick. They finish every lap at the same moment, but the bottom dot has furthest to go, because the bottom clock’s tick lasts longest. Make the clocks agree, and the cone becomes a tube again.

“Now,” said Albert, “drop the apple.”

A straight line on a cone

Cal took a roll of sticky tape. He started it at the apple’s height, at the moment of release, running level round the cone: that’s the way the apple’s worldline sets off when Albert lets go, not yet moving up or down, only on through time. Then he let the tape find its own way, smoothing it down without creasing it or stretching it. That’s a dependable way to draw the straightest possible line on a curved surface: a strip of tape laid flat can’t turn to one side or the other without wrinkling.

Before following Cal’s tape, it’s worth seeing what a straight line looks like on a cone, on a cone of its own. In the next figure, a straight strip of tape is pressed onto a tall cone without bending it sideways, and then the cone is cut and laid flat. On the cone the strip spirals; laid flat, it is still perfectly straight, while a circle round the cone, which might look like the straight way round, turns out to be curved. The strip is a geodesic of the cone: the straightest path its surface allows.

The tape ran round the cone. And as it went, it drifted downwards, towards the wide end, slowly at first and then more steeply, until it met the ring at the bottom: the floor.

The tape had gone as straight as the paper allowed, with nobody pushing it down, and the shape of the paper, wider below, had carried it there. On a cone that’s wider at the bottom, a line that sets off running level can’t stay level and stay straight. To keep to the same height, it would have to keep turning, curling round the cone. Left to go straight, it drifts towards the wider part: towards the slower clocks.

That, in a picture, is why the apple falls. It isn’t pulled. It goes as straight as it can through a spacetime in which time runs at different rates at different heights, and going straight carries it down, towards where time runs slower.

Unrolling the cone

Then Albert slit the cone along one of its lines and laid it flat again, and it became a fan on the counter, with the tape across it.

Laid flat, the tape was perfectly straight, as it had been all along.

The floor’s ring, though, had turned into an arc: part of a circle, curving round. On the flat fan you could see at once which of them was going straight and which wasn’t. To follow the floor’s arc, you’d have to keep turning, like a car going round a roundabout, which, as the first journey said, is accelerating all the time even though it never gets any closer to the middle. That’s what the floor’s accelerometer had been reading all along: 1 g, the push needed to keep the floor going round its arc instead of straight on.

And here at last is the answer to John’s objection, and to Cal’s question for one small place. The floor can be pushed off the straight path for ever without going anywhere, just as a car can go round a roundabout for ever without reaching the middle.

“So the floor really is coming up to meet the apple,” said John slowly. “At one g, all the time, without ever getting anywhere.”

“And to meet you,” said Cal. “Like The Van’s floor in deep space. Only this engine never switches off.”

“That’s the bit I can’t get past,” said John. “It isn’t only this floor. The ground in New Zealand is coming up to meet their apples too, and from here that’s downwards. Every bit of ground on Earth, accelerating outwards, all the time. The whole planet ought to be swelling like a balloon.”

“Is the car on the roundabout getting further from the middle?” asked Albert.

“No. It’s turning towards it all the time, and it never gets any closer.”

“So accelerating doesn’t have to mean going anywhere. It means being pushed off the straight path. Here the straight path is the apple’s, and the apple falls. The floor is pushed off that path, upwards, and stays exactly where it is.”

“And in New Zealand,” said Cal slowly, “their apples fall towards the middle of the Earth too. From the other side.”

“From every side at once,” said Albert. “Keep hold of that. One small piece of paper can’t show you what it means for the whole Earth. You will see it before the afternoon is out.”

Finally, Albert drew the apple’s straight line and the floor’s arc back onto ordinary graph paper, height against time, flat. On it, the floor was a straight line along the bottom again, and the apple’s path curved down to meet it: John’s graph, exactly as he had drawn it at the start. Same paper, same two paths. On the cone and on the fan, the apple’s path was the straight one; on the graph, the floor’s was.

The next figure runs Albert’s demonstration through, and back again: the apple’s cone with Cal’s tape, laid flat, and drawn back onto John’s graph.

The idea of rolling a spacetime diagram into a tube and tapering it into a cone, to show how a difference in the rate of time makes things fall, is Mahesh Shenoy’s, from his Float Head Physics videos; the drawings here are new. The older idea underneath it, of gravity as time bending, with the falling apple going straight while the tree, held up by the Earth, follows the bending, goes back at least to Lewis Carroll Epstein’s book Relativity Visualized (1981), where it is drawn as a time axis bent into an arc.

Where the cone picture fails

The cone is an analogy, a way of seeing, and it can mislead in several ways if it’s pushed too far.

First, as we said when the paper was rolled, time isn’t round. The rolling is a device for giving the paper a shape.

Second, the taper is wildly exaggerated. Between the café and the coast, the real difference in the clocks’ rates is about two parts in ten million million. A cone tapering that gently would be indistinguishable from a straight tube for an enormously long way: if you followed it up until it came to a point, the point would be about a light-year above the café. Everyday falling comes from a difference far too small to see on any paper.

Third, and most subtly, paper and spacetime don’t measure paths in the same way. On paper, a straight line is the shortest way between two points. In spacetime, as A Return Ticket to Tomorrow found, the free path between two events is the one along which a clock records the most time. The cone gives the right answer here, an apple falling down rather than up, because of the way it was built: each lap a tick of the local clocks, the paper widest where those ticks last longest. Build it the other way round, wider where the clocks are fast, and the tape would drift upwards. So the cone points the right way, but it can’t be measured with a ruler. For real numbers, the parabola on the graph, and the working behind it, are what to trust.

And fourth, the cone, like the plain tube, can be unrolled flat without stretching or tearing it, which is exactly how the fan was made. That is true of real spacetime too: over a region as small as the café, for a second or two, spacetime really is very nearly flat, which is why the falling Van of Einstein’s Happiest Thought felt just like empty space. What makes the Earth’s spacetime truly curved shows up only over larger distances, and it needs a better picture than paper.

What general relativity says

In the flat spacetime of special relativity, there is a rule for working out how much time a clock records between two events, given how far apart they are in space and in time. It’s the rule behind the light clock and the twins: the more a clock moves between two events, the less time it records. Rules like this, which turn the separations between events into what clocks and rulers actually measure, have a name.

The metric

The rule that turns the separation between two nearby events into what a clock or a ruler would actually measure between them. In empty space, far from any mass, it’s the rule of special relativity: the further a clock travels between two events, the less time it records. A clock that covers six light-seconds (the distance light travels in six seconds, about 1.8 million kilometres) while ten seconds pass records only eight seconds; one that kept pace with light, covering ten light-seconds in those ten seconds, would record no time at all. Near the Earth, the rule changes from place to place: for instance, a clock standing still higher up records more time than one on the ground, between the same two moments, by about one part in ten million billion for every metre of height. In general relativity, the metric is the complete description of the shape of spacetime, and matter and energy decide what it is.

In general relativity, gravity is the metric. Mass and energy shape it, according to equations Einstein found in 1915, and anything moving freely follows its geodesics. The physicist John Wheeler put it in a single line: “Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve.”

Near the Earth, for slow-moving things like apples, almost all of the effect comes from one part of the metric, the part that says how fast clocks run at different heights, which is what the cone was drawn to show. That’s why the picture of tapering time works so well for a falling apple. It can’t do everything. Light, which moves as fast as anything can, also feels the way gravity stretches space, and that part of the metric is just as important for it, as the next journey will show.

Curved spacetime

Now back to Cal’s question, for the whole Earth rather than the café floor.

Over the café, the probe’s pictures and the graph paper and the cone all work, because the café is small. But imagine dropping two apples at once, one on Teide and one on the other side of the Earth. Each falls freely; each has an accelerometer reading zero; each, on its own little patch of graph paper, is going perfectly straight. Yet they are falling towards each other, both heading for the centre of the Earth. Two straight lines that start out parallel and then converge: that can’t happen on flat paper. It happens on a globe, where lines of longitude, each one as straight as the surface allows, start out parallel at the equator and meet at the poles.

This is the tidal effect of Einstein’s Happiest Thought, and it’s the sign of true curvature. You can make gravity vanish in one small place by falling with it; you can’t make it vanish everywhere at once. No single flat sheet can hold all the Earth’s falling apples as straight lines. The ground everywhere is pushed off its geodesic, and the geodesics themselves converge, so the ground can be pushed outwards all round the planet, for billions of years, without the Earth getting any bigger.

“Like the screws at either end of the stretched Van,” said Cal. “They drifted together because both were falling towards the same middle. Make the Van as wide as the Earth.”

“So the ground isn’t racing away from the centre,” said John. “The straight paths are closing in on it, from every side, and the ground is holding out against them.”

“Everywhere at once,” said Albert. “That is what one g on every floor on Earth is measuring. And the Earth stays exactly the size it is.”

Curved spacetime

Spacetime whose geometry is not the flat geometry of special relativity. Over a small enough region, for a short enough time, curved spacetime always looks flat: a small falling room is just like empty space. But over larger regions, the curvature shows. Freely falling things that start out side by side drift together or apart, the tidal effects, and clocks at different places tick at different rates, in ways that no single flat diagram can show. Near the Earth, the curvature that matters most for a falling apple is in time: clocks lower down run slower.

Why the apple’s path has the most time

In A Return Ticket to Tomorrow, two travellers parted and met again, and the one who coasted between the meetings, following a straight worldline, recorded the most time. Free paths are the ones with the most time on them. That’s still true here, and now there are two effects to balance.

Suppose Cal throws the apple straight up and catches it as it comes down, two seconds later. Between leaving his hand and coming back to it, the apple could, in principle, have taken all sorts of paths: stayed low and loitered, or shot up high and come straight back. Which path gives its clock the most time?

  • Going higher helps, because clocks run faster higher up, as the clocks on the mountain showed.
  • But to go higher and still be back in his hand after two seconds, the apple would have to move faster, and moving clocks run slow, as A Clock Made of Light showed.

The path the apple actually takes, rising about five metres and falling back in a parabola, is the best bargain between those two. Along it, the apple’s clock records more time than along any nearby path with the same start and finish, and more than Cal’s clock in his hand, by about four ten-million-billionths of a second. That’s far too little to measure with a thrown apple, but it’s there. Falling, in Einstein’s theory, is what happens when a free object takes the path through spacetime with the most time on it.

Why the famous rubber sheet is not enough

You may have seen gravity illustrated with a heavy ball placed on a stretched rubber sheet. The sheet sags, and smaller balls rolled across it curve round the dip, or spiral into it. It’s a lovely demonstration, and it gets one thing right: mass changes the shape of something, and things moving freely follow that shape.

But it has two serious problems. The small balls roll down into the dip because the Earth’s gravity pulls them down the slope, so the demonstration uses gravity to explain gravity. And, more seriously, the rubber sheet is a picture of curved space, with no time in it at all, so it can’t explain the most basic thing an apple does: start falling from rest. Put a marble down on a rubber sheet, perfectly still, and even if the sheet is curved, there is no reason for a still marble to go anywhere; without the Earth’s pull on it, it would just sit there. An apple held still above the floor isn’t moving through space at all. So how does curved space make it move?

The answer is that the apple is moving, even while it hangs in Albert’s hand: through time, at one second per second, like everything else. That’s the line running level round the tube. And because time is what’s curved, near the Earth, the apple’s motion through time is turned, gradually, into motion through space: downwards. A sheet with no time in it can never show that. The cone, crude as it is, puts time first, which is where, for the apple, the curvature is.

Back to Teide

The Van never left the car park. It was a rare occasion on which an entire adventure had been conducted with paper, an apple, a roll of tape and Albert’s apparently bottomless collection of props.

It had not, however, been entirely uneventful for The Van. When Cal had gone out to fetch the accelerometer, the navigation screen had been showing the café’s position, as usual, to within a few hundred metres. Later, while Cal was putting the instruments back in the cupboard, John had called across the car park that the floor of the café was accelerating upwards at 9.8 metres per second per second, and always had been. When Cal came back past the dashboard, the screen read:

NAVIGATION SYSTEM HAS RESIGNED. THE GROUND IS NOT WHERE I LEFT IT.

It had not withdrawn its resignation.

Outside, John stood looking at the distant rock formations. In the late light, everything looked firmly rooted to the ground. The rocks, the café, The Van: all of them were being held up, every moment, by the ground beneath them, pushed off the paths they would otherwise take through spacetime. Only the things that fell, the odd stone, a dropped apple, were following their own way.

“I used to imagine the apple being forced to go down,” he said. “Now I’m imagining the floor stopping it from carrying on.”

“Only until you drop it,” said Cal.

Albert came out and stood beside them. A single star had appeared above the mountain, the first of the evening. He pointed at it.

“Now,” he said, “if the shape of spacetime changes the path of an apple, what does it do to light?”

John looked at the star. “We know that already,” he said. “The torch you had us imagine in The Van: its beam curved, so light near the Earth must bend too, just as the apple falls. By the same amount, presumably.”

Albert smiled. “I was hoping you would say that.”
