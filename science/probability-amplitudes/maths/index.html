Arrows as numbers

An arrow of length rrr pointing at an angle φ\varphiφ is exactly what a complex number is:

z=reiφ=rcos⁡φ+i rsin⁡φ.z = r e^{i\varphi} = r\cos\varphi + i\, r\sin\varphi.z=reiφ=rcosφ+irsinφ.

Adding arrows head to tail is adding complex numbers, and the length of an arrow is the modulus, ∣z∣=r|z| = r∣z∣=r. The arrow for each way something can happen is its probability amplitude ψ\psiψ, and the chance of an outcome is the squared length of the sum of the amplitudes of all the ways it can happen:

P=∣∑jψj∣2.P = \Big|\sum_j \psi_j\Big|^2.P=​j∑​ψj​​2.

The stopwatch. For light, Feynman’s stopwatch turns once for every wavelength travelled, so a way of length LLL has the phase

φ=2πLλ=kL,k=2πλ,\varphi = 2\pi \frac{L}{\lambda} = kL, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda},φ=2πλL​=kL,k=λ2π​,

and its amplitude is a eikLa\, e^{ikL}aeikL. Red light of 650 nm turns 1/(650×10−9)≈1.5×1061/(650 \times 10^{-9}) \approx 1.5 \times 10^61/(650×10−9)≈1.5×106 times a metre.

Only differences matter. Turning every arrow by the same angle α\alphaα multiplies every amplitude by eiαe^{i\alpha}eiα, and ∣eiαψ∣=∣ψ∣|e^{i\alpha}\psi| = |\psi|∣eiαψ∣=∣ψ∣. So where the watches start is irrelevant; only the differences in phase between the ways can change a chance.

Not knowing, and both. If something goes one way or the other with chances PAP_APA​ and PBP_BPB​ (the coin under the cup), P=PA+PBP = P_A + P_BP=PA​+PB​. If both ways are open with nothing to tell them apart (a superposition), P=∣ψA+ψB∣2P = |\psi_A + \psi_B|^2P=∣ψA​+ψB​∣2, which is not the same, as the next section shows.

The square of the sum

For two ways with amplitudes ψA=a eiφA\psi_A = a\,e^{i\varphi_A}ψA​=aeiφA​ and ψB=b eiφB\psi_B = b\,e^{i\varphi_B}ψB​=beiφB​,

∣ψA+ψB∣2=a2+b2+2abcos⁡(φA−φB).|\psi_A + \psi_B|^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab\cos(\varphi_A - \varphi_B).∣ψA​+ψB​∣2=a2+b2+2abcos(φA​−φB​).

The first two terms are the chances each way would give alone; the third, the interference term, is what adding chances leaves out. It can be positive or negative, which is how a second way can lower a chance.

Two equal slits. With a=ba = ba=b (one slit’s chance is a2a^2a2),

P=2a2(1+cos⁡Δφ)=4a2cos⁡2Δφ2,P = 2a^2\big(1 + \cos\Delta\varphi\big) = 4a^2\cos^2\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2},P=2a2(1+cosΔφ)=4a2cos22Δφ​,

four times one slit’s chance where the hands agree (Δφ=0\Delta\varphi = 0Δφ=0) and nothing where they are opposed (Δφ=π\Delta\varphi = \piΔφ=π). The cosine averages to zero across a stripe, so the average is 2a22a^22a2: the same total as adding chances, moved from the dark stripes into the bright. At a point where the ways differ in length by Δ=dsin⁡θ\Delta = d\sin\thetaΔ=dsinθ, Δφ=2πΔ/λ\Delta\varphi = 2\pi\Delta/\lambdaΔφ=2πΔ/λ, which gives the stripes of the fourth journey’s maths page.

Unequal slits. If A lets through twice as much light as B, a2=2b2a^2 = 2b^2a2=2b2, the chance swings between (a−b)2(a - b)^2(a−b)2 and (a+b)2(a + b)^2(a+b)2. The stripes’ strength (their visibility) is

V=Pmax−PminPmax+Pmin=2aba2+b2=223≈0.94,V = \frac{P_\text{max} - P_\text{min}}{P_\text{max} + P_\text{min}} = \frac{2ab}{a^2 + b^2} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3} \approx 0.94,V=Pmax​+Pmin​Pmax​−Pmin​​=a2+b22ab​=322​​≈0.94,

so the dark stripes keep about 3 per cent of the bright ones’ light: faded a little, not gone.

The mirror and the grating

Where a mirror’s light comes from. Put the lamp and the eye a height h1h_1h1​ and h2h_2h2​ above the mirror and a distance apart along it. The way via the point xxx on the mirror has length

L(x)=(x−x1)2+h12+(x2−x)2+h22,L(x) = \sqrt{(x - x_1)^2 + h_1^2} + \sqrt{(x_2 - x)^2 + h_2^2},L(x)=(x−x1​)2+h12​​+(x2​−x)2+h22​​,

and

dLdx=sin⁡θin−sin⁡θout,\frac{dL}{dx} = \sin\theta_\text{in} - \sin\theta_\text{out},dxdL​=sinθin​−sinθout​,

the difference of the sines of the angles the two legs make with the upright. It is zero, and LLL is at its least, where the angles are equal. Near that point L≈L0+12L′′(x−x0)2L \approx L_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}L''(x - x_0)^2L≈L0​+21​L′′(x−x0​)2, so the arrows stay within half a turn of each other while 12L′′(x−x0)2<λ/2\tfrac{1}{2}L''(x - x_0)^2 < \lambda/221​L′′(x−x0​)2<λ/2: across a patch of half-width λ/L′′\sqrt{\lambda/L''}λ/L′′​. For a lamp and an eye both a metre from the mirror and close together, L′′≈2L'' \approx 2L′′≈2 per metre, and for light of 500 nm the patch is about 2.5×10−7≈0.5\sqrt{2.5 \times 10^{-7}} \approx 0.52.5×10−7​≈0.5 mm in half-width: nearly all the light you see in the mirror comes from a patch about a millimetre across. Beyond it, the arrows curl into spirals (Cornu’s spiral) and cancel.

Fermat’s principle. That the light’s route is the one where LLL (or, in glass and water, the travel time) is stationary, unchanged to first order by small changes of route, is Fermat’s principle of 1662. The arrows explain it: only near a stationary route do neighbouring arrows agree.

The grating. Remove every other strip of width Λ/2\Lambda/2Λ/2, so that what is left repeats every Λ\LambdaΛ. Near a point where the length changes by exactly one wavelength across one repeat,

Λ ∣dLdx∣=mλ,that is,Λ ∣sin⁡θin−sin⁡θout∣=mλ,\Lambda\,\Big|\frac{dL}{dx}\Big| = m\lambda, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \Lambda\,\big|\sin\theta_\text{in} - \sin\theta_\text{out}\big| = m\lambda,Λ​dxdL​​=mλ,that is,Λ​sinθin​−sinθout​​=mλ,

every strip left keeps the same half of the turn, and their arrows add. This is the grating equation. A CD’s tracks are Λ=1.6 μ\Lambda = 1.6\ \muΛ=1.6 μm apart; for light falling straight on (θin=0\theta_\text{in} = 0θin​=0), the first bright direction is at sin⁡θ=λ/Λ\sin\theta = \lambda/\Lambdasinθ=λ/Λ:

red, 650 nm: θ≈24°,blue, 450 nm: θ≈16°.\text{red, 650 nm: } \theta \approx 24°, \qquad \text{blue, 450 nm: } \theta \approx 16°.red, 650 nm: θ≈24°,blue, 450 nm: θ≈16°.

A soap film. Light reflected from the front and back of a film of thickness ttt and refractive index n≈1.33n \approx 1.33n≈1.33 has two ways whose lengths differ by 2nt2nt2nt (at near-normal incidence), and the reflection at the front, from air into soap, turns its arrow by an extra half turn. The two add where

2nt=(m+12)λ,2nt = \left(m + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda,2nt=(m+21​)λ,

which picks out different colours at different thicknesses. As t→0t \to 0t→0 the two arrows are exactly opposed for every colour, and the film goes black.

Phase and the path of most time

The hand turns with the particle’s own clock. For a particle of mass mmm, the amplitude for a path through spacetime is eiS/ℏe^{iS/\hbar}eiS/ℏ, where the action of a free particle (gravity included) is

S=−mc2τ,S = -mc^2 \tau,S=−mc2τ,

τ\tauτ being the proper time, the time a clock carried along the path records (the relativity series’ proper time, with the clock’s rate in gravity included). So the hand turns mc2/hmc^2/hmc2/h times for every second of the particle’s own time:

mc2h=9.11×10−31×(3.00×108)26.626×10−34≈1.2×1020 s−1 (an electron),≈1.4×1049 s−1 (an apple of 100 g).\frac{mc^2}{h} = \frac{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times (3.00 \times 10^8)^2}{6.626 \times 10^{-34}} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{20}\ \text{s}^{-1}\ \text{(an electron)}, \qquad \approx 1.4 \times 10^{49}\ \text{s}^{-1}\ \text{(an apple of 100 g)}.hmc2​=6.626×10−349.11×10−31×(3.00×108)2​≈1.2×1020 s−1 (an electron),≈1.4×1049 s−1 (an apple of 100 g).

In weak gravity and at everyday speeds, τ≈∫(1+gh/c2−v2/2c2) dt\tau \approx \int \big(1 + gh/c^2 - v^2/2c^2\big)\,dtτ≈∫(1+gh/c2−v2/2c2)dt, so

S≈∫(−mc2+12mv2−mgh) dt,S \approx \int \Big(-mc^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - mgh\Big)\,dt,S≈∫(−mc2+21​mv2−mgh)dt,

which, apart from the constant first term, is the action of Newton’s mechanics. (This was Dirac’s remark of 1933, made into a method in Feynman’s thesis of 1942.)

Why the arrows pick the path of most time. Where τ\tauτ is at its greatest, it is stationary: small changes of path change it only at second order. So the phases of neighbouring paths agree there, and nowhere else, exactly as for the mirror.

How sharp the path is. Take the true path and bend it sideways by a distance δ\deltaδ at its middle, keeping its ends (a straight leg out and a straight leg back). Over a time TTT the extra velocity is 2δ/T2\delta/T2δ/T each way, and the action grows by

ΔS=∫12m(2δT)2dt=2mδ2T\Delta S = \int \tfrac{1}{2} m \Big(\frac{2\delta}{T}\Big)^2 dt = \frac{2 m \delta^2}{T}ΔS=∫21​m(T2δ​)2dt=T2mδ2​

(the gravity term, being linear in height, adds nothing at this order). The arrows disagree by half a turn when ΔS=πℏ\Delta S = \pi\hbarΔS=πℏ:

δ=πℏT2m.\delta = \sqrt{\frac{\pi\hbar T}{2m}}.δ=2mπℏT​​.

For Cal’s throw, T=2T = 2T=2 s: an apple of 100 g gives δ≈5.8×10−17\delta \approx 5.8 \times 10^{-17}δ≈5.8×10−17 m, about a thirtieth of a proton’s width of 1.7×10−151.7 \times 10^{-15}1.7×10−15 m; an electron gives δ≈1.9\delta \approx 1.9δ≈1.9 cm.

Back to one turn a wavelength. For a particle of definite energy EEE going from one place to another (as from a slit to the screen), what matters is not SSS but W=S+EtW = S + EtW=S+Et, its phase compared at the same energy. For a free particle, E=γmc2E = \gamma mc^2E=γmc2 and τ=t/γ\tau = t/\gammaτ=t/γ, so

with p=γmvp = \gamma m vp=γmv the momentum and L=vtL = vtL=vt the length of the way. The phase W/ℏ=2πL/(h/p)=2πL/λW/\hbar = 2\pi L/(h/p) = 2\pi L/\lambdaW/ℏ=2πL/(h/p)=2πL/λ: one turn for every de Broglie wavelength of the way, the stopwatch of the slits.

Gravity turns the arrows: neutrons, 1975. In Colella, Overhauser and Werner’s interferometer, the two ways run a height HHH apart for a horizontal length ℓ\ellℓ, enclosing an area A=HℓA = H\ellA=Hℓ (tilted by an angle α\alphaα, Asin⁡αA \sin\alphaAsinα is what counts). The higher way’s clock runs fast by a fraction gH/c2gH/c^2gH/c2 (gravitational time dilation) for the time ℓ/v\ell/vℓ/v the neutron spends there, so its hand gets ahead by

using v=h/(mλ)v = h/(m\lambda)v=h/(mλ). For neutrons of wavelength 0.14 nm and ways enclosing about 10 cm², Δφ≈55\Delta\varphi \approx 55Δφ≈55 radians: nearly nine whole stripes go by as the interferometer is turned from flat to on edge. Their measured shifts agreed with this formula.
