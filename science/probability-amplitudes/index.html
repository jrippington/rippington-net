Why opening a second door can stop you getting through

Opening a second slit made some places harder to reach, and Cal wants to know how more ways in can mean fewer arrivals. Albert suggests that what adds up isn't the chances. By the evening, every path has a little clock hand, a mirror has been scraped into strips, and the apple from the last series turns out to have been doing quantum mechanics all along.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Field Guide to the Very Small, part 5 of 16

Two doors

The café had two doors. One opened onto the car park, and it was the one the coach parties used, coming in out of the wind in a hurry for coffee. The other opened onto the terrace, where John and Cal sat when the sun was out, and in the mornings Albert propped it open with a chair.

Cal had brought his question with him, as Albert had asked.

“Here’s what I can’t get past,” he said. “Pick a place on the screen in the middle of a dark stripe. With only slit A open, photons land there. Lots of them. With only slit B open, just as many. With both open, none. More ways to get there, and nothing gets there.”

“That’s interference,” said John. “Waves cancelling.”

“I know. But they came one at a time. Each one is a single photon, landing in one place, and the question for each one is just: what’s the chance it lands here? And chances don’t work like that.” He nodded at the doors. “When Albert opens the terrace door, it doesn’t stop anyone coming in through the car park door. More people come in, not fewer.”

Cal is right about chances. The rule he’s using is one of the most reliable there is.

When something can happen in two separate ways, the chance that it happens is the chance of the first way plus the chance of the second. Roll a die: the chance of a one is one in six, the chance of a two is one in six, so the chance of a one or a two is two in six, a third. A customer can come into the café through the car park door, or through the terrace door; the chance of coming in at all is the chance of one plus the chance of the other. And a chance is never less than nothing. The worst a way can contribute is nothing at all. So adding a way can never take anything away: the chance can only stay the same or grow.

Now put the two slits into that rule. If slit A alone sends a hundred photons an hour to some place on the screen, and slit B alone sends a hundred, then with both open, by the rule, two hundred should arrive there. At a dark stripe, none do. A hundred and a hundred have made nothing.

Albert had been listening while he filled the coffee machine.

“Suppose,” he said, “that what you add isn’t the chances.”

What can add up to nothing?

It’s a strange suggestion, but it can be tested against something simple. What kinds of things, added together, can come to nothing?

Not chances, and not anything else that only comes in amounts: weights, distances walked, money in a till. Add two amounts of anything like that, and you get more. To add two things and get nothing, each of them needs a direction as well as a size.

Pushes are like that. If John pushes the café’s terrace door from outside, and Cal pushes it just as hard from inside, the door doesn’t move: two real pushes, neither of them nothing, add up to nothing, because they point opposite ways. Steps are like that too. Take ten paces towards the café and ten paces back, and you have walked twenty paces and arrived nowhere.

And waves are like that. In the fourth journey, the stripes from Albert’s hair came from waves arriving by two routes: a crest from one way and a trough from the other cancel. A wave’s height at a place has a direction of its own, up or down, and that’s exactly what lets it cancel.

So here is the step that can’t be dodged. At a dark stripe, two ways each contribute something, and together they contribute nothing. Chances can’t do that, because a chance has only a size. Whatever each way contributes before the adding, it must have a direction as well as a size, so that the two can point against each other. Only after the adding do you get a chance.

“So each way gets an arrow,” said John.

“Each way gets an arrow,” said Albert. He took a napkin from the pile by the till, and a pen from behind his ear, where it had been hidden in his hair. “A man in California once explained all of this to an audience who knew no physics at all, with nothing but arrows. It is the best explanation I know.”

The stopwatch

The man was Richard Feynman, one of the physicists who built the modern quantum theory of light, and in 1983, at the University of California in Los Angeles, he gave four public lectures, the Alix G. Mautner Memorial Lectures, explaining it to a general audience. They became a short book, QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter, published in 1985. The arrows in this journey, the stopwatch that turns them, and the mirror further on are all Feynman’s, from that book; the drawings are new.

Here is the rule, as Albert drew it on the napkin.

Think of every way the photon could get from the lamp to the place on the screen you’re asking about: through slit A, or through slit B. Imagine timing the photon along each way with a stopwatch whose single hand spins round as the photon travels, one turn for every wavelength it covers. For red light that’s about one and a half million turns for every metre. When the photon reaches the end of its way, stop the watch and see where the hand is pointing. Draw an arrow pointing that way. That arrow belongs to that way.

Do the same for every other way. Then add all the arrows together, head to tail: put the tail of the second arrow at the head of the first, and draw a new arrow from the tail of the first to the head of the last. That final arrow is the one that counts. Its length tells you the chance of the photon arriving there: the longer the final arrow, the likelier. And the chance grows faster than the length does: an arrow twice as long means four times the chance, and an arrow three times as long, nine times.

Probability amplitude

The arrow that belongs to each way something can happen: a size and a direction. To find the chance of an outcome, add the arrows of every way it can happen, head to tail, and look at the length of the result: the chance is that length multiplied by itself, so a final arrow twice as long means four times the chance. Arrows pointing opposite ways cancel, which is how a second way can make something less likely. Chances themselves can’t cancel; amplitudes can.

The direction an arrow points has a name of its own, because almost everything in the rest of this series turns on it.

Phase

How far round a repeating cycle something has got: where the hand of the stopwatch points, or how far along a wave is between one crest and the next. Two waves, or two arrows, with the same phase point the same way and add; half a turn apart in phase, they point opposite ways and cancel. In quantum mechanics, the phase of each way’s arrow is set by how far, or for how long, the thing has travelled along it.

One more thing about the stopwatches stops the rule seeming arbitrary. It doesn’t matter which way the hands point when the watches start, so long as every way’s watch starts together, with the photon, at the lamp. Turn every arrow round by the same amount, and the final arrow turns round with them, but its length doesn’t change, and nor does the chance. What decides everything is how far each way’s hand has turned compared with the others’. For light, that comes down to one thing: how much longer one way is than another, counted in wavelengths.

Two slits, with arrows

Now the slits. Pick a place in the very middle of the screen, straight ahead. The way through slit A and the way through slit B are exactly the same length, so the two stopwatches stop with their hands pointing the same way. The two arrows point the same way, and added head to tail they make an arrow twice as long as either. Twice the length means four times the chance: four times as many photons land there as with one slit open.

Notice that’s not twice, which is what adding chances would give. The arrows have done something chances can’t.

Now move to a place a little off to one side, where the way through B is half a wavelength longer than the way through A. B’s stopwatch has had half a turn longer to run, so its hand points exactly the opposite way to A’s. Head to tail, the second arrow brings you straight back to where you started. The final arrow has no length at all. Nothing arrives: a dark stripe.

In between, the hands are partly out of step, and the final arrow is somewhere between nothing and twice the length of one. Move steadily along the screen, and the difference between the two ways grows steadily, and the final arrow grows and shrinks, grows and shrinks: bright, dark, bright. The stripes.

Close slit B, and there’s only one way, with one arrow, and nothing to add it to. Its length is the same everywhere you look (more or less, across the middle of the screen), so the chance is the same everywhere: no stripes. Open B, and a second arrow appears. Where it points along with A’s, the chance goes up to four times; where it points against A’s, the chance falls to nothing. That’s Cal’s answer. A second way can make something less likely because it brings a second arrow, and an arrow can point backwards.

“And it all balances out?” said Cal. “Four times at the bright stripes and nothing at the dark ones?”

“On average, twice,” said Albert. “The same as adding the chances. Nothing is made and nothing is lost. The photons are only moved, out of the dark stripes and into the bright ones.”

The simulator adds the arrows

John wanted to see it done properly, so they took the napkin out to The Van. The dashboard screen was showing a kettle again. Cal hit it in the place that usually worked, and John asked the simulator to rebuild the two slits from the day before, and this time to show the arrows: one for the way through each slit, to whatever place on the screen he pointed at.

He pointed at the middle of a dark stripe. Two arrows appeared, pointing exactly opposite ways.

ARROW A: 21.0 TURNS. ARROW B: 21.5 TURNS. TOTAL: NOTHING

“Now add the chances instead,” said John.

The simulator did as it was told, and the stripes on its screen melted into one smooth patch.

CHANCES ADDED. STRIPES NOT FOUND

“That’s the world where the photons go one way or the other,” said Cal. “It isn’t this one.”

John put the arrows back, and then, because he was John, tried to break them. He asked the simulator to make slit A twice as wide as B, so that more light came through it. Now the way through A had the longer arrow. At the dark stripes the two arrows still pointed opposite ways, but B’s shorter arrow couldn’t reach all the way back. The final arrow was short, but not quite nothing, and the dark stripes were no longer perfectly dark.

STRIPES: FADED A LITTLE. CAUSE: UNEQUAL ARROWS

That’s a real effect, and it fits the rule. Arrows cancel completely only when they’re the same length. Make one way much more likely than the other, and the stripes get fainter, though they never quite go, as long as both ways are open and nothing tells them apart.

In the figure below, the two ways run from slits A and B to a point on the screen, drawn as waves, one crest for each turn of the stopwatch’s hand. The hands themselves are on the two clock faces, and under them the two arrows are added head to tail, with the final arrow in amber. Move the point along the screen with the slider, or press play and let it sweep up and down. Beside the screen, the chance at each place the point has visited is traced out, and the stripes appear. Then press “Add chances instead”: one slit’s chance plus the other’s, as Cal’s rule would have it, and the trace goes flat. The waves are drawn absurdly long, so that you can count them; real light’s are thousands of times shorter, and its stripes far finer.

Both, not one or the other

John had been staring at the napkin.

“So the photon goes through both slits,” he said.

“The theory says something more careful than that,” said Albert. “It says that when there are two ways, and nothing anywhere tells which way was taken, you add the two arrows. Whether that means the photon went through both, or neither, or something else again, is a question people have argued about for a hundred years.”

“But it isn’t going through one or the other and we just don’t know which.”

“No,” said Albert. “That would be a different thing altogether. Let me show you.”

He took a coin from the till, spun it on the counter, and clapped an upturned cup over it before it fell.

“Heads or tails?”

“No idea,” said Cal.

“Exactly. It is one or the other. By now it has fallen, and it is lying under the cup, heads up or tails up, and you don’t know which. That is ordinary not knowing. The chances add: a half for heads and a half for tails, which makes it certain to be one of them.” He lifted the cup. Tails. “And when you look, you find out which.”

The difference matters enormously, and the two slits show why. If each photon really went through one slit or the other, and we merely didn’t know which, it would be like the coin under the cup. The rule for not knowing is Cal’s rule: add the chances. And adding the chances gives no stripes, as the fourth journey found when it added A’s patch to B’s. The stripes prove that the photon is not like the coin. Before it arrives, it isn’t secretly going one way or the other. Both ways count at once, each with its arrow, and the arrows are added.

That also settles the puzzle from the fourth journey, of how a photon going through one slit could know whether the other was open. It doesn’t need to know anything. The rule simply adds an arrow for every way that’s open. Close a slit, and its way is gone, and so is its arrow; open it, and there’s an arrow to add. The photon isn’t checking on the other slit. There is an arrow for each way that’s open, and the arrows do the rest.

Superposition

The state of something that has two or more possibilities open at once, each with its own arrow, so that their arrows add (and can cancel) rather than their chances. A photon passing two slits, with nothing to tell which it took, is in a superposition of the two ways. It is not the same as being in one of them without anyone knowing which, like a coin under a cup: in that case the chances add, and there is no interference. Only a superposition gives stripes.

What a photon in a superposition is really doing, on its way through the slits, is one of the great open questions of physics, and this series comes back to it at the end, in the fifteenth journey. For now, the arrows are enough. They tell you exactly where the photons will land, and they’ve never yet been wrong.

Every path at once

The arrows weren’t invented for slits. Feynman’s point, in his lectures, was that they explain everything light does, including the most ordinary things, the ones nobody ever thinks need explaining.

There was a mirror behind Albert’s counter, in an old frame, between the shelves of cups. Look at a lamp in it, and the light comes to your eye from one place on the mirror: the place where it comes in and goes out at equal angles, as everyone learns at school. But why there? Light from the lamp can reach the mirror anywhere, and bounce from anywhere. What picks out that one place?

Feynman’s answer is that nothing picks it out. Light from the lamp reaches your eye by every way it can: off the middle of the mirror, off the left end, off the right end, off every point in between. Each way gets its arrow, set by its length. To find out how much light reaches your eye, add all the arrows.

Now look at the lengths. Near the equal-angle place, the way via the mirror is at its shortest, and close to its shortest, it hardly changes. Think of the bottom of a valley: right at the bottom, the ground is almost flat, and a step to either side barely changes your height. So the ways via points near the middle all have almost the same length, their stopwatches stop with their hands almost together, and their arrows all point nearly the same way. Added, they make a long run in one direction. Further out towards the ends, the length changes quickly from one point to the next, like the steep sides of the valley, and each way’s hand has turned a little further than its neighbour’s. Their arrows swing round and round, and added head to tail they curl up into tight spirals that go nowhere. Nearly all the light that reaches your eye comes from the middle, because everywhere else the arrows cancel.

So light doesn’t bounce at equal angles because of some special rule about angles. It goes every way at once, and equal angles is what’s left over when the rest has cancelled. The same reasoning says why light seems to travel in straight lines through empty air: the ways close to the straight one have nearly the same length, and agree, while all the crooked ones cancel each other out.

The idea of light picking out a special path is older than any of this. In 1662 the French mathematician Pierre de Fermat proposed that light always travels between two places by the quickest route, and from that one idea he could explain both why a mirror reflects at equal angles and how light bends when it passes from air into water, where it travels more slowly. It worked, but it left people uneasy, because it seemed to need the light to know in advance which route would be quickest. Feynman’s arrows remove the unease. Light doesn’t know anything. It goes every way, and the quickest route is simply where the arrows agree.

Scraping the mirror

If that sounds like a story told after the event, Feynman had a test for it, and it works. Suppose that near one end of the mirror, where the arrows curl round and round and cancel, you scraped away the silver in narrow strips: just the strips whose arrows point, say, roughly downwards, and left the rest. Then the arrows near that end would no longer cancel. The ones left would all point more or less the same way, and they’d add up. Light would come into your eye from the end of the mirror, at an angle where an ordinary mirror sends none.

There’s a catch, and it’s the best part. The strips that need scraping depend on how fast the hands turn, which depends on the light’s wavelength: its colour. Strips spaced just right for red light leave arrows for blue light pointing every which way. So a mirror scraped in strips sends red light into your eye from one place on it, green from a place a little further along, and blue from further still. It throws a rainbow.

You have almost certainly seen this. A CD is a mirror scraped in strips. Its music, or its data, is stored in a spiral track of tiny pits, and the turns of the spiral are about 1.6 thousandths of a millimetre apart: thousands of narrow strips across its shiny face. Tilt a CD under a lamp, and it shines in bands of colour that slide across it as you tilt it. That’s the arrows near the ends of Feynman’s mirror, with the cancelling ones scraped away. Hold a CD under a single bright lamp and try it. If you’ve no CD, the shiny side of an old DVD does the same, with its strips even closer together.

The figure below is Feynman’s mirror. A lamp and an eye stand above it, with a shade between them so that no light goes straight across. Some of the ways from the lamp to the eye are drawn; those whose arrows add up are drawn in the light’s colour. Below are all the arrows, one for each of 141 ways, added head to tail, the arrows from the middle of the mirror in white. The amber arrow is the total. With the whole mirror, the middle’s arrows make the long run and the ends curl away into nothing. Then scrape the mirror. In the middle, half the arrows have gone, and the light from there is weaker. But near each end, the strips that are left are the ones whose arrows pointed one way, and that light now reaches the eye: watch the place on the mirror glow. Change the colour and the glowing place moves.

A soap bubble works in a similar way, with only two ways to add for each colour: light bouncing off the front of the thin film of soap, and light bouncing off the back. Whether the two arrows agree or cancel depends on how much further the second way goes, the film’s thickness there, and on the light’s colour. Where the film is just thick enough for red’s two arrows to agree, the bubble shines red; a little thinner, and blue’s agree instead. As the film drains and thins, the colours swirl. Just before it bursts, the film is so thin that every colour’s two arrows cancel, and the top of the bubble goes black.

The path with the most time

Cal had been turning the CD over and over, watching the colours slide.

“So light tries every way,” he said, “and the ways that agree are the ones you see.” He stopped. “That’s what the apple does.”

He meant the apple from The Apple That Fell in a Straight Line, the tenth journey of the series before this one. There, John and Cal found that an apple, thrown up and falling back into Cal’s hand, follows the one path through spacetime along which its own clock records more time than along any other path between the same start and finish. Going higher gains time, because clocks run faster higher up; going higher in the same two seconds means moving faster, and moving clocks run slow. The path the apple actually takes is the best bargain between the two: the path with the most time.

“I always wondered,” said Cal, “how the apple knows which path has the most time. It would have to try them all.”

“Perhaps it does,” said Albert.

He was being only slightly mischievous. Feynman’s arrows aren’t only for light. Electrons, atoms, molecules and doughnuts all have them, for every way they can go. And for anything with mass, there’s a deeper answer to the question of what turns the stopwatch’s hand. The hand turns with the thing’s own clock: a fixed number of turns for every second of its own time, the time a clock riding along with it would record, as in A Clock Made of Light. The rate is enormous. An electron’s hand turns about a hundred million million million times a second. An apple’s turns so fast that writing down how many times a second takes a number fifty digits long. (For an electron flying steadily from a slit to the screen, this comes to the same thing as before: one turn of the hand for every wavelength of the way, its matter wave’s wavelength from the fourth journey. The maths page shows why.)

Now do for the apple exactly what was done for the mirror. Think of every path the apple could take from leaving Cal’s hand to arriving back in it two seconds later: high, low, wobbly, straight up and straight down. Each path has its arrow, and the arrow’s direction is set by how much time the apple’s clock records along that path. Add all the arrows.

Near the path with the most time, the time hardly changes from one path to its neighbours, for the same reason the ways near the middle of the mirror hardly change in length: right at the top of a hill, as at the bottom of a valley, the ground is flat. So the arrows of the paths near the most-time path all point almost the same way, and add up. Further from it, a small change of path changes the clock’s time by only a tiny amount, but the hand is turning so unimaginably fast that a tiny amount of time is millions of turns. Neighbouring paths’ arrows point every which way, and cancel. What’s left is the path with the most time, and the paths very, very close to it.

So the apple doesn’t need to know which path has the most time. Every path is tried, and everywhere except along that one the arrows cancel. The rule the relativity series found for falling things, the path with the most time, is what the arrows leave behind.

How close is “very, very close”? For the apple, in Cal’s two-second throw, the paths whose arrows agree lie in a band around the path with the most time about a thirtieth of the width of a proton across. That is as sharp a path as anything could ever have, which is why an apple seems simply to follow its path, and why the relativity series could describe the fall without mentioning arrows at all. But an electron’s hand turns far more slowly, because it has so little mass, and for an electron the band over the same two seconds is a couple of centimetres wide: easily wide enough to take in both sides of Albert’s hair. That’s why electrons make stripes and apples don’t.

The idea of building quantum mechanics this way, by adding an arrow for every path, came out of a remark Paul Dirac made in 1933, which the young Feynman turned into a whole method in his doctoral thesis, in 1942. Feynman showed that it gives exactly the same answers as every other way of doing quantum mechanics, and that, for big things, it turns into the ordinary rules of motion, the ones Newton wrote down, and the path of most time that Einstein’s give.

It has been measured, too. In 1975, at the University of Michigan, Roberto Colella, Albert Overhauser and Samuel Werner sent neutrons from a nuclear reactor through an interferometer carved from a single crystal of silicon, which split each neutron’s ways and brought them back together to make stripes. Then they slowly tilted the crystal, so that one way ran higher than the other. Gravity changed the turning of the higher way’s arrows relative to the lower’s, and the stripes moved, by the amount the arrows predicted. It was the first time anyone had seen gravity act on quantum mechanics’ arrows. Today the same trick, done with clouds of atoms, makes some of the most sensitive instruments for measuring gravity there are.

Albert looked quietly pleased, as though gravity had done him a small personal kindness.

Back to Teide

In the evening the plateau went gold, and then grey. Albert was washing cups at the sink, and the bubbles in the bowl, caught in the last of the light through the kitchen window, swirled with colours as they thinned. He had found, in the cupboard of props, an old CD with no box and no label, and Cal had propped it on the terrace wall, where the low sun threw a smear of rainbow from it across the tiles.

“Every way at once,” said Cal. “Each with its arrow. Add them up, and that’s the chance.”

“And it’s never been wrong,” said John. He didn’t sound happy about it. “But it only tells you what happens at the end. It tells you where the photon lands, and how likely each place is. It doesn’t tell you what happened on the way.”

“It tells you to add the arrows for both ways.”

“That’s my point. A photon goes through the slits. It lands in one place. On the way, which way did it really go? Through A? Through B? Both? It must have done something.”

“Then look,” said Cal.

“We could,” said John slowly. “We could put something beside the slits and watch. We’ve got the probe. It’s never once broken down.”

Albert dried his hands, and looked out at The Van in the car park, where the probe sat in its cupboard behind the kettle.

“Many people have tried looking,” he said. “It is a very good thing to try. Do it in the morning.”
