Why tidal effects make tails

The stretch. A star at distance rrr from the centre of its galaxy (mass MMM) feels that galaxy’s pull, GM/r2GM/r^2GM/r2. A passing galaxy of mass mmm at distance DDD pulls the star and the centre slightly differently, and the difference, its tidal effect, stretches the disc along the line between them. for a while, the edge of the disc is pulled outwards with nearly a third of the force holding it in. For two equal galaxies passing 60,00060{,}00060,000 light-years apart, the tidal effect at the edge is larger than the disc’s own pull.

That’s almost exactly Ω\OmegaΩ: stars on the side of the disc nearest the visitor, going round the same way, keep pace with it for most of the pass and feel its pull in nearly the same direction the whole time, so its effect adds up. A star going round the other way sees the visitor sweep by at Ω+ω≈2.1\Omega + \omega \approx 2.1Ω+ω≈2.1, twice as fast, and the pull’s direction swings round past it before it can do much. Black holes and their galaxies

Kormendy and Ho (2013) fitted the masses of the black holes in the centres of nearby galaxies against the masses of the round bulges of stars round them:

M∙109 M⊙=0.49(Mbulge1011 M⊙)1.16,\frac{M_\bullet}{10^9\, M_\odot} = 0.49 \left(\frac{M_\text{bulge}}{10^{11}\, M_\odot}\right)^{1.16},109M⊙​M∙​​=0.49(1011M⊙​Mbulge​​)1.16,

with a scatter of about a factor of two. At Mbulge=1011M_\text{bulge} = 10^{11}Mbulge​=1011 Suns the black hole is 0.490.490.49 per cent of the bulge; at 101010^{10}1010, 0.340.340.34 per cent; at 101210^{12}1012, 0.710.710.71 per cent. (Earlier estimates, before the biggest black holes were weighed better, gave about 0.10.10.1 to 0.150.150.15 per cent.) The relation holds for true bulges and for round galaxies; disc galaxies without one, like M33, and those whose central bulge is mostly a bar, like the Milky Way, don’t follow it. The Milky Way’s bulge and bar together hold about 0.9×10100.9 \times 10^{10}0.9×1010 Suns (Licquia and Newman 2015), for which the fit gives about 3×1073 \times 10^{7}3×107; its black hole is 4.3×1064.3 \times 10^64.3×106.

Why the black hole can matter to the whole galaxy. Feeding a black hole sets free about a tenth of the infalling mass’s rest energy (next section). Growing one of 10810^8108 Suns sets free

The energy holding 101010^{10}1010 Suns of gas in a bulge whose stars move at σ≈200\sigma \approx 200σ≈200 km/s is about Mσ2≈2×1040 kg×(2×105 m/s)2≈8×1050M\sigma^2 \approx 2 \times 10^{40}\ \text{kg} \times (2\times 10^5\ \text{m/s})^2 \approx 8 \times 10^{50}Mσ2≈2×1040 kg×(2×105 m/s)2≈8×1050 J, two thousand times less. If even a small share of the black hole’s output reaches the gas, it can heat it or blow it out of the galaxy, which stops both the galaxy’s star-making and the black hole’s own feeding: the two hold each other in check.

How a quasar shines

Distance. 3C 273’s lines are shifted by z=0.158z = 0.158z=0.158. In a flat universe with today’s measured expansion (H0=67.7H_0 = 67.7H0​=67.7 km/s/Mpc, 69 per cent dark energy), light with that redshift set out about 2.0 billion years ago.

Power. It shines like about 4×10124 \times 10^{12}4×1012 Suns, L≈4×1012×3.83×1026 W≈1.5×1039L \approx 4 \times 10^{12} \times 3.83 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{39}L≈4×1012×3.83×1026 W≈1.5×1039 W: roughly a hundred times the light of the whole Milky Way (a few times 101010^{10}1010 Suns).

Size. Its brightness changes over a few months. Nothing can brighten all over faster than light can cross it, so the region giving out the light is less than about c×3c \times 3c×3 months, a quarter of a light-year, across.

What it eats. Gas spiralling into a black hole through its disc sets free a share η\etaη of its rest energy, about η≈0.1\eta \approx 0.1η≈0.1 (about 0.060.060.06 for a black hole that doesn’t spin, up to 0.420.420.42 for one spinning as fast as it can). The rate it must be fed is

M˙=Lηc2=1.5×1039 W0.1×(3.00×108 m/s)2≈1.7×1023 kg/s≈2.7 M⊙ a year.\dot{M} = \frac{L}{\eta c^2} = \frac{1.5 \times 10^{39}\ \text{W}}{0.1 \times (3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s})^2} \approx 1.7 \times 10^{23}\ \text{kg/s} \approx 2.7\, M_\odot\ \text{a year}.M˙=ηc2L​=0.1×(3.00×108 m/s)21.5×1039 W​≈1.7×1023 kg/s≈2.7M⊙​ a year.

Fusion, which sets free 0.0070.0070.007 of the rest energy, would need about 393939 Suns’ worth a year, and could never do it from so small a space.

How heavy. Light pushes on the gas it shines through. Gas falling onto anything that shines brighter than the Eddington luminosity is blown back:

LEdd=4πGMmpcσT≈1.26×1031 W×MM⊙.L_\text{Edd} = \frac{4\pi G M m_p c}{\sigma_T} \approx 1.26 \times 10^{31}\ \text{W} \times \frac{M}{M_\odot}.LEdd​=σT​4πGMmp​c​≈1.26×1031 W×M⊙​M​.

For 3C 273 to be fed at all, M≳1.5×1039/1.26×1031≈1.2×108M \gtrsim 1.5 \times 10^{39} / 1.26 \times 10^{31} \approx 1.2 \times 10^8M≳1.5×1039/1.26×1031≈1.2×108 Suns. The black hole must weigh at least about a hundred million Suns.

When quasars were commonest. Their numbers peak at redshifts of about 2 to 3. At z=2z = 2z=2 the light set out about 10.5 billion years ago, when the universe was about 3.3 billion years old, a quarter of its present age.
