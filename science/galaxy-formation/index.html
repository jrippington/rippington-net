John expects a galaxy to be born whole, like a loaf rising in its tin. Albert is rolling out the scraps from a morning's doughnuts. The simulator, John's own pictures from his garden and two brothers with a computer in 1972 agree: big galaxies are built from small ones, by collisions, with a black hole growing at the heart of each. Then the simulator takes them out of the Milky Way to look back at it.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 7 of 18

Scraps

In the morning Albert was cutting doughnuts.

He had rolled the dough out across the counter into a broad, even sheet, and he was pressing the cutter into it in neat rows, lifting out each round and laying it on a floured tray to rise. When the rows were done, what was left on the counter was a ragged lace of scraps. He gathered them up, pressed them together into a ball, and rolled it out again.

John and Cal sat at their table by the window with their coffee. John had been thinking about the end of the sixth journey, when the simulator had run the universe on through its long night: the dark matter gathering into clumps in the dark, the hydrogen falling in after it, and at last the first stars lighting up, until their light had cleared the fog of the dark ages away.

“So that’s how you make a galaxy,” he said. “A great cloud of gas falls into one of the dark matter’s clumps, and it all lights up at once. Stars, arms, the lot. Like a loaf rising in its tin.”

“Is it?” said Albert, without looking up from the scraps.

“Isn’t it?”

“A loaf,” said Albert, “is made all at once, in one tin. Look at your galaxies and tell me whether they were.”

John’s picture is the one most people would draw, and it’s a reasonable one: a galaxy is a single thing, so surely it was made in a single event. In 1962 three astronomers, Olin Eggen, Donald Lynden-Bell and Allan Sandage, put forward something very like it for our own galaxy, the Milky Way. They had measured how some of the oldest stars near the Sun move, and they argued that the whole galaxy had formed from the collapse of one great cloud of gas, quickly, in a few hundred million years at most. Sixteen years later, in 1978, Leonard Searle and Robert Zinn looked at the galaxy’s oldest clusters of stars and found that they didn’t fit: they looked as though they had been made in many separate small pieces, at different times, and gathered in over a billion years or more.

Both had looked at the same galaxy, and they couldn’t both be right.

Albert cut the last rounds from the second rolling, pressed the scraps together again, and rolled out a third, smaller sheet.

The simulator runs on

The Van was in its corner of the car park, and the simulator still had the universe where they had left it, a few hundred million years after the beginning. On the dashboard screen the first stars glittered in the dark, scattered through faint threads and knots of dark matter, which the simulator drew in grey because nobody can see it.

Cal had brought a doughnut from the third rolling. He put it on the dashboard, and the simulator, which these days grades everything it sees, considered it.

“It’s a very good doughnut,” said Cal, loyally.

John ran the simulator on. What it showed was nothing like a loaf.

The first galaxies weren’t great clouds lighting up all at once. They were small: little ragged swarms of stars a few thousand light-years across, many of them, each sitting in its own small clump of dark matter. And the clumps weren’t keeping still. They were falling towards each other, drawn together by their gravity, along the threads. Two small galaxies met and became one. That one met a third. Over hundreds of millions of years, the screen filled with galaxies colliding, merging and growing, small ones into middling ones, middling ones into large. A few of the large ones, by the time the simulator had run on for a few billion years, had settled into spinning discs with spiral arms.

BIG GALAXIES BUILT FROM SMALL ONES: RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

“Why small ones first?” said John.

The answer is a matter of distance. A small clump of dark matter only has to gather the matter close around it, and nearby matter falls in quickly. A clump big enough to hold a galaxy like ours has to gather matter from millions of light-years around, and that takes far longer, because the matter far away has much further to fall, and is pulled much more weakly. So the small clumps finish first, while the big ones are still gathering, and by the time a big clump has pulled itself together, most of what it has pulled in is small clumps that finished long before, with their small galaxies already shining inside them. A big galaxy is built out of small ones because the small ones are ready first.

“The simulator says its confidence is high,” said Cal. “Why? Nobody watched.”

Two things give it that confidence. One is the physics: gravity, and the dark matter that the fifth journey weighed, and the ripples in the oldest light that the fourth journey measured, which say how lumpy the young universe was. Put those into a computer and let gravity work, and this is what comes out, every time.

The other is looking, because looking far away is looking back. In December 1995 the Hubble Space Telescope stared for ten days at a patch of sky near the handle of the Plough, so small that a pinhead held at arm’s length would hide it, and so empty that nobody had seen anything there. The picture, the Hubble Deep Field, held about three thousand galaxies. The nearer ones were spirals and smooth round galaxies, like those round us today. The faintest and farthest, whose light had set out when the universe was young, were small, lumpy and irregular, many of them in pairs and knots, as though caught in the middle of being put together.

The James Webb Space Telescope, which sees the infrared light that the oldest galaxies’ stretched light has become (Further Apart Every Day explains the stretching), has looked further still. In 2025 it confirmed a galaxy called MoM-z14, whose light set out only about 280 million years after the beginning. It’s tiny: most of its light comes from a patch less than five hundred light-years across, two hundred times smaller than the Milky Way.

There’s a puzzle here too, and the simulator was honest about it. Webb has found more of these early galaxies than anyone expected, and brighter. Small, yes, but already shining fiercely, so early that it’s hard to see how they made so many stars so fast. Perhaps stars formed more readily in the young universe; perhaps its stars were bigger and brighter; perhaps black holes at their hearts add some of the light. Nobody knows yet.

Collisions in a garden

“That’s a computer, and a telescope looking back,” said John. “If galaxies are built by bumping into each other, shouldn’t some still be at it?”

“Some are,” said Albert, who had come out with his coffee. “You have photographed them.”

John had. His pictures were on his laptop, and he set it on the dashboard beside the doughnut. Several of the galaxies in them, taken from his own garden in Oxfordshire with his own telescope, are in the middle of exactly what the simulator had shown.

The first was the Whirlpool, a great spiral seen face on, with a smaller galaxy, NGC 5195, sitting at the end of one of its arms.

The two have met. NGC 5195 swung past the Whirlpool within the last few hundred million years, perhaps more than once, and it’s behind the Whirlpool’s arm now: in John’s picture the arm’s dark lanes of dust cross in front of its bright core. The faint shell round them both is made of streams of old stars pulled out of both galaxies by the encounter, and the Whirlpool’s two strong, clean spiral arms are probably the encounter’s doing too. Simulations of a passage like this raise just such a pair of arms.

The second was M81 and M82, two galaxies of a group about 12 million light-years away. In ordinary light they look like separate galaxies minding their own business. Radio telescopes, which can see the hydrogen gas between them, show bridges of gas about a hundred thousand light-years long joining them, torn out by gravity. M82 swung past M81 about 220 million years ago, and the encounter set off a burst of star birth at M82’s heart so violent that the explosions of its young stars are blowing a wind of hot gas out of it, at more than a thousand kilometres a second, the red plume across its middle in John’s picture. Out in the debris of the encounter, young dwarf galaxies have formed: one of them, Holmberg IX, seems to have been born only about 130 million years ago.

The third was Stephan’s Quintet, a knot of galaxies about 290 million light-years away, half a degree from a big spiral in John’s picture. Four of its five galaxies are tangled together within about 260,000 light-years. (The fifth, the biggest and bluest, only happens to lie in front of them, six times nearer.) One of the four, NGC 7318B, is falling into the group for the first time, and as it ploughs through gas the group lost in earlier encounters, it’s driving a shock wave about 130,000 light-years long.

The simulator, asked for its view, was precise about it.

COLLISION IN PROGRESS: OBSERVED. 290 MILLION YEARS AGO

The last was Centaurus A, in a picture John took with a telescope in Chile, used over the internet. It’s a giant galaxy, its old stars glowing golden, with a dark, twisted lane of dust across its middle. Most of its stars are about 12 billion years old, but a fifth to a third of them formed only 2 to 4 billion years ago, and simulations show that a single great merger of two galaxies, about 2 billion years ago, can explain those young stars, its shape and the faint shells of stars round it. The dust lane, a disc of gas and young stars seen nearly edge on, seems to have settled more recently, after a gas-rich galaxy fell in.

“That’s three stages,” said Cal, looking from one picture to the next. “Stephan’s lot are crashing now. The Whirlpool and the other two are a few hundred million years after. Centaurus is two billion years after, when it’s all settled down into one.”

That’s exactly how astronomers read pictures like these. Nobody can watch two galaxies merge, because it takes hundreds of millions of years, but the sky is full of pairs caught at different stages, and put in order, they’re one story. In 1977 Alar Toomre picked out eleven galaxies from the catalogues and set them in just such a sequence, from two galaxies approaching to one merged galaxy with a few tangled tails. It’s still called the Toomre sequence.

Two swarms passing

What happens to the stars when two galaxies collide?

Almost nothing hits anything. Stars are tiny compared with the distances between them. If the Sun were the grain of sugar in Cal’s shirt pocket, half a millimetre across, the nearest star would be another grain about fifteen kilometres away, a good way down the mountain, and in between there’d be nothing at all. Two galaxies colliding are two swarms of such grains passing through each other, and the chance of any two grains meeting is negligible. The galaxies’ gas is another matter. Its clouds are vast, and they do crash into each other, and squeezed together they collapse and make new stars in a great burst, as M82’s did.

But if the stars don’t hit each other, what pulls out the long tails and bridges in John’s pictures?

The answer is tidal effects. In Einstein’s Happiest Thought, John and Cal found that the Moon raises tides on the Earth because it pulls the near side of the Earth a little harder than the middle, and the middle a little harder than the far side, so the oceans are stretched out along the line to the Moon. A galaxy passing close to another does the same thing to it, only on a far larger scale. The stars on the side nearest the passing galaxy are pulled harder than the centre, and the stars on the far side less hard, and the disc is stretched.

Teleport to the Relativity moonEinstein's Happiest ThoughtTidal effects: why a pull that differs from place to place stretches things

On its own, a stretch like that would only make the disc a little oval while the visitor went by. What turns a stretch into a tail is timing. A star in the disc is going round the centre, and so is the visitor, as it swings past. If the star happens to be going round the same way as the visitor’s swing, and at about the same rate, it keeps pace with the visitor for a while, and feels the same pull, in the same direction, all the time it’s keeping pace. It’s like pushing a child on a swing: small pushes, well timed, add up. Stars going round the other way meet the visitor head on, feel its pull for a moment, and are only jostled.

In 1972 Alar Toomre, at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and his brother Juri tested this with a computer. Their galaxies were as simple as galaxies could be: a heavy point at the centre of each, and rings of stars round it, each star pulled by the two heavy points but too light to pull anything itself. They sent one such galaxy past another and watched. Out came bridges and tails, very like the real ones in photographs, from gravity alone. They could even match particular galaxies: one of the pairs they rebuilt was the Whirlpool and its companion.

The figure below does what the Toomres did. The disc in the middle is a galaxy about the size of the Milky Way, seen face on, its stars going round anticlockwise. The visitor swings past it on the right. Choose a small companion, a third of the big galaxy’s weight, as the Whirlpool’s is, or two equal galaxies, each with a disc of its own. Then set the visitor’s path: turning the same way as the disc, tilted across it, or turning the opposite way. Play it, and watch the time since the closest pass: tails come out within a couple of hundred million years. The ledger counts how much of the disc has been flung out, into its tails. Try the opposite way round, and the disc is hardly disturbed. Make the visitor slower, and it doesn’t get away: it swings back, again and again, closer each time, until the two centres fall together and merge.

The Toomres’ simple galaxies couldn’t merge. Their heavy points swung past each other and away again for ever, like a comet past the Sun. Real galaxies do merge, because each sits inside a great cloud of dark matter, and when the clouds pass through each other, each drags on the other, as a ball rolling through deep sand is slowed by the sand. A pair of galaxies that pass once slows, swings back, passes again, closer, and falls together. (The figure stands in for that drag with a simple brake between the two centres.) When two spirals merge, their stars’ neat orbits are scrambled, and what settles out is a great round swarm of stars on orbits in every direction: a smooth, round galaxy, with no disc and no arms. In 1977 Alar Toomre suggested that many of the smooth round galaxies in the sky were made this way. Centaurus A looks like one, two billion years after.

So the answer to John’s question at breakfast is the scraps. A big galaxy isn’t made all at once. It’s gathered, from smaller pieces, over billions of years, and it goes on gathering.

“So the Milky Way,” said John, “is made of leftovers.”

“The best things usually are,” said Albert.

Galaxy

A great system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter, held together by its own gravity. The smallest have a few thousand stars; the Milky Way, our own, has between a hundred and four hundred billion, in a disc about a hundred thousand light-years across; giants can have trillions. Most of a galaxy’s mass is dark matter. Galaxies are built over billions of years from smaller ones, which fall together and merge, and most big ones have a giant black hole at the centre. There are hundreds of billions in the part of the universe we can see.

A black hole at every heart

There was one more thing in John’s picture of Centaurus A: up and to the left of the galaxy, a short chain of pink knots of glowing gas. Something is lighting that gas up, about thirty thousand light-years out from the galaxy’s centre, along the line of a jet fired from the black hole at its heart, about 55 million times the mass of the Sun.

In One Way In, John and Cal met the black hole at the centre of the galaxy M87, the first ever photographed, six and a half billion times the mass of the Sun, and the one at the centre of our own galaxy, about 4.3 million. M87 is in another of John’s pictures, Markarian’s Chain, the curve of galaxies at the heart of the Virgo Cluster, about 54 million light-years away: it’s the big round galaxy at the lower left. And another of his pictures, M106, which the second journey met as the galaxy whose distance is measured by pure geometry, shows what a black hole can do to its galaxy. Jets from the black hole at its centre, fired at an angle to its disc, drive shocks through the disc’s gas, which glows in hydrogen’s red light along two extra arms that don’t follow the ordinary spiral arms at all.

Nearly every big galaxy has a black hole at its heart, and there’s a curious rule about them. Astronomers who weighed dozens of them found that a black hole’s mass goes with the mass of the round central bulge of stars round it: the bigger the bulge, the bigger the black hole, over a range of thousands of times, the black hole always a few thousandths of the bulge’s mass, about half a per cent in the latest measurements. That’s surprising, because the black hole is tiny. Even M87’s, which is about the size of the Solar System, is far too small for its gravity to reach out and arrange stars thousands of light-years away.

So something must tie them together, and the likeliest answer is that they grow together. When galaxies merge, gas is driven into the centre of the new one, where some of it makes stars and some of it feeds the black hole. A black hole that’s feeding gives out so much energy that it heats the gas round it, and blows some of it away, and gas that’s hot or gone can’t make stars or feed the black hole any more. So each holds the other back, and they grow in step. How exactly is still argued over, but the rule is there in the measurements.

And not every galaxy follows it. The Triangulum Galaxy, M33, the third great galaxy of our own small family, in another of John’s pictures, is a pure disc with no central bulge at all, and when Hubble looked at its centre in 2001 it found that any black hole there must weigh less than about 1,500 Suns. No bulge, and no giant black hole. The rule ties black holes to bulges, and M33 has neither.

The brightest things in the universe

In Albert’s Bet, the settings of an experiment on La Palma were chosen by the light of two quasars, whose light had set out 7.78 and 12.21 billion years ago. Nobody stopped to say what a quasar was.

Teleport to the Quantum Mechanics moonAlbert's BetThe quasars whose light chose the settings of the Bell test on La Palma

In the 1950s, radio astronomers found that some of the radio waves from space came from tiny points on the sky. When the best telescopes looked at those points, a few turned out to be faint blue specks that looked exactly like stars, and their light, spread into its colours, showed lines nobody could recognise. In 1963 Maarten Schmidt, at Caltech, looked at the spectrum of one of them, called 3C 273, which he had taken with the great telescope on Palomar Mountain, and saw that its strange lines were hydrogen’s own familiar ones, the ones the first journey read in the stars, but stretched to longer wavelengths by sixteen per cent. That was a redshift (Further Apart Every Day). This “star” was far beyond our galaxy, so far that its light had been travelling for about two billion years.

That was the shock. For something to look as bright as a star from so far away, it must be giving out an enormous amount of light: 3C 273 shines like about four trillion Suns, about a hundred times as bright as the whole of the Milky Way. And its brightness changes over a few months, which means whatever makes the light must be small, no more than a few light-months across, because nothing can brighten all over faster than light can cross it to tell it to. The light of a hundred galaxies, from a space smaller than the gap between the Sun and its nearest star.

Only one thing can do that, and in 1964 two astronomers, Edwin Salpeter in America and Yakov Zel’dovich in Russia, separately suggested it: a giant black hole, eating. Gas falling towards a black hole doesn’t fall straight in. It swirls round it in a flat, spinning disc, the accretion disc of One Way In, rubbing against itself as it spirals inwards, until it’s heated to hundreds of thousands of degrees and shines fiercely. A quasar is the accretion disc of a giant black hole, fed so generously that it outshines the galaxy it sits in.

Quasar

The brilliant centre of a distant galaxy, where gas is spiralling into a giant black hole of millions or billions of times the Sun’s mass. The gas is heated as it falls, in a disc round the black hole, until it shines far brighter than all the galaxy’s stars together, from a region not much bigger than the Solar System. From far away a quasar looks like a faint star (the name is short for “quasi-stellar”, “like a star”). Quasars were commonest when the universe was a few billion years old; few shine today.

Albert, it turned out, was very pleased about quasars.

“Think what it means,” he said. “When the Sun turns hydrogen into helium, it sets free less than one per cent of the hydrogen’s rest energy. Seven tenths of one per cent. Gas falling into a black hole can give out a tenth of its rest energy, more than ten times as much. Short of matter meeting antimatter, a black hole is the most efficient engine in the universe.” He looked out at the mountain with the air of a man whose equation has been put to good use. “A few Suns’ worth of gas a year, and it outshines a hundred galaxies.”

For the mathematically inclinedHow a quasar shines

Quasars are a story about the past. The further back astronomers look, the more there are, up to about ten billion years ago, when the universe was about a quarter of its present age; before that they thin out again. Then, galaxies were young, full of gas and crashing into each other often, and the crashes drove gas to their centres, where the black holes ate it. Today the gas has mostly gone into stars, the collisions are rarer, and the giant black holes go hungry: M87’s and our own are quiet. You can see the quasars’ age in John’s pictures. His Whirlpool’s frame has about 190 known quasars in it, faint dots that look like stars, and the farthest that shows, near the bottom edge, is a quasar whose light set out about 12.2 billion years ago, as old as the light that chose the settings on La Palma.

The view from outside

“And our own galaxy?” said Cal. “Is it like any of these?”

“Let us go and look,” said Albert.

John asked the simulator to take them out of the Milky Way and turn round. The view on the screen rose, out of the crowd of stars round the Sun and up through the galaxy’s thin disc, which from inside had been a band of light across the sky, and kept rising, until the whole galaxy lay below them. The simulator, as usual, was careful to say what it was showing.

VIEW OF YOUR GALAXY FROM OUTSIDE: RECONSTRUCTED. NOBODY HAS EVER BEEN HERE

Nobody ever will be: to see the Milky Way like this, from well above it, you would have to be tens of thousands of light-years away. Every view of our galaxy from outside, in books and on screens, is a reconstruction, worked out from inside, and the simulator’s was too.

It was a spiral, like the Whirlpool, about a hundred thousand light-years across, with a short bar of old stars across its middle and the arms winding out from the bar’s ends. The Sun was a long way out from the centre, about 27,000 light-years, between two of the great arms, on a smaller spur of stars. At the very centre, hidden from the Sun by the dust of the disc, was the black hole of One Way In, 4.3 million times the mass of the Sun.

How can anyone know what it looks like, from inside? Mostly by radio. In Three Pairs of Sunglasses, John and Cal found that the cold hydrogen gas between the stars hums at a single radio note, a wavelength of 21 centimetres, given out whenever an atom’s electron flips over to point its little magnet the other way, and that those radio waves pass straight through the dust that hides most of the galaxy from ordinary light. Because the gas in different parts of the galaxy is going round at different speeds, its note arrives a little shifted, by the Doppler effect, and the shift says where along the line of sight each cloud of gas is. In 1958 Jan Oort, Frank Kerr and Gart Westerhout put together the measurements from radio telescopes in the Netherlands and in Australia, which between them could see the whole sky, and drew the first map of our galaxy’s arms. Three Pairs of Sunglasses promised that a later series would come back to that map; this is it. Since then, radio telescopes have measured the distances to star-forming clouds in the arms by pure geometry, and the European Space Agency’s Gaia satellite has measured the positions of nearly two billion stars, and the motions of most of them.

Everything in the disc goes round. The Sun, carrying the Earth with it, goes round the centre at about 230 kilometres a second, more than 800,000 kilometres an hour, and it takes about 230 million years to go round once. That’s a galactic year. One galactic year ago, the first dinosaurs were just appearing. The Sun has been round about twenty times since it formed.

Still under construction

The Milky Way is no exception to the rule of the scraps. It has its own collisions written in it, and they aren’t all over.

About ten billion years ago, it swallowed a galaxy a little more massive than the Small Magellanic Cloud, the Large Cloud’s smaller neighbour. In 2018 Amina Helmi and her colleagues in Groningen, using Gaia’s measurements, found that a great many of the old stars in the faint halo round our galaxy move together, on long stretched orbits, and share a chemistry unlike the galaxy’s own: they are the remains of that galaxy, which they named Gaia-Enceladus. Many of the halo’s stars came from it, and it puffed up the galaxy’s disc as it fell in.

Another collision is happening now. In 1994 Rodrigo Ibata, Gerry Gilmore and Mike Irwin found a small galaxy hidden almost directly behind the centre of ours, about 80,000 light-years from us, stretched out along the line towards the centre and being torn apart: the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy. Its stars have been pulled into a stream that wraps right round the Milky Way.

And one is coming. The Large Magellanic Cloud, the small galaxy in another of John’s pictures, about 162,000 light-years away, is moving fast, about 321 kilometres a second relative to our galaxy, and it’s probably on its first close pass. In 2019 Marius Cautun and his colleagues in Durham worked out that it’s heavier than had been thought, heavy enough to be losing speed to the Milky Way’s dark matter, and that it should fall in and merge with our galaxy in about two and a half billion years.

MILKY WAY: STILL UNDER CONSTRUCTION

Beyond them all is Andromeda, the nearest big galaxy, about two and a half million light-years away, bigger than ours, and coming towards us, as Albert pointed out in Further Apart Every Day. Allowing for the Sun’s own motion, it’s closing on the Milky Way at about a hundred kilometres a second. For decades the two were expected to collide in about four and a half billion years, but newer measurements of their sideways motion have made that less certain: one study in 2025 gave only about an even chance of their merging in the next ten billion years, and a reanalysis in 2026 put it at about nine in ten. It isn’t settled, and the seventeenth journey comes back to it.

The Milky Way is still making stars, too: about one and a half times the Sun’s mass of new stars every year, mostly in its arms.

The largest pattern of all

“Further out,” said Cal. “What’s the biggest thing there is?”

John asked the simulator to keep going. The Milky Way shrank to a speck, with Andromeda another speck beside it, and the two of them the biggest of a small family of galaxies, most of the rest tiny. The family shrank in turn, and became one of many small groups scattered round a great cluster of galaxies, Virgo’s, the one in John’s picture of Markarian’s Chain. And then, as the view went on widening, the galaxies stopped looking scattered. They lay along threads, and on thin walls, and the threads and walls ran together into knots where the great clusters sat, and between them all were huge empty spaces, a hundred million light-years across and more, with almost no galaxies in them at all.

Albert, who had gone back into the café to wash the mixing bowl, came out again with his sleeves rolled up and his hands covered in suds, and looked at the screen.

“There,” he said, holding up a handful of washing-up foam. “That is it.”

It is, nearly. Soap foam is bubbles, and the soap is all in the thin films between them, and along the edges where the films meet. In 1986 Valérie de Lapparent, Margaret Geller and John Huchra, at the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, measured the distances of about 1,100 galaxies in a thin slice of sky, plotted them, and found exactly that: galaxies on the surfaces of great bubbles, up to about 150 million light-years across, with almost nothing inside. They wrote that it was “like a slice through the suds in the kitchen sink”. Three years later Geller and Huchra found a single wall of galaxies stretching across their whole map, hundreds of millions of light-years long, which they called the Great Wall. Bigger surveys since have mapped millions of galaxies, and the foam is everywhere.

Cosmic web

The pattern galaxies make on the largest scales: long threads (filaments) and thin walls of galaxies, meeting in dense knots where the great clusters sit, round huge, nearly empty spaces (voids) a hundred million light-years or more across. The pattern is set by the dark matter, which gathered first along the slight unevenness of the young universe, with the ordinary matter falling in after it. Gas still flows along the threads into the galaxies, feeding them.

The web is the dark matter’s pattern, and the galaxies only light it up. When the simulator ran the universe forward from the ripples of the oldest light, as it had done that morning, the dark matter drained out of the slightly emptier places and gathered in the slightly fuller ones, and the emptier places grew emptier as the matter flowed away from them, until they were voids, with the matter piled up round their edges. The biggest such computer runs, like the Millennium Simulation of 2005, which followed ten billion particles of dark matter across a cube of space more than two billion light-years wide, grow a web just like the measured one. And the small galaxies falling together, which John had watched that morning, were falling along its threads.

The same history, read the other way, is on Nyx, where the universe in depth is drawn as a map, each object placed by how old its light is: John’s galaxies, his quasars and the clusters behind them, out to the oldest light at the edge.

Back to Teide

The sky over the plateau was clear that night, and John and Cal sat out on the terrace wall after the coaches had gone, while Albert brought out the last of the doughnuts from the third rolling.

The Milky Way rose over the dark shape of the mountain, a pale, uneven band from one side of the sky to the other, and for the first time John saw it as the simulator had shown it: not a band at all, but a disc, seen edge on from inside it. They were sitting a long way out in it, between two of its arms. Low over the south-western horizon, where the band was broadest and brightest, lay the direction of the centre, 27,000 light-years away, with its black hole hidden behind clouds of dust. The whole of it, and the plateau and the café and the doughnuts with it, was going round at 230 kilometres a second, and had been round twenty times.

Cal put two doughnuts side by side on his plate and pushed them together. They didn’t merge into a smooth round galaxy. He ate one, which he said was a reasonable model of a merger in its own way.

“It’s still making stars, you said,” he said. “Our galaxy. One and a half Suns a year.”

“Yes,” said John.

“Then where? Where are they being born?”

John looked along the band of the Milky Way, at the dark lanes running down its middle, which the simulator had told him were dust.

“I’ve photographed it,” he said slowly. “Haven’t I? The Eagle, Orion, the Horsehead. I always thought of them as nebulae. Clouds.”

“They are clouds,” said Albert. He gathered up the plates. “Bring them to breakfast, all of them. We will put them on the table and see what they say.”

Out in the car park, the simulator was still showing the galaxy from outside, the Sun a tiny marked point a long way from its centre, and the readout under it, which nobody had told to stop.
