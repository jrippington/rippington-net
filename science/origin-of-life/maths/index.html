The junkyard’s sums

Typing at random. The simulator’s line, ANOTHER DOUGHNUT PLEASE, has L=23L = 23L=23 places, each one of K=27K = 27K=27 keys (26 letters and a space). The number of different lines is

KL=2723≈8.3×1032,K^{L} = 27^{23} \approx 8.3 \times 10^{32},KL=2723≈8.3×1032,

about eight hundred thousand billion billion billion. Typed at random, each try has a chance of 1/27231/27^{23}1/2723 of being the right one, so on average it takes 272327^{23}2723 tries. At 10910^{9}109 tries a second that’s

8.3×1032109 s−1=8.3×1023 s≈2.6×1016 years,\frac{8.3 \times 10^{32}}{10^{9}\ \text{s}^{-1}} = 8.3 \times 10^{23}\ \text{s} \approx 2.6 \times 10^{16}\ \text{years},109 s−18.3×1032​=8.3×1023 s≈2.6×1016 years,

and against the universe’s age, about 13.8×10913.8 \times 10^{9}13.8×109 years (4.4×10174.4 \times 10^{17}4.4×1017 seconds), that’s about 1.9 million times as long. The readout’s sixty billion tries is a minute’s typing.

Fred’s number. Fred Hoyle and Chandra Wickramasinghe (Evolution from Space, 1981) put the chance of a cell’s roughly 2,000 enzymes each falling into a working order by chance at about one in 102010^{20}1020 apiece, so that all of them together come out at

(10−20)2000=10−40 000,\left(10^{-20}\right)^{2000} = 10^{-40\,000},(10−20)2000=10−40000,

one in a number with forty thousand noughts. The sum is right for what it describes: a modern cell’s proteins assembled at random, all at once. Nobody thinks that is what happened.

Keeping what works. In the second run, each generation makes 100 copies of the current line, each place in each copy changed to a random key with a chance of 1/231/231/23 (so about one change a copy), and keeps the copy with the most places right. A place already right is rarely lost, because among a hundred copies there’s nearly always one that kept every right place and changed a wrong one. Near the start, with www places wrong, a copy fixes one with a chance of about

w23×127,\frac{w}{23} \times \frac{1}{27},23w​×271​,

the chance of changing a wrong place, times the chance of hitting its right key. With a hundred copies, a wrong place is put right every generation or two at first, and more slowly at the end, when only a place or two is wrong and the right copy must hit exactly those. Run two thousand times, it takes a median of 59 generations (from about 42 to 88), about 5,900 copies in all, against 8.3×10328.3 \times 10^{32}8.3×1032. The article’s run, from a fixed seed, took 49.

Panspermia’s extra time. If life began somewhere else in the galaxy, the most extra time it could have had is the universe’s age before the Earth’s oceans formed, about 13.8−4.4≈9.413.8 - 4.4 \approx 9.413.8−4.4≈9.4 billion years, against the Earth’s own 4.4 billion: about three times as long in all. The code’s numbers

Why three letters. DNA has four letters, A, C, G and T. Words of one letter could name only 444 things, words of two only 42=164^2 = 1642=16, too few for twenty amino acids. Words of three give

43=64,4^3 = 64,43=64,

more than enough: 61 of them name amino acids and 3 mean stop. Most amino acids have several words: leucine, serine and arginine have six each; methionine and tryptophan only one.

How many dictionaries there could be. A dictionary gives each of the 64 words one of 21 meanings (20 amino acids and stop). Allowing any meaning for any word, there are

2164≈4.2×108421^{64} \approx 4.2 \times 10^{84}2164≈4.2×1084

ways to do it. Insisting that every one of the 21 meanings is used somewhere, the count, by including and excluding the dictionaries which leave out one meaning, then two, and so on, is

∑k=021(−1)k(21k)(21−k)64≈1.5×1084,\sum_{k=0}^{21} (-1)^k \binom{21}{k} (21 - k)^{64} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{84},k=0∑21​(−1)k(k21​)(21−k)64≈1.5×1084,

about ten followed by eighty-four noughts. Two separate beginnings, each choosing a dictionary by its own chemistry, would share one with a chance of about one in 108410^{84}1084, if every dictionary were as likely as any other. Some dictionaries are better than others: the one life uses is unusually good at limiting the damage when a letter is misread, and comparisons of it with random dictionaries (Freeland and Hurst, 1998) find it better than nearly all of them. But many work, as the recoded bacterium of 2019 showed, and none is forced by chemistry.

How long the oxygen would last

How much there is. The air holds about 1.2×10181.2 \times 10^{18}1.2×1018 kilograms of oxygen, which at 32 grams a mole is about

N≈3.7×1019 moles.N \approx 3.7 \times 10^{19}\ \text{moles}.N≈3.7×1019 moles.

The slow sinks. Without life, oxygen is taken out of the air mainly as fresh rock is worn away and rusts (iron, and the sulphur in fool’s gold), and by gases from volcanoes. Estimates of these sinks run to about 1.51.51.5 to 2×10132 \times 10^{13}2×1013 moles a year (Catling and Zahnle, 2003). If nothing replaced the oxygen, the amount left would fall as

N(t)=N0 e−t/τ,τ=Nsinks≈3.7×10191.8×1013 yr−1≈2×106 years,N(t) = N_0\, e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{N}{\text{sinks}} \approx \frac{3.7 \times 10^{19}}{1.8 \times 10^{13}\ \text{yr}^{-1}} \approx 2 \times 10^{6}\ \text{years},N(t)=N0​e−t/τ,τ=sinksN​≈1.8×1013 yr−13.7×1019​≈2×106 years,

and other estimates of the sinks give up to about 8 million years (Daines and colleagues, 2017). After one τ\tauτ about 37 per cent would be left; after five, under 1 per cent. That is the article’s “a few million years”.

The fast loop. Living things make and use oxygen far faster. The world’s plants, algae and cyanobacteria fix about 105 billion tonnes of carbon a year beyond what they burn themselves, land and sea together, which is about

105×1015 g12 g mol−1≈8.8×1015 moles a year\frac{105 \times 10^{15}\ \text{g}}{12\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} \approx 8.8 \times 10^{15}\ \text{moles a year}12 g mol−1105×1015 g​≈8.8×1015 moles a year

of carbon, with a mole of oxygen let out for each mole of carbon fixed. Dividing,

3.7×10198.8×1015 yr−1≈4,200 years:\frac{3.7 \times 10^{19}}{8.8 \times 10^{15}\ \text{yr}^{-1}} \approx 4{,}200\ \text{years}:8.8×1015 yr−13.7×1019​≈4,200 years:

the air’s oxygen passes through living things every few thousand years, made by photosynthesis and used again, nearly all of it, by everything that breathes and rots.

The ozone layer. Ozone is measured in Dobson units, each the thickness, in hundredths of a millimetre, that the ozone over a place would make if brought down and squeezed to the pressure and temperature at the ground. A typical column is about 300 Dobson units:

300×0.01 mm=3 mm.300 \times 0.01\ \text{mm} = 3\ \text{mm}.300×0.01 mm=3 mm.
