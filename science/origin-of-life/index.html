By evening they have read the code every living thing shares, and found out who filled the sky with oxygen.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 14 of 18

A beetle in a jar

Charles came up from the car park a little after eight, before the first coffee had been poured, stopping every few steps to look at something on the ground. He was a tall, stooping man with a long white beard, eyebrows like two small hedges and an overcoat from another century, and at the terrace wall he bent down, cupped something in his hands and tipped it into a glass jar from his pocket. Then he came in and shook Albert’s hand for a long time.

“A beetle,” he said, as if apologising. “I have never been able to help it.”

John and Cal knew who he must be. The evening before, at the end of the thirteenth journey, a striped stone on the terrace wall had told them how the great striped iron rocks gave way to the first red, rusted land, about 2.4 billion years ago: the air had filled with oxygen, and nothing in the rocks could have made it. Something had been making it. And Albert had mentioned an old friend, due for breakfast, who had been wondering about that sort of thing since 1871.

Charles sat down with them and looked at the beetle again, through a small lens on a cord round his neck.

“Albert tells me,” he said, “that your planet filled its air with oxygen, and you can’t find out who did it.”

“Something did,” said Cal. “Nothing in the rocks makes it.”

“Then I should think it was something alive.” He let the lens drop. “Which only moves the question. Something alive has to have come from somewhere.”

He had written as much to his friend Joseph Hooker, the botanist, on 1 February 1871. People were saying that all the conditions for life to begin must still be present, so why didn’t it begin again? But if, he wrote, and it was a very big if, the first stuff of life had formed in “some warm little pond”, with ammonia and phosphorus salts, light, heat and electricity, then today it would be eaten the moment it appeared. On a planet with nothing living on it yet, it would have been left alone.

“That was a hundred and fifty-five years ago,” said John. “Surely somebody knows by now.”

They went out to The Van and asked. John set the simulator to show them life beginning on the Earth, and it showed a grey shore under a hazy orange sky, black rock, steaming pools and a flat sea, and then nothing more at all.

ORIGIN OF LIFE: SPECULATION. CONFIDENCE: NONE

“An honest machine,” said Charles, delighted. “I should have liked one.”

Nobody knows how life began, and this journey keeps to that: the first step is SPECULATION, worked out from chemistry and argued over. But a great deal is known about when, and what came after, written in the rocks and in every living cell.

“Before you ask how it began,” said Albert, from the doorway, “go and look at it. The oldest kind of living thing there is, still doing what it did then.” He glanced at the clock over the counter. “It is on the other side of the world. It will be teatime there.”

Teatime in Western Australia

DESTINATION: SHARK BAY, WESTERN AUSTRALIA. LOCAL TIME: AFTERNOON TEA

The Van came down first in the Pilbara, in Australia’s far north-west, among dry red hills of spinifex grass. Near a mining town called Marble Bar are the grey rocks of the Dresser Formation, and in them, under Charles’s lens, were small domes and wrinkles, layer laid over thin layer, like the skin on boiled milk set into stone.

THESE ROCKS: 3,480 MILLION YEARS OLD. RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH. WHO LAID THE LAYERS: ARGUED ABOUT

They were laid down round hot springs on land, in an old volcano’s crater: in 2017 a team led by Tara Djokic found in them geyserite, a mineral which forms only where hot springs bubble up on land. The layered domes look exactly like mounds which mats of microbes build today, trapping sand and growing up through it towards the light. Whether life built these, or chemistry which happens to leave the same shapes, has been argued for decades, and most people who study them think they’re the real thing.

A second kind of evidence needs no shapes at all, and it comes from the third journey’s isotopes. Carbon comes in two stable kinds: ordinary carbon, and a slightly heavier kind with one neutron more, about one atom in ninety. Living things build themselves from carbon taken out of the air or the water, and the chemistry they grab it with works a little faster on the lighter kind, so carbon which has been through life ends up with two or three per cent less of the heavy kind than carbon which hasn’t. It lasts, in a lump of coal and in specks of carbon in rocks more than three and a half billion years old. There was life on the Earth within about a billion years of its making, and very likely sooner.

“So it didn’t hang about,” said Cal.

“No,” said Charles. “I had always supposed it needed an enormous time. It seems it needed the time afterwards.”

On the way south The Van passed low over the gorges of Karijini, whose walls were striped red and grey and red again, band on band: the striped iron rocks of the evening before, about two and a half billion years old. Nobody said anything about them yet.

Hamelin Pool is a bay inside Shark Bay, cut off from the ocean by banks of seagrass, so that the sun evaporates its water faster than the tides replace it. It’s about twice as salty as the open sea, too salty for most of the snails which would otherwise graze anything soft growing on its floor. They walked out along a boardwalk in the low gold light of a late Australian afternoon, and in the shallows on either side were hundreds of dark, rounded lumps, like loaves or cushions or the tops of mushrooms.

They were alive. Each was built by a thin, slimy mat of microbes on its surface, among them the blue-green microbes called cyanobacteria. The mat traps grains of sand and lime and glues them down, then grows up through them towards the light, layer by layer, a few centimetres a century; some of these have been growing for two or three thousand years. They are called stromatolites, and their layers are the Pilbara’s layers. For about three billion years, mounds like these were the grandest living things on the planet.

Charles knelt on the boardwalk, which took some time, and looked down into the clear water with his lens.

“There,” he said.

On the dark surface of the nearest mound, glinting in the low sun, were tiny silver bubbles. Every so often one let go and rose, wobbling, to the surface.

“They’re breathing out,” said Cal.

“They are,” said Charles. “And I am told that what they breathe out is oxygen.”

It seemed absurd that a slime on a stone could have changed the whole sky.

Charles stood up slowly.

“I was on this coast once,” he said, “a great deal further south, a long time ago. I wrote that I left it without sorrow or regret.” He looked down at the mounds. “I should like to take that back.”

What being alive takes

Over lunch at the café, Cal asked the question everyone had been holding.

“How does anything start being alive? Those mats are alive. A rock isn’t. Where’s the line?”

Every living thing is made mostly of six elements: the grain of sugar’s three, carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with nitrogen, and a little phosphorus and sulphur. All but the hydrogen were made in stars, as the eleventh journey found. Life also needs water, to dissolve things and carry them about, and energy: sunlight, lightning, or the heat and chemistry of volcanoes. The young Earth had all of these. The question is how they came to be arranged into something alive.

And what is alive? Charles’s answer, worked out over twenty years and published in 1859, is the one biologists still use. A living thing makes copies of itself. The copies are nearly, but not quite, perfect, so they differ a little. Some differences help a copy to survive and make more copies, and those spread, because there are more of them every generation; differences which hurt die out. Nobody has to choose. It happens by itself, to anything which copies itself imperfectly in a world which can’t support every copy. He called it natural selection. Alfred Russel Wallace worked out the same idea on his own, and the two announced it together in 1858.

“Mr Wallace thought of it in a fever, in the Malay islands,” said Charles. “I took rather longer.”

So Cal’s line lies between chemistry, where molecules simply react, and the first thing able to copy itself. Once there was a copier, Charles’s idea could take over; before, it couldn’t. That gap is the part nobody has crossed.

Sparks in a flask

The first person to try crossing it with an experiment was a student of twenty-two.

In the autumn of 1952, at the University of Chicago, Stanley Miller built a loop of glass to stand in for the young Earth. A small flask of water, heated from below, was its sea. Steam rose from it into a big five-litre flask holding the gases then thought to make up the young Earth’s air: methane, ammonia and hydrogen. In the big flask were two metal points, and between them Miller ran a spark, day and night, as lightning. Below, a jacket of cold water turned the steam back to rain, which ran down into a U-bend and back to the sea, carrying whatever the sparks had made.

The simulator rebuilt it in The Van from his notes.

MILLER’S FLASK, 1952: RECONSTRUCTED. FROM HIS NOTES

After a day the water in the U-bend had turned pink. By the end of a week it was deep red and cloudy, and in it were amino acids, glycine, alanine and others, five kinds in all. Amino acids are the pieces every living thing builds its proteins from, and proteins are the long chains, folded into shapes, which do nearly all the work in a living cell. Miller published it, in a paper less than two pages long, in May 1953.

“Pieces of life,” said John. “Out of gas and a spark.”

“Pieces,” said Charles. “Not life. But pieces.”

Miller kept his samples, and after his death in 2007 they were found in his boxes. Measured again in 2008, with far more sensitive instruments, the vials from a version with a jet of steam blowing into the sparks, like a volcano’s plume in a thunderstorm, held twenty-two kinds of amino acid.

The figure below is Miller’s loop. Play the week, or move through it by hand, and watch new molecules form at the spark, rain down and gather in the U-bend as the water darkens; then switch to the steam-jet version, as its vials were measured in 2008.

There’s an honest catch. The young Earth’s air, breathed out by the thirteenth journey’s volcanoes, was probably mostly carbon dioxide and nitrogen, not Miller’s mix, and in air like that, sparks make far fewer amino acids, though the right minerals in the water bring the numbers back up.

And the building blocks were arriving from space anyway. On 28 September 1969 a meteorite broke up over Murchison, in Victoria, in the south-east of Australia, and its stones held amino acids, many of kinds life doesn’t use, so they couldn’t be contamination from the ground. In 2023 the spacecraft OSIRIS-REx brought home dust from an asteroid, Bennu, sealed against the Earth’s air, and in 2025 scientists reported finding in it fourteen of the twenty amino acids life uses, and all five of the chemical letters of life’s genetic molecules. The pieces of life make themselves easily, almost wherever there’s carbon, water and energy. Putting them together into something which copies itself is the hard part.

Down in the dark

In February 1977 geologists in a small submarine, Alvin, went two and a half kilometres down to the Pacific floor near the Galápagos Islands, looking for hot springs where the sea floor pulls apart. They found them, and round them, in water which had never seen sunlight, life nobody had expected: clams as long as a forearm, crabs and tube worms, all living on bacteria which drew their energy from the chemistry of the hot water.

In 2000 another kind was found in the middle of the Atlantic, Lost City: a forest of pale stone chimneys, the tallest about sixty metres, where seawater soaks into the rock, reacts with it and comes back up warm, rich in hydrogen and as alkaline as soapy water. Some biologists think life began in vents like these. Their thin mineral walls separate two very different waters, like the two sides of a battery, and every cell alive today runs on a battery of just that kind, pumping charged particles across a thin skin and using their flow back to drive its chemistry.

Others think Charles’s pond was nearer the mark, or a hot spring on land like the Pilbara’s: a pool which dries in the sun and fills again with rain, concentrating its chemicals each time, the way a stock reduces in a pan.

WHERE LIFE BEGAN: SPECULATION. CANDIDATES: AT LEAST TWO

“So I may have been right after all,” said Charles.

“Or wrong,” said Albert.

“Or wrong.” Charles seemed equally pleased with both.

A tornado in a junkyard

Fred arrived at about three, for tea.

He came in out of the afternoon in the heavy coat he never needed, saw the beetle in the jar and the old man with the lens, and stopped.

“Albert,” he said, “you didn’t tell me you had company.”

It was his fourth visit, after the second journey, the fourth and the eleventh. He took his tea as he took everything, strong, and he and Charles looked each other over like two old dogs in a park, and liked what they saw.

“You’re the one who thinks it all started in a puddle,” said Fred.

“I thought it might have,” said Charles. “I’m told I may have been wrong.”

“You were.” Fred stirred his tea. “It didn’t start here at all.”

He had argued it for years with his colleague Chandra Wickramasinghe. The dust between the stars, they said, isn’t just rock and ice but rich in carbon chemistry: John and Cal had seen as much in the eleventh journey, where carbon’s football-shaped molecules turned up in the Iris Nebula. Life started out there, they said, and reached the Earth in comets and dust. The idea is called panspermia, “seeds everywhere”, and it’s older than Fred: in 1871, the year of Charles’s letter, Lord Kelvin suggested that life might have reached the Earth on meteorites.

Teleport to NyxThe Iris NebulaThe Iris Nebula, where carbon’s football molecules were first found between the stars

“Why not here?” said Cal.

“Because it’s impossible,” said Fred, “here or anywhere small. A living cell is thousands of different proteins, each a chain of hundreds of amino acids, each one of twenty kinds, in exactly the right order, or it doesn’t work. Work out the chance of shaking them into the right order by luck. We did. It’s one in a number with forty thousand noughts on the end. That’s a tornado sweeping through a junkyard and leaving a jumbo jet behind it, ready to fly.”

He put it that way in the early 1980s. The arithmetic is right: if a cell had to be assembled at random, all at once, it would never happen, here or anywhere.

“Show us,” said John, and Cal, who had been waiting for this, set the simulator going. He asked it for something much simpler than a jumbo jet: the twenty-three letters and spaces of ANOTHER DOUGHNUT PLEASE, typed by hitting twenty-seven keys at random, a billion tries a second.

TRIES: 60,000,000,000. DOUGHNUTS ORDERED: 0

There are about eight hundred thousand billion billion billion possible lines, and at a billion tries a second the simulator expected to finish in about two million times the present age of the universe.

“There you are,” said Fred.

“Now try it the other way,” said Albert.

This time the simulator started from a random line, made a hundred copies of it with a letter or so changed at random in each, kept whichever copy was closest to the target, and copied that: it kept what worked and threw away what didn’t.

GENERATION 1: N ATFPEZRNQVGUZXZGQUCME

GENERATION 20: ANOTHZR DPUEHUUXZPQEASE

GENERATION 49: ANOTHER DOUGHNUT PLEASE

Albert brought him one.

“That’s cheating,” said Fred at once. “You told it what you wanted. Nature doesn’t know it’s looking for a doughnut.”

He was right: nothing in nature aims at anything. But Charles leaned forward.

“It doesn’t need to,” he said. “It keeps whatever copies itself more. That’s all. Your tornado has to build the whole aeroplane at once, because nothing it does today is kept for tomorrow. Give it one thing which copies itself, and every step is kept, and the next starts from there.”

That’s the first answer to the junkyard: once anything copied itself, nothing had to be assembled at random again, because selection kept what worked and built on it.

The second is that molecules aren’t junk. Scrap metal has no tendency to arrange itself into anything; atoms do. They join in particular ways, held by the bonds of Room for Only Two, and the molecules they make fall into shapes by themselves. Salt from seawater grows into perfect cubes, and water freezing in a cloud grows six-armed snowflakes; nobody assembles either. The amino acids in Miller’s flask and Bennu’s dust made themselves. The starting point was never a junkyard. It was chemistry, which builds things, given energy and time.

“And the first copier?” said Fred. “Where did that come from?”

“Nobody knows,” said Albert.

“So I’m right.”

“About that much, yes.”

The third answer is about Fred’s way out. Starting life somewhere else wouldn’t make it likelier, since the ingredients and the chemistry out there are the same; it only moves the question. More time would help, but the universe is only about three times as old as the Earth, and against forty thousand noughts that’s nothing. Fred’s steady universe, with no beginning, would have had all the time there ever was, but the oldest light of the fourth journey had done for it.

“I’d noticed,” said Fred.

So if Fred’s number were right, life couldn’t exist anywhere, and it does: the number measures the chance of a modern cell falling together all at once, which nobody thinks happened. But Fred was right that the ingredients are made in space and fall on the Earth, as Murchison and Bennu showed. Of anything living arriving that way, nobody has found the slightest sign.

BUILDING BLOCKS FROM SPACE: OBSERVED. LIFE FROM SPACE: SPECULATION

Fred looked at it for a while.

“I’ll take observed,” he said, “for now.”

He and Charles got on wonderfully after that, as two contrary men will who have each been told, for most of their lives, that they were wrong. Fred said the magazines had drawn Charles as an ape. Charles said they had, several times, and that he had thought the beard rather well done. Albert, at the counter, looked delighted.

The first copier

Fred’s question is the right one, and it’s the hardest.

In a cell today, the instructions are kept in DNA, and proteins do the work, building more proteins and copying the DNA. But the proteins are made from the DNA’s instructions, and the DNA can only be copied by the proteins. Neither can come first: a chicken and an egg, made of molecules.

DNA

The long molecule every living cell keeps its instructions in. It’s two strands twisted round each other into a double helix, each a chain of four chemical letters, A, C, G and T, on a backbone of sugar. The letters on one strand pair with those on the other, A only with T and C only with G, so pulling the strands apart and building a new partner for each makes two copies, which is how a cell copies its instructions before it divides. Read three letters at a time, a stretch of DNA spells out the order in which to join amino acids into a protein. Its name is short for deoxyribonucleic acid.

The likeliest answer is a third molecule every cell still has: RNA, a close cousin of DNA, a chain of chemical letters on a backbone. It can carry instructions, as DNA does, and in 1982 and 1983 Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman found that some RNA molecules also do work, as proteins do, cutting and joining other molecules. In 1986 Walter Gilbert named the idea the RNA world: an early Earth where RNA did both jobs, carrying instructions and copying itself, before DNA and proteins took them over.

Cal’s hand had gone to his shirt pocket, where the grain of sugar was folded in its napkin.

“What’s the backbone made of?”

“Sugar,” said Albert.

RNA’s backbone is a chain of a sugar called ribose, joined by phosphorus and oxygen; DNA’s is a sugar with one oxygen atom fewer. Every gene in every living thing hangs on a chain of sugar.

The RNA world has evidence of its own. In every cell’s protein factory, the ribosome, the part which joins each amino acid to the next turned out in 2000 to be made not of protein but of RNA: a working fossil of the world before proteins. In 2009 Tracey Lincoln and Gerald Joyce made a pair of RNA molecules which build copies of each other out of pieces, over and over, with no proteins and no cells: not alive, but copying. A copier also needs a wall, and chemistry is generous again: soap-like molecules in water gather by themselves into tiny hollow balls, which grow and divide as more are added. Nobody has put it all together and watched something come alive, but every step has been seen working on its own.

FIRST COPIER: SPECULATION. EACH STEP: OBSERVED IN A LABORATORY

At some point the jobs were split. DNA, sturdier than RNA, took over keeping the instructions, and proteins, far more varied, took over the work, with RNA carrying messages between them. DNA’s shape was worked out in Cambridge by James Watson and Francis Crick, from X-ray photographs taken in London by Rosalind Franklin, and published on 25 April 1953, three weeks before Miller’s paper.

One code

The way DNA is read is called the genetic code, and it’s a dictionary of sixty-four words. Each word is three letters long, and with four letters to choose from in each place there are four times four times four of them. Sixty-one words each mean one of twenty amino acids, several words to most of them, and three mean “stop”. A cell reads a gene from its start, three letters at a time, adding one amino acid for each word, until it reaches a stop.

For the mathematically inclinedThe code’s numbers

And the dictionary is the same in every living thing ever examined: the mats at Hamelin Pool, the beetle in Charles’s jar, a mushroom, a whale, an oak and John. TTT means the amino acid phenylalanine in all of them. There are a few tiny dialects, a word or two read differently in some microbes and in the small power stations inside John’s cells, but the language is one language.

You can see it in any pharmacy. Insulin, which diabetics need, is a small human protein, and since 1978 people have been putting the instructions for it into bacteria, which read them in their own code and make human insulin. Much of the world’s supply is made that way now, in bacteria and in baker’s yeast, a fungus, the mushroom’s cousin.

The figure below reads the start of one of insulin’s two chains, thirty words long, as a bacterium, a yeast and John’s own cells read it, with the whole dictionary under it; as each word is read, its entry lights up and its amino acid joins the chain. Try each reader, then a made-up dictionary: the same words and the same twenty amino acids, shuffled.

Why is the code the same everywhere? You might suppose it has to be: that chemistry forces TTT to mean phenylalanine, as it forces salt into cubes. It doesn’t. Nothing about the letters TTT grabs hold of phenylalanine. The link is made by small adaptor molecules, each carrying one amino acid at one end and reading one word with the other, loaded by proteins. The dictionary is held in those molecules, and it can be changed. In 2019 a team in Cambridge led by Jason Chin rewrote the whole of a bacterium’s DNA to do without three of its sixty-four words, eighteen thousand changes in all, and the bacterium lived, grew and divided. So countless other dictionaries would work: there are about ten followed by eighty-four noughts ways to share sixty-four words among twenty amino acids and a stop.

Clocks are a good comparison. Every clock in the world turns the same way, not because a clock couldn’t turn the other, but because the first clocks copied sundials, whose shadows go round that way in the northern lands where clocks were invented, and every clock since has copied the ones before. When a thing could be done any number of ways and is done one way everywhere, it was copied.

Follow it through:

  • Every living thing reads its genes with the same dictionary.
  • The dictionary isn’t forced by chemistry: others work, as the bacterium of 2019 showed.
  • With about ten followed by eighty-four noughts dictionaries to choose from, separate beginnings would never land on the same one.
  • So every living thing must have inherited it, from an ancestor which already had it.

The only way out would be that this code is the only one possible, and it isn’t. So all life on the Earth is one family, from one ancestor: the mats at Shark Bay, the beetle, the mushroom, the whale and John. Comparing the genes all living things share, letter by letter, draws the same single family tree.

Common ancestor

A living thing from which two or more others are descended. Any two living things on the Earth share one somewhere back in time: John and a chimpanzee, a few million years ago; John and a mushroom, more than a billion; John and a bacterium, further back still. The last universal common ancestor is the population of cells from which everything alive today descends. It wasn’t the first life: others lived before it, and beside it, and left no descendants.

One study in 2024, by Edmund Moody and his colleagues, found that it probably lived about 4.2 billion years ago, only a few hundred million years after the Earth was made, with perhaps 2,600 proteins. That’s one estimate, with wide uncertainties, and others put it later and simpler. But all agree it was already a cell, with DNA, proteins, the code and the battery across its skin, so a great deal had happened before it.

Charles took one of Albert’s napkins and drew a little branching tree: one line at the bottom, dividing and dividing again as it rose, some branches ending, others spreading. Above it he wrote two words.

I THINK

“I drew one of those in a notebook in 1837,” he said. “Nobody knew about the code. I only guessed the tree.”

Living on light

Then it was time for the bubbles.

The microbes in the mats at Hamelin Pool live on sunlight, as plants do.

Photosynthesis

How plants, algae and some microbes make sugar out of carbon dioxide and water, using the energy of sunlight. A green pigment, chlorophyll, catches red and blue light; the light’s energy is used to pull water molecules apart, and stored in the chemical bonds of new sugar, built from the carbon of the carbon dioxide and the hydrogen of the water. The water’s oxygen is left over, and let out as waste. Burning the sugar, or eating it, sets the energy free again, and turns it back into carbon dioxide and water. Nearly all the oxygen in the air, and nearly all the energy in everything anyone eats, came from it.

The oxygen comes from the water, not the carbon dioxide: in 1941 Samuel Ruben and Martin Kamen gave plants water made with a heavy kind of oxygen atom, and found the heavy oxygen in the gas the plants let out.

Cyanobacteria were the first to do it, and at first their oxygen went nowhere. Oxygen is fiercely reactive, which is why things burn in it and iron rusts in it, and the young Earth was full of things for it to react with. The sea held dissolved iron, brought up by hot springs on the ocean floor, and as the oxygen met it, the iron rusted and fell to the bottom as a fine red sediment; when the oxygen ran short, grey silica settled instead, then red iron again. Those are the bands at Karijini, the striped iron rocks of the thirteenth journey, and most of the world’s iron ore comes from them. The volcanoes’ gases soaked up more. For hundreds of millions of years, every bubble the mats let out was used up almost at once.

Then, about 2.4 billion years ago, the sinks could no longer keep up, and oxygen began to build up in the air. Geologists call it the Great Oxidation, and the rocks record it in three ways. In old river gravels, rounded grains of fool’s gold, tumbled smooth by the water, are common before it and vanish after it, because fool’s gold rusts away in air with oxygen in it. Rocks laid down on land after it rust red, as the thirteenth journey’s red land did, and none did before. And the great striped iron rocks grow rare as the sea’s iron is used up, until by about 1.8 billion years ago they are gone.

High in the new air, the Sun’s ultraviolet light broke some of the oxygen into single atoms, which joined other oxygen molecules to make ozone, oxygen in threes, and ozone soaks up the Sun’s harshest ultraviolet, which breaks DNA. It’s a thinner shield than it sounds: all the ozone in the air, squeezed to the pressure at the ground, would make a layer about three millimetres thick.

The figure below is the air’s oxygen through the Earth’s history, the band as wide as the evidence is uncertain, on a scale where each step up is ten times the one below. Above it are the rocks. Move through time, or let it play, and watch how long the air stayed empty, and how the oxygen came in steps.

And the oxygen doesn’t last. Left alone, it slowly reacts with fresh rock as the land wears away, and with the gases from volcanoes, and in a few million years it would all be locked away in rust. The air is full of it only because life makes more, every day, as fast as it’s taken away; the oxygen in the café passes through living things every few thousand years. Nothing else on the Earth could have filled the sky with it, and nothing else keeps it there. Every breath John and Cal had taken that day had been let out, as waste, by a plant or a microbe.

“So the bubbles at Shark Bay,” said John, “are the air in here.”

“Some of it,” said Albert. “The rest is from the trees.”

From one cell to Megalosaurus

The simulator ran the rest of the story, faster and faster, while the light went gold over the plateau.

For a long time after the oxygen came, life stayed small. Then something odd happened, as the biologist Lynn Margulis argued in 1967, in a paper fifteen journals had turned down. Inside every animal’s, plant’s and fungus’s cells are small power stations which burn food with oxygen, with their own DNA and ribosomes, dividing by themselves like bacteria. They were bacteria. Once, one cell swallowed another, and instead of digesting it, kept it, and the two have lived together ever since. The green parts of a leaf, where photosynthesis happens, are the same: once cyanobacteria, swallowed and kept. The green of every leaf on the plateau descends from mats like those at Hamelin Pool. The oldest fossils everyone agrees are of these complex cells are about 1.6 billion years old; older ones are argued about.

Twice at least, the whole Earth very nearly froze: perhaps soon after the Great Oxidation, when the new oxygen destroyed much of the methane keeping the young Earth warm, and again between about 717 and 635 million years ago, when ice reached the tropics and may have covered the sea almost from pole to pole. Geologists call them the Snowball Earths. Each ended when the volcanoes’ gases, with no rain to wash them out of the air, built up until the planet warmed and the ice let go.

After the last of them the oxygen rose a second time, and living things grew large: soft, flat, fronded things on the sea floor, about 570 million years ago. A fifteen-year-old, Tina Negus, found one of them in Leicestershire in 1956, and was told there could be no fossils in rocks so old.

From about 538 million years ago came the Cambrian, when within a few tens of millions of years the sea filled with animals with shells, legs, jaws and eyes. Plants reached the land about 470 million years ago, under the ozone shield, and animals followed them. Five times since the Cambrian most of the life on the Earth has been wiped out; after the worst, about 252 million years ago, the dinosaurs rose.

The simulator slowed at last, in a forest of tree ferns about 166 million years ago, in what is now Oxfordshire, and something large walked out of it on two legs.

“I’ve seen the Megalosaurus,” said John. “In the Natural History Museum, in Oxford.”

“Megalosaurus,” said Charles. “Buckland’s.”

It was the first dinosaur given a scientific name. In 1824 William Buckland, at Oxford, described a jaw bristling with curved, saw-edged teeth, and some bones from the slate quarries at Stonesfield, and called their owner Megalosaurus, the “great lizard”. The word “dinosaur” came eighteen years later. Buckland’s jaw is in Oxford still, in that museum.

The dinosaurs lasted until about 66 million years ago, when an asteroid about ten kilometres across hit the sea off what is now Mexico. In 1980 the physicist Luis Alvarez, his son Walter, a geologist, and two chemists found that a thin layer of clay laid down at that moment, near Gubbio in Italy and then all over the world, is full of iridium, rare in the Earth’s crust and common in asteroids; in 1991 the crater was found, about 180 kilometres across, under the Yucatán peninsula. Three quarters of all species died. Not quite all the dinosaurs did: some small, feathered ones survived, and their descendants are the birds.

A small bird was hopping along the terrace wall outside, looking for crumbs.

“So that’s a dinosaur,” said Cal.

“Strictly speaking,” said Charles, “yes.”

In the space the dinosaurs left, the mammals spread out. The oldest fossils of people like John and Cal, from Jebel Irhoud, in Morocco, are about 300,000 years old.

The Earth’s year

In 1977 the astronomer Carl Sagan, in The Dragons of Eden, squeezed the whole history of the universe into one calendar year. Do the same with the Earth’s: let it be made at midnight as the year begins, and let now be midnight at the end of 31 December. Then each day is about twelve million years, each hour about half a million, and each second about 144 years.

On that calendar the Moon is made on 4 January. The Pilbara’s signs of life are from late March, and the air fills with oxygen in the third week of June, just after midsummer. Through the whole summer and most of the autumn, nearly every living thing is too small to see on its own. The Snowball Earth comes in the first days of November, and the Cambrian on 18 November. Megalosaurus walks through Oxfordshire on 18 December, and the asteroid falls on 26 December, at about twenty to five in the afternoon. People like John and Cal appear at about twenty-five past eleven on the night of 31 December. All of written history fits in the last thirty-five seconds, and Buckland named Megalosaurus about a second and a half before midnight.

“Twenty-five past eleven,” said Cal. “On the last night.”

“And mats like the ones at Shark Bay,” said John, “have been at it since March.”

Back to Teide

By evening Fred had decided to go, and had offered Charles a lift down the mountain, and Charles, who had never been driven by Fred, had accepted. They stood by the door, the beetle in its jar in Charles’s pocket, to be let go, he said, exactly where he had found it.

“There is grandeur in it,” said Charles. He was looking out at the plateau: the old lava going gold, the dark pines far down the slopes, the bird on the wall, and the light beyond them all. “All this, from so simple a beginning. And all the while the planet going round and round, by the fixed law of gravity.”

“Fixed,” said Albert, mildly.

Charles looked at him.

“Nearly,” said Albert.

Fred laughed all the way to his car. Then they were gone, the exhaust rattling down the road to the first bend, and the plateau was quiet.

John and Cal sat on the terrace wall with Albert while the sun went down in the west. Everything they could see was alive, or made by something alive, or breathed out by it: the pines below, the lichen on the lava, the bird, the air itself. Four billion years ago the planet had been bare rock and water under an orange sky, and all anyone knew for certain about how it came alive was that it had happened once, and that everything since was one family.

The stars came out over Teide, one after another, and then in their thousands. Cal looked up at them for a long time.

“If the Sun’s disc made planets,” he said slowly, “then every star’s disc should have. We saw HL Tauri’s doing it. So every one of those should have planets of its own.” He waved at all of them. “Where are they?”

“You will not see them,” said Albert. “A planet beside its star is a moth beside a lighthouse, a long way off. The lighthouse is all anyone sees.”

He watched the stars for a while, and said nothing.

“But a moth can fly in front of the lamp.”
