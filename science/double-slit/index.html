Speckles

The picture of The Van was still on Cal’s phone in the morning.

It wasn’t much of a picture. The night before, out in the dark car park, the probe’s camera had been turned up as far as it would go, and pointed at The Van under a sky with no moon. For a long time it had shown nothing at all. Then single specks of light had begun to appear on the screen, one here, one there, each a lump of light that had bounced off The Van and come to rest in one place on the camera’s sensor. They came in no order anyone could see. But they kept coming, and slowly, speck by speck, the outline of The Van had gathered itself out of the dark: the roof, the mismatched panels, the rear door that needed persuasion.

Cal was looking at it when John came out with the coffee.

“Every one of those dots is one photon,” said Cal.

“That’s what the third journey was for,” said John. “Light comes in lumps. Each lump lands in one place, all at once.”

“Then I’ve got a problem with Chasing a Beam of Light.”

John handed him a cup. In Chasing a Beam of Light, the second journey of the series before this one, they had found that light is a wave, and one of the ways people had found that out, two hundred years ago, was by shining it through two narrow slits. Light coming through two slits makes stripes, bright and dark, as two sets of ripples on a pond make a pattern where they cross. Particles, thrown through two gaps, just make two piles.

“If light is lumps,” said Cal, “every lump has to go through one slit or the other. A lump can’t go through two slits. So where do the stripes come from?”

John thought about it. “The lumps bump into each other on the way. The ones from one slit get in the way of the ones from the other.”

“So if you sent them one at a time, there’d be no stripes.”

“I suppose so.”

“Have you ever sent them one at a time?”

They went inside to ask Albert, who was rolling the first doughnuts of the day in their lemon sugar. The grain of sugar sat at the end of the counter under its upturned glass, with its napkin, DO NOT EAT, propped against it. Albert listened to the question, put down the tray, wiped his hands, and looked at the two of them for a long moment.

“Come into the kitchen,” he said.

A hair in a laser beam

Albert’s kitchen was small and very clean, with a white wall at one end and a window at the other, over the sink. He drew the blind. Then he went to his cupboard of props, the one that had once produced a currant dough, a doughnut box with a lamp at each end and, on one occasion, an apple, and came back with a laser pointer: a small red one, the kind used for lectures.

He propped it on a pile of cookery books on the table, pointed at the white wall, and switched it on. A single red dot appeared on the wall, about three metres away.

“Now,” said Albert. “I need a hair.”

Cal looked at Albert’s hair again. Albert sighed, considered it for a moment, chose one, and pulled it out. There were plenty to choose from. He taped it across the hole in an old slide frame, so that it stood upright, and held the frame in the beam, a few centimetres from the pointer, so that the hair ran straight across the middle of the beam.

The dot on the wall changed. It wasn’t a dot any more. It was a row: a bright red spot in the middle, where the dot had been, and then on either side of it, spreading sideways across the wall, a line of fainter spots, evenly spaced, dimmer the further out they were, with darkness between them.

“That’s not the shadow of a hair,” said John.

“No,” said Albert. “It is something much more interesting.”

Two ways to every place on the wall

A hair is about a fifteenth of a millimetre thick. Its shadow, if light travelled in perfectly straight lines, would be a thin dark line across the middle of the red dot, far too thin to see. Instead the light has spread out sideways, across the wall, into a row of spots and gaps many centimetres wide.

The spreading is the first clue. When a wave meets an edge, it doesn’t carry on in a straight line past it, as a stream of bullets would. It spreads out round the edge. You have seen it with water if you’ve watched waves come into a harbour through a gap in its wall: inside, they don’t stay in a narrow lane the width of the gap, but spread out in curving ripples across the whole harbour. Sound does it too, which is why you can hear someone talking in the next room through an open door without being able to see them. Light does it as well, but light’s waves are so short (less than a thousandth of a millimetre, for this red light) that you only notice it when the edge or the gap is very small. A hair is small enough.

So light goes past the hair on both sides, and from each side it spreads out across the wall. Now pick any place on the wall, a little to the left of the middle. Light reaches it by two routes: round the left side of the hair, and round the right. The two routes aren’t quite the same length. And what happens when two waves meet depends on how they arrive.

In Echoes of Spacetime, the thirteenth journey of the series before this one, Albert set up a laser and some mirrors on the counter, and John and Cal found that when two waves meet, their heights add. Where a crest of one arrives with a crest of the other, they make a crest twice as high. Where a crest of one arrives with a trough of the other, they cancel, and there is nothing. Noise-cancelling headphones work this way, and so does an interferometer, the instrument built to hear gravitational waves.

The two routes to a place on the wall work the same way. As you move along the wall, away from the middle, the route round the far side of the hair gets steadily longer than the route round the near side. Each time the difference grows by another half a wavelength, the light from the two sides swaps between arriving in step, crest with crest, and arriving out of step, crest with trough. So bright and dark take turns across the wall, evenly spaced, with a new bright spot each time the difference grows by a whole wavelength. That is the row of spots.

Interference

What happens when two or more waves arrive at the same place: their heights add. Where crests meet crests, the waves reinforce each other; where crests meet troughs, they cancel. When the same wave reaches a place by two routes of different lengths, setting out in step, the difference decides which: bright where the routes differ by a whole number of wavelengths, dark where they differ by a half. A regular pattern of bright and dark stripes is the signature of interference, and so of waves.

You can check the explanation against the wall. If the spots come from routes differing by whole wavelengths, then shorter waves should give spots closer together, and a thinner hair, whose two sides are closer together, should spread them further apart. Both are true. Green light makes a tighter row than red. A fine blond hair makes a wider one than a thick dark one.

Thomas Young’s card

The first person to see this, and to say what it meant, was Thomas Young, a London doctor who also made one of the first starts on reading the Rosetta Stone. In November 1803 he described an experiment to the Royal Society. He made a tiny hole in a window shutter, so that a thin beam of sunlight came into the darkened room, and held in it a strip of card about a thirtieth of an inch wide. In the card’s shadow, where there should have been only darkness, there were stripes. Then he put a second card at the edge of the first, blocking the light passing one side of it, and the stripes in the shadow vanished.

That second step is the heart of it. The stripes need light from both sides. Take away either route, and there is nothing to interfere with, and no stripes.

“Now the other one,” said Albert.

He had a microscope slide in the cupboard, which he had held over a candle until it was black with soot. Across the soot, with two razor blades taped together, someone had scratched two fine, clear lines, very close side by side. He held the slide in the beam instead of the hair. Again the dot on the wall became a row of red stripes, this time finer and more closely spaced, with a broader glow over them. Light through two slits, crossing and adding: the experiment Young described in his lectures of 1807, with a drawing of two sets of ripples spreading from two openings and crossing in a pattern of lines. Here the rule is at its simplest. Both slits are lit by the same beam, so the light leaves them in step, and the wall is bright wherever the routes from the two slits differ by a whole number of wavelengths, and dark wherever they differ by a half.

For the rest of the nineteenth century, that settled it. Particles can’t do this. Waves can. Light is a wave.

Try it

You can do Albert’s experiment in your own kitchen. You need a red laser pointer of the ordinary low-power kind sold for presentations, a hair, a little sticky tape and a pale wall a few metres away. Tape the hair across the opening of something that will hold it still (a slide frame, or a hole cut in a piece of card), with a bit of slack taken out so that it’s straight. Rest the pointer on something steady, so that you don’t have to hold it, shine it at the wall, and then put the hair in the beam close to the pointer. The darker the room, the more spots you’ll see.

Warning

Use only a low-power laser pointer (Class 1 or Class 2, under 1 milliwatt) and never look into the beam, or point it at anyone’s eyes, at people, at animals, or at cars or aircraft. Watch out for reflections from shiny surfaces such as mirrors, glass and spoons. Powerful laser pointers sold online can blind in an instant, and many are labelled wrongly. Laser pointers are not toys: keep them away from children.

With a wall about three metres away and an ordinary hair, the spots come out about three centimetres apart. Try a hair from someone else. Move the wall further away and watch the row spread.

Turning the light down

Cal looked at the stripes for a while.

“That’s a lot of photons,” he said.

He was right. A laser pointer like Albert’s sends out thousands of millions of millions of photons every second. Every spot on the wall is made of a vast crowd of them, all arriving at once. John’s explanation, that the lumps somehow get in each other’s way, was still possible. The only way to test it was to do what Cal had suggested: turn the light down until the photons come through one at a time.

Albert’s pointer couldn’t do that. The Van’s simulator could.

The dashboard screen, when they got back out to The Van, was showing a picture of a kettle. Cal hit it firmly in the place that usually worked, the kettle went away, and John asked the simulator to rebuild Albert’s kitchen: the laser, the sooty slide with its two scratches, and, in place of the wall, the probe’s camera, which had counted single photons in the car park the night before. Then he told it to turn the laser down. Down, and down, until it sent out one photon at a time, with a long pause between each one and the next.

For a while, nothing happened. Then a single dot appeared on the camera’s picture, somewhere off to the left.

PHOTONS DETECTED: 1. PATTERN: NOT FOUND

Then another, near the middle. Then one far out on the right. The dots came one at a time, each landing in one place, whole, and apparently anywhere: no dot ever arrived in two places at once, or as half a dot, and nothing about where the last one landed told you anything about where the next would go.

“There you are,” said John. “No stripes.”

“Give it a minute,” said Cal.

They gave it several. The dots went on arriving, one at a time, and gradually something began to show in where they landed. There were more of them in some places than others. Then the places where they were crowding began to line up in bands, and in between the bands, where no dot had yet landed, there were gaps.

PHOTONS DETECTED: 12,000. PATTERN: STRIPES

The stripes were the same stripes Albert had thrown on his kitchen wall, built up out of single photons, one dot at a time. And the dark bands stayed dark. However long they waited, almost no photon ever landed in them.

Three months in Cambridge

Someone had tried this long before there were simulators. In 1909 the young physicist Geoffrey Ingram Taylor, in Cambridge, set up the faintest light he could manage: a gas flame’s light, dimmed by passing it through screens of smoked glass. With it he photographed the shadow of a needle, whose edges make fine stripes in the same way as the hair. The light was so feeble that his longest exposure lasted about three months. And at the end of it the stripes on his photographic plate were as sharp as they were in bright light.

Taylor’s light was dim enough that, on average, there was much less than one photon at a time inside his apparatus. A flame’s light doesn’t come out in an orderly queue, though, so now and then two would have been in there together. The truly one-at-a-time version, with a source giving out one photon and only one at a time, was done in 1986, in France, and it gave the same answer: one photon at a time, through two paths, still builds the stripes.

Which slit did it go through?

Here is the puzzle, laid out a step at a time, because everything in the rest of this series grows from it.

Start with what is certain. Each photon arrives whole, in one place, as a single dot. No camera has ever recorded half a photon, or one photon in two places. That is how particles behave, and it is what the third journey found.

Now make the obvious assumption about what happens before it arrives. A single, whole photon, on its way from the laser to the camera, goes through one slit or the other. It’s either one slit or the other; there’s nothing else for a particle to do.

You can find out what each slit does on its own by closing the other one. Cover slit B and send the photons through A alone. They still arrive one at a time, as dots, and the dots pile up into one broad smooth patch, brightest in the middle and fading out to either side, with no stripes in it at all. That’s how a single narrow slit spreads light, like the harbour’s gap. Cover A instead and send them through B, and you get the same broad smooth patch.

Now open both. If every photon goes through one slit or the other, then about half of them go through A, and those make A’s patch, and the other half go through B and make B’s patch. Each photon, going through its slit, can’t be affected by a slit it didn’t go through. So the pattern with both slits open must be A’s patch and B’s patch added together: one broad, smooth patch, twice as bright, brightest in the middle, and with no stripes.

It isn’t. With both slits open, the photons make stripes, and in the dark stripes almost none arrive at all, though with either slit alone, plenty arrived in exactly those places.

So the assumption has to go, unless there’s another way out. There are two to try.

The first is John’s: the photons get in each other’s way. But they came one at a time, with nothing else in the apparatus to bump into. In Taylor’s experiment, most of the time each photon had been swallowed up by the photographic plate long before the next was made. A photon can’t interfere with another photon that has already been absorbed, or that doesn’t exist yet. And the stripes were there anyway.

The second is that each photon really does go through one slit, but somehow knows whether the other slit is open, and behaves differently if it is. Look at what that says, though: what each single photon does depends on both slits. That isn’t a way out. It’s the conclusion, said another way.

So there is only one thing left. Each photon, travelling alone, is affected by both slits. Something about it went both ways. It sets off from the laser as a particle and lands on the camera as a particle, and in between, it is guided by a pattern only a wave, going through both slits at once, could make.

Wave-particle duality

The way light and matter behave both like particles and like waves, depending on what you look at. Each photon or electron is always detected whole, in one place, like a particle. But where it is likely to be detected follows a pattern that only waves can make, such as the stripes from two slits, and the pattern builds up even when the particles come one at a time. Neither picture, a little ball or a spreading ripple, describes it completely.

“So it’s a wave and a particle,” said John.

“It’s something that arrives like a particle,” said Cal, “and decides where to arrive like a wave.”

Albert, who had followed them out with his coffee, looked at the stripes on the screen with an expression of mild discomfort. “A physicist said in Salzburg, in 1909, that the next stage in the theory of light would be a sort of fusion of the wave and the particle,” he said. “It was a long time before anyone knew how.”

Electrons, which are certainly particles

“Light was always odd,” said John. “A wave that comes in lumps. Let’s try something that’s definitely a particle.”

He meant the electron. In the first journey, they had met it as J. J. Thomson found it in 1897: a particle far lighter than any atom, with a definite mass and a definite negative charge, which could be boiled out of a hot wire and steered by a magnet, and which once painted the picture on the screen of every television with a deep glass back. Electrons hit a screen one at a time, each one at a single point, and you can count them. Nobody had ever thought an electron was a wave.

So John asked the simulator for an electron gun in place of the laser, and two slits a thousandth of a millimetre apart, and the probe’s camera, which can count electrons as well as photons. He set the gun to fire one electron at a time.

The dots came one at a time, each in one place, apparently at random. And slowly, dot by dot, they built stripes.

That has been done for real, though it took until the 1970s to manage it with electrons one at a time. It’s hard to make slits fine enough for electrons, so the experimenters used a trick. In 1974 a team in Bologna, Pier Giorgio Merli, Gian Franco Missiroli and Giulio Pozzi, put an extremely fine wire across the path of an electron beam inside an electron microscope and gave the wire an electric charge, so that electrons passing on either side of it were bent towards each other, and their two routes crossed on the far side. It is, more or less, Albert’s hair, for electrons. They turned the beam down until the electrons came through singly, and filmed the stripes building up on a screen. In 1989 Akira Tonomura’s team at Hitachi, in Japan, did it again, with electrons fired at 50,000 volts. Their electrons came so seldom, and crossed the microscope so fast, in about a hundred-millionth of a second each, that the microscope was empty almost all the time: no electron ever had another to bump into. The first frames of their film show a scatter of dots. After twenty minutes, there are stripes. In 2002, the readers of the magazine Physics World voted the single-electron version of the two-slit experiment the most beautiful experiment in physics.

In the figure, choose “Electrons” and watch it happen again. Electrons, which are certainly particles, each with its mass and its charge, each arriving whole at one place, build the stripes of a wave, one at a time. Each single electron is affected by both slits, just as each photon was.

A wavelength for matter

If an electron makes stripes, it must have a wavelength: the stripes’ spacing depends on it, as it did for the red light. What sets an electron’s wavelength?

The answer came, as it happens, three years before anyone had seen an electron behave like a wave. In 1924, a young French physicist, Louis de Broglie, wrote a thesis for his doctorate with an idea that most physicists found very strange. He started from light. In The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe, the seventh journey of the series before this one, John and Cal found that light carries momentum, the quantity of motion a thing carries in its direction, even though it has no mass, and that bluer light, with shorter waves, carries more of it. Each photon carries a definite momentum, set by its wavelength: the shorter the wave, the more momentum.

Teleport to the Relativity moonThe Most Expensive Doughnut in the UniverseMomentum, and the momentum light carries

De Broglie turned the idea round. If waves of light come in lumps that carry momentum like particles, perhaps particles that carry momentum come with waves. And perhaps the rule is the same both ways: a thing’s wavelength is Planck’s constant, the tiny number from the second journey that sets the size of the lumps, divided by its momentum.

Momentum, at everyday speeds, is mass times speed. So the faster something moves, the shorter its wave; and the heavier it is, the shorter its wave, at the same speed. Planck’s constant is so small that even an electron, the lightest thing in an atom, has a wavelength about the width of an atom when it moves at about seven thousand kilometres a second, a fortieth of the speed of light. Anything heavier, or faster, has a wave shorter still.

Matter wave

The wave belonging to every moving piece of matter, an electron, an atom, a molecule or anything else, which decides where it is likely to be found, just as light’s wave decides where its photons land. Its wavelength is Planck’s constant divided by the thing’s momentum (its mass times its speed, at everyday speeds), so faster or heavier things have shorter waves. For electrons it is about the width of an atom; for anything you can see, it is far too small ever to notice. The idea is Louis de Broglie’s, from 1924.

“Somebody read his thesis,” said Albert, who was sitting on The Van’s step with his coffee, “and wrote to his professor that he had lifted a corner of the great veil.”

“Who?” said Cal.

“Somebody who read a great deal,” said Albert, and finished his coffee.

Electrons off a crystal

A wave as short as an atom’s width needs slits about as narrow and as close together as atoms, and nature makes those. In a crystal, the atoms sit in neat rows, a fraction of a nanometre apart, and the rows on its surface can act like a great many slits side by side. Light’s waves are thousands of times too long to notice them: that was the first journey’s peppercorn in Albert’s oven gloves. But electrons, at the right speed, have waves just the right length.

At the Bell Telephone Laboratories in New York, Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer had been firing electrons at a block of nickel and measuring how many bounced off in each direction, for reasons that had nothing to do with de Broglie. In 1925 an accident let air into their apparatus, and to clean the nickel they heated it, which turned it into a few large crystals. After that, the electrons no longer bounced off evenly. By 1927 they had shown that the electrons came back strongest at one particular angle, about fifty degrees from straight back, just where waves from neighbouring rows of atoms would arrive in step, for exactly the wavelength de Broglie’s rule gave their electrons. In the same year in Aberdeen, George Paget Thomson fired electrons through thin films of metal and found them spreading out in rings, the pattern waves make in a jumble of tiny crystals. George Thomson was J. J. Thomson’s son. The father had won a Nobel Prize for his work on the electron as a particle, and the son won one for showing that it is a wave, and they were both right.

The next figure fires electrons straight down at a crystal of nickel, as Davisson and Germer did, with the electrons’ wave drawn to the same scale as the rows of atoms. Slide their speed up and down. Slow electrons have waves longer than the gap between the rows, and can only come straight back. Speed them up, and once the wave is shorter than the gap, bright beams appear at an angle; the faster they go, the shorter the wave, and the closer the beams close in towards straight back. The first button sets Davisson and Germer’s own speed; the second, the much faster electrons of the Hitachi film. Below, the wavelength is set against things you know, on a ruler where each step is ten times smaller than the last. A thrown doughnut’s wavelength is so far off the end that the ruler would have to be more than four times as long to reach it.

Footballs of carbon

Electrons are very light, so their waves are comparatively long. What about something bigger: a whole atom, or a molecule made of many atoms? De Broglie’s rule says it too should go through two slits like a wave, as long as nothing disturbs it on the way. But the heavier the thing, the shorter its wave, and the finer the slits and the more delicate the experiment have to be.

In 1999, in Vienna, Markus Arndt, Anton Zeilinger and their colleagues tried it with one of the most beautiful molecules in chemistry: sixty carbon atoms joined in a hollow cage, with twelve five-sided faces and twenty six-sided ones, exactly the pattern of the panels on a football. It is about a nanometre across, and about seven hundred times as heavy as a hydrogen atom. They heated carbon footballs in an oven until they turned to vapour and streamed out in a thin beam, flying at about 220 metres a second, and sent them through a grating of slits a ten-thousandth of a millimetre apart. Each molecule’s wavelength was about 400 times smaller than the molecule itself. And the molecules, arriving one by one at the detector, made stripes.

These same footballs float in space. In 2010, in the Iris Nebula, a cloud of dust lit by a young star in the constellation of Cepheus, the Spitzer Space Telescope made the first firm detection of them between the stars.

Since then the molecules have kept getting bigger. In 2019, the Vienna group sent molecules of nearly two thousand atoms through their gratings, and they interfered. In January 2026, a team reported the same for clusters of more than seven thousand sodium atoms, each cluster more than 170,000 times as heavy as a hydrogen atom: the largest things yet seen to go through two paths at once and make stripes. Nobody has found a size at which it stops. Choose “Carbon footballs” in the first figure, and watch them build their stripes like everything else.

Doughnuts through The Van

By the middle of the afternoon, John had decided to try it for himself.

“The Van has two doors,” he said. “That’s two slits.”

Cal looked at The Van. Its sliding side door was open, and so was its back door, persuaded open with a firm shove.

“They’re not on the same side,” he said.

“The doughnut won’t know,” said John.

He climbed in, with a plate of Albert’s lemon doughnuts, and stood in the middle of The Van, facing the back, and began to throw them out, one at a time, as evenly as he could, aiming between the two doors. Some went out through the back door and landed on the tarmac behind The Van. Some went out through the side door and landed beside it. The rest hit the inside of The Van and fell on the floor, where Cal made a note of them.

After twenty doughnuts, there were two piles in the car park: one behind the back door and one outside the side door. There was nothing between them, and nothing that looked even slightly like a stripe.

INTERFERENCE: NONE DETECTED. DOUGHNUTS: 20. PLEASE CONSULT YOUR DEALER

Albert came out of the café and looked at the piles. He looked at them for some time.

“I do not make them to be thrown,” he said.

The result is exactly what de Broglie’s rule predicts. One of Albert’s doughnuts weighs about sixty grams, and John was throwing them at about three metres a second. A doughnut is vastly heavier than an electron, and that makes its wavelength smaller than a proton, the nucleus of a hydrogen atom, by about half a billion billion times. Even if John had found two doors side by side in one wall, the doughnuts’ stripes would be so close together that about a hundred million billion of them would fit across the width of one proton. Nothing could ever see them. Each doughnut’s wave would make, in practice, a sharp-edged beam through its door, and the doughnuts would land in two piles, one behind each, exactly as if they were simply little balls. The figure’s last button throws them.

So matter waves aren’t only for electrons and molecules. Everything has one: the doughnut, The Van, John. It’s just that for anything big enough to see, the wave is so absurdly short that it never shows, and the everyday rules, in which things go one way or the other, work perfectly. (There is another reason big things never show stripes, which has nothing to do with how short their waves are. It waits for the fourteenth journey.)

Back to Teide

By evening the doughnuts had been collected, without comment, by Albert, and they were not seen again. The coaches had gone back down to the coast. The low sun lit the old lava gold, and long shadows lay across the car park from The Van and its two open doors.

Cal hadn’t said much since the doughnuts. He was sitting on the terrace with his coffee, and on The Van’s screen through the windscreen John could see the stripes, still building, the simulator still firing single photons at its two slits.

“Something’s bothering me,” said Cal.

“The doughnuts?”

“Not the doughnuts. The dark stripes.” He pointed at the screen. “Take a place in the middle of one of those. With only one slit open, photons land there all the time. It’s in the middle of the broad patch. Now open the other slit, and they stop landing there. Almost none, ever.”

“That’s what interference does,” said John.

“I know it’s what it does. But think about what it means. We opened a second way through, and fewer of them got there.” He waved at the café behind them. “If Albert opens his second door, more customers can come in. Not fewer. Giving something more ways to get somewhere can’t make it less likely to get there. Can it?”

John opened his mouth, and closed it again.

Albert had come out to collect their cups. He looked at the screen, and then at the café, which had a door onto the car park and another onto the terrace, both standing open in the warm evening.

“Come back in the morning,” he said. “And bring that question with you.”

Through the windscreen, the simulator went on firing its photons, one at a time, each landing in one place, and none of them in the dark.
