The stripes’ spacing

Two slits. Two narrow slits a distance ddd apart are lit by the same beam, so light leaves them in step. On a screen a distance LLL away (with LLL much larger than ddd), the point at height yyy from the middle is reached by two routes whose lengths differ by

Δ≈dsin⁡θ≈d yL,tan⁡θ=yL.\Delta \approx d \sin\theta \approx \frac{d\,y}{L}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{L}.Δ≈dsinθ≈Ldy​,tanθ=Ly​.

The waves arrive in step, and the screen is bright, where Δ\DeltaΔ is a whole number of wavelengths, Δ=mλ\Delta = m\lambdaΔ=mλ, so the bright stripes sit at

ym=mλLd,m=0,±1,±2,…y_m = \frac{m \lambda L}{d}, \qquad m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldotsym​=dmλL​,m=0,±1,±2,…

and they are evenly spaced, λL/d\lambda L / dλL/d apart. Dark stripes lie halfway between, where Δ=(m+12)λ\Delta = (m + \tfrac{1}{2})\lambdaΔ=(m+21​)λ. For red light (λ=650\lambda = 650λ=650 nm) through two scratches d=0.25d = 0.25d=0.25 mm apart onto a wall L=3L = 3L=3 m away,

λLd=650×10−9×30.25×10−3≈7.8×10−3 m,\frac{\lambda L}{d} = \frac{650 \times 10^{-9} \times 3}{0.25 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 7.8 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m},dλL​=0.25×10−3650×10−9×3​≈7.8×10−3 m,

about 8 millimetres.

The spread from each slit. A slit of width aaa spreads light into a broad patch whose brightness falls off as

I1(θ)∝(sin⁡ββ)2,β=πasin⁡θλ,I_1(\theta) \propto \left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2, \qquad \beta = \frac{\pi a \sin\theta}{\lambda},I1​(θ)∝(βsinβ​)2,β=λπasinθ​,

with its first dark place at sin⁡θ=λ/a\sin\theta = \lambda / asinθ=λ/a. With both slits open, the stripes are laid over that spread:

I2(θ)=4 I1(θ)cos⁡2 ⁣(πdsin⁡θλ).I_2(\theta) = 4\, I_1(\theta) \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi d \sin\theta}{\lambda}\right).I2​(θ)=4I1​(θ)cos2(λπdsinθ​).

At the bright stripes the brightness is four times one slit’s; at the dark ones, nothing. Averaged across a stripe it is twice one slit’s, the same as the two one-slit patterns added: the stripes move the light about, they don’t make more of it. (Why it is four times at the bright stripes, rather than twice, is the next journey’s subject.)

The hair. A hair of width aaa blocks the beam where a slit of width aaa would let it through. By a rule of wave optics called Babinet’s principle, away from the direct beam the two make the same pattern: the spread above, with dark places at sin⁡θ=mλ/a\sin\theta = m\lambda/asinθ=mλ/a and so spots λL/a\lambda L / aλL/a apart. For a hair 70 micrometres thick, red light and a wall 3 m away,

λLa=650×10−9×370×10−6≈2.8 cm.\frac{\lambda L}{a} = \frac{650 \times 10^{-9} \times 3}{70 \times 10^{-6}} \approx 2.8\ \text{cm}.aλL​=70×10−6650×10−9×3​≈2.8 cm.

A thinner hair, or a further wall, spreads them wider; green light (about 530 nm) brings them about a fifth closer together.

Why one slit or the other can’t make stripes

Call PA(y)P_A(y)PA​(y) the share of particles landing near yyy with only slit A open, and PB(y)P_B(y)PB​(y) with only B open. Suppose every particle goes through one slit or the other, and that what a particle does after going through A doesn’t depend on whether B is open. Then with both open, a fraction fff go through A and make A’s pattern, and 1−f1 - f1−f through B and make B’s (with equal slits equally lit, f=12f = \tfrac{1}{2}f=21​):

Pboth(y)=f PA(y)+(1−f) PB(y).P_\text{both}(y) = f\,P_A(y) + (1 - f)\,P_B(y).Pboth​(y)=fPA​(y)+(1−f)PB​(y).

Both terms are positive or zero, so Pboth(y)≥f PA(y)P_\text{both}(y) \geq f\,P_A(y)Pboth​(y)≥fPA​(y) everywhere: wherever slit A alone sends particles, both slits together must send at least half as many. At the middle of a dark stripe the measured PbothP_\text{both}Pboth​ is nearly zero while PAP_APA​ is near its largest. So the supposition fails, and no choice of fff rescues it.

Matter’s wavelength

De Broglie’s rule gives a particle of momentum ppp the wavelength

λ=hp,p=γmv≈mv (slowly),\lambda = \frac{h}{p}, \qquad p = \gamma m v \approx m v \ \text{(slowly)},λ=ph​,p=γmv≈mv (slowly),

where h=6.626×10−34h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}h=6.626×10−34 J s is Planck’s constant and γ\gammaγ the Lorentz factor of the relativity series. For a photon, p=h/λp = h/\lambdap=h/λ is the momentum of light; de Broglie read the same rule backwards for matter.

An electron as long as an atom is wide. For λ=0.1\lambda = 0.1λ=0.1 nm, an electron (m=9.11×10−31m = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}m=9.11×10−31 kg) needs

v=hmλ=6.626×10−349.11×10−31×10−10≈7.3×106 m/s,v = \frac{h}{m \lambda} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 10^{-10}} \approx 7.3 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m/s},v=mλh​=9.11×10−31×10−106.626×10−34​≈7.3×106 m/s,

about 7,300 kilometres a second, a fortieth of the speed of light. At 1 per cent of light’s speed its wavelength is 0.24 nm.

Davisson and Germer. Their electrons were accelerated through 54 volts, so each had a kinetic energy E=54E = 54E=54 eV =8.65×10−18= 8.65 \times 10^{-18}=8.65×10−18 J, a speed v=2E/m≈4.36×106v = \sqrt{2E/m} \approx 4.36 \times 10^6v=2E/m​≈4.36×106 m/s, and a wavelength

λ=h2mE≈0.167 nm.\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m E}} \approx 0.167\ \text{nm}.λ=2mE​h​≈0.167 nm.

Fired straight at a nickel crystal whose surface rows of atoms are d=0.215d = 0.215d=0.215 nm apart, the waves from neighbouring rows come back in step at the angle θ\thetaθ from straight back where dsin⁡θ=λd \sin\theta = \lambdadsinθ=λ:

sin⁡θ=0.1670.215=0.776,θ≈51°.\sin\theta = \frac{0.167}{0.215} = 0.776, \qquad \theta \approx 51°.sinθ=0.2150.167​=0.776,θ≈51°.

They found the strongest beam at 50°. Slower electrons, with λ>d\lambda > dλ>d, have no such angle.

Carbon footballs. A C₆₀ molecule has a mass of 720 atomic mass units, 1.20×10−241.20 \times 10^{-24}1.20×10−24 kg. At 220 m/s,

λ=6.626×10−341.20×10−24×220≈2.5×10−12 m,\lambda = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{1.20 \times 10^{-24} \times 220} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{m},λ=1.20×10−24×2206.626×10−34​≈2.5×10−12 m,

A doughnut’s wavelength

λ=6.626×10−340.18≈3.7×10−33 m.\lambda = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{0.18} \approx 3.7 \times 10^{-33}\ \text{m}.λ=0.186.626×10−34​≈3.7×10−33 m.
