The Van's old television has shown nothing but snow all night, and John blames the wiring. About one speck in a hundred of that snow is older than any star. By evening John and Cal have found why the young universe was a fog, how its glow became microwaves, and what its faint ripples say the universe is made of. Fred, in for tea, is not impressed.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 4 of 18

Snow

The Van’s television had been hissing all night.

It was a little portable set from the days before flat screens, with a handle on top and a telescopic aerial, and it had come with The Van, on a shelf above the kettle. The evening before, John had switched it on to see what was on. Nothing had been on. The screen had filled with a restless grey storm of dots, and the speaker had hissed like a kettle about to boil.

By the time Cal came across the car park with two coffees, John had the back off the set and was peering into it with a torch.

“It’s the wiring,” he said. “It’s always the wiring.”

The Van had evidently been listening. A line came up on the dashboard screen.

FAULT NOT FOUND. FAULT NOT IN VEHICLE

“There’s nothing for it to pick up,” said Cal. “Spain switched off the old kind of television in 2010. Every channel’s between channels now.”

“Then the snow’s just the set,” said John. “Its own electronics, fizzing.”

“Mostly,” said Albert.

He had come out behind Cal with a plate of lemon doughnuts, which he set on the dashboard, well out of reach of the open television, and he watched the snow for a while like a man looking at an old photograph.

“Mostly?” said John.

“Most of what you see is the set’s own fizz, as you say, and some is the warmth of the ground, and some is our galaxy, which hisses faintly in radio. But about one speck in every hundred, or a little less, has come rather further than that.”

“From where?”

“From everywhere,” said Albert. “It is older than the Sun, and older than every star. It is the oldest light there is.”

A battered car came up the road from the coast, rattling, and pulled into the car park beside The Van. A man in an old tweed jacket climbed out, looked at the television with its back off, at the snow, and at Albert, and sighed.

It was Fred, Albert’s old friend, back for the second time. On his first visit he had told them, at length and with great enjoyment, that the universe had never had a beginning at all, and that he should know, because he had given the idea its name, on the radio, in 1949.

“Don’t tell me,” he said. “He’s told you that’s the echo of the big bang.”

“Not in so many words,” said Albert.

“In my words,” said Fred. “I gave it that name, you know.”

“You said,” said Cal. “Last time.”

“And I didn’t mean it rudely.”

“Nobody said you did.”

“Everybody says I did,” said Fred, and went inside to see about his tea.

He took it as he took everything: strong. The café’s teapot, which had once come out only for Niels, now came out for Fred too, and Albert had learned to leave the tea in it until a spoon would very nearly stand up. Fred tasted it, nodded, and came back out to The Van with the cup.

“That snow,” he said, “is your set’s own fizz, and the bit that isn’t is starlight, knocked about on its way here until it’s lost its shape. Nothing to do with any beginning.” He sat down on The Van’s step. “But go on. I like to hear the argument. It’s a good argument. It’s wrong, but it’s good.”

A noise nobody could get rid of

The first people to find the oldest light didn’t want it. They were trying to get rid of it.

In 1964 two radio astronomers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, were working at Bell Telephone Laboratories in Holmdel, New Jersey, with a strange radio aerial: a metal horn about fifteen metres long, with a mouth six metres square, like an ear trumpet lying on its side, built in 1959 to catch signals bounced off a balloon satellite called Echo. They wanted to measure the faint radio glow of our own galaxy with it, and to do that, they had to know exactly how much hiss the horn picked up from an empty piece of sky.

Every radio receiver hisses: some of the hiss comes from its own electronics, some from the air, and some from the ground, which, like everything warm, glows faintly in radio waves. Radio engineers measure a hiss as a temperature, the temperature of a hot thing whose glow would make as much hiss, which is natural, because, as John and Cal found in Red Hot, White Hot, how much anything glows at each wavelength depends only on how hot it is.

They added up every source of hiss they knew of, at a wavelength of 7.35 centimetres, and the sky gave more than their sum: about three and a half degrees’ worth more, the same wherever they pointed the horn, day and night, from July 1964 into the spring of 1965. Whatever it was, it wasn’t coming from the Sun, or the galaxy, or New York.

They checked everything. They found that two pigeons had been nesting in the narrow end of the horn, beside the warm machinery cooling the receiver, and had covered the inside of it with what Penzias called “a white dielectric material”. The pigeons were caught and sent away, by one account in the company’s internal post to another Bell site, and came back, and were eventually discouraged more firmly. The horn was cleaned. The hiss went down a very little, and stayed.

About fifty kilometres away, at Princeton University, Robert Dicke, Jim Peebles, Peter Roll and David Wilkinson were building a small receiver to look for exactly that hiss: if the universe had begun hot and dense, they had worked out, the glow of that heat ought to be left over, cooled, filling space. Word passed between the two groups, and Penzias telephoned Dicke, who is said to have put the phone down and told his team they had been scooped. They published side by side in 1965, Bell Labs with the measurement and Princeton with what it meant, and Penzias and Wilson shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for it in 1978.

They weren’t quite the first. In 1948 Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman, working with George Gamow on the first minutes of the universe, had predicted a leftover glow of about five degrees, and nobody had gone to look. And in 1941 the Canadian astronomer Andrew McKellar had found that molecules in a cloud of gas between the stars were very slightly warmed, as if bathed in something at about two degrees above absolute zero. Nobody, McKellar included, knew what it meant until long afterwards.

A glow with nowhere to go

“Then why should there be a glow at all?” said John.

“You tell me,” said Albert. “Two journeys ago you ran the film backwards.”

They had, in the second journey. If every distance between the galaxies is growing now, every distance was smaller once, and squeezing anything heats it. Run the film far enough back, and the whole universe was hot and dense, everywhere at once.

“So it was hot,” said John. “And hot things glow.”

“And?”

John thought about the oven in Red Hot, White Hot, with the small hole in its door. Inside a closed oven, once everything has settled to the same temperature, the light depends on the temperature and nothing else: every wall, shelf and tray glows with the same perfect glow, whatever it’s made of. Kirchhoff had shown that in 1859. The young universe was an oven of exactly that kind, only better: hot everywhere, the same in every direction, and with no door, because there was nothing outside it for the light to leak into. So it must have been filled with the most perfect glow there could be.

Now ask where that glow could have gone. Light travels until something stops it. But in a universe filled evenly with glowing stuff, every lump of light leaving one place is replaced by another arriving from somewhere else. And if something absorbs the light, it warms up, and glows in its turn. There is no outside for the glow to escape into and nothing to swallow it for good. The glow can’t have gone anywhere. It must still be here, in every part of space, including the car park, and The Van, and the space inside Fred’s teacup.

But it can’t still look the way it did, because the universe has grown. In Further Apart Every Day John and Cal watched a pulse of light cross the expanding universe and saw its waves stretched by exactly the factor the universe grew while it travelled. The oldest light has been travelling for almost the whole age of the universe, and in that time every distance has grown about 1,100 times. So have its waves. A glow setting out at about 3,000 degrees, the warm yellow-white glow of something a little hotter than a lamp’s filament, arrives with waves 1,100 times longer: the glow of something less than three degrees above absolute zero. Its brightest waves are now about a millimetre long. They’re microwaves, the same family of waves as a microwave oven’s or a mobile phone’s, though a good deal shorter, and unimaginably fainter.

The stretching doesn’t thin out the lumps of light, the photons; it only makes each one weaker. In every cubic centimetre of space, the size of a sugar cube, there are about four hundred photons of the oldest light, right now, here in the café. There are more of them than of anything else in the universe: more than a billion for every atom, as the third journey found when it counted the light against the matter.

So the argument closes. If the universe was once hot and dense everywhere, it once glowed everywhere, and the glow can’t have gone anywhere: it must fill space still, stretched into microwaves, the same in every direction. The only way out is that the universe was never hot and dense everywhere at all, which is Fred’s way, and it has to answer to the glow.

“I’ll answer to it,” said Fred. “After my tea.”

The sea of clouds

They had been at the café since early, and the morning had come up clear above and white below. The trade winds had piled their clouds against the north side of the island, as they did on most days, and from the edge of the plateau the clouds lay spread out far below, a level white floor reaching to the horizon, with the tops of a few ridges sticking up through it like islands. Above the floor the air was perfectly clear, so clear that La Palma stood sharp on the western horizon, about 140 kilometres away.

John had driven up through that cloud an hour earlier. Inside it he could barely see the end of The Van’s bonnet. Then, quite suddenly, The Van had climbed out of the top, into sunshine and blue sky, and he could see for a hundred kilometres.

A cloud isn’t dark: from the plateau, its top glows brilliant white in the sunshine. What a cloud does is stop you seeing through it. It’s made of countless tiny droplets of water, each of which turns light aside, so light entering it soon hits a droplet and goes off in a new direction, then another, zigzagging about until it has forgotten where it came from. Inside, everything is a bright, even grey. Above, the light goes straight, and you can see.

The young universe was a fog of exactly that kind, and the droplets were electrons.

A hydrogen atom, as Why the Atom Survives found, is a proton holding on to one electron, and pulling the electron free costs 13.6 electronvolts. Freeing it is like buying one of Albert’s doughnuts at his till, whose rule John and Cal met in Red Hot, White Hot: whole doughnuts only, and no clubbing together. The price has to be paid by a single lump of light, carrying all the energy at once; a crowd of smaller lumps can’t share it. In the young universe, hot enough to glow white, there were lumps carrying that price everywhere, and any electron settling on a proton was knocked off again almost at once. So the electrons were free.

A free electron is the best fog droplet there is. It’s tiny and light and electrically charged, and light is a wave of electric and magnetic forces, so a passing wave shakes it, and the shaken electron sends the light off in a new direction, as a radio mast sends out the waves its electrons are shaken by. An atom is different. An atom takes only the colours of its own fingerprint, the sharp lines of Why the Atom Survives, and lets all the rest straight through. A gas of free electrons is a fog. A gas of atoms is as clear as the air above the clouds.

So the young universe glowed, but nobody could have seen anything in it: light zigzagged from electron to electron through a bright, even fog. Then, as the universe went on expanding and cooling, the price of an electron’s freedom at last became too high for the glow to pay. The electrons settled on to the protons and stayed, the fog cleared, and the light went straight, as it has ever since.

Recombination

The moment in the history of the universe when it had cooled enough for its electrons to settle on to its nuclei and stay, making the first atoms. Before it, the universe was full of free electrons, which turned light aside at every step, so it was a glowing fog. After it, the atoms let most light straight through, and the glow travelled freely. It happened about 380,000 years after the beginning, at about 3,000 degrees. The name is misleading: the electrons had never been joined to the nuclei before, so it was really the first combination.

Cal had been doing sums on Albert’s napkin.

“Something’s wrong,” he said. “An electron’s freedom costs 13.6 electronvolts. A glow whose average lump carries that much is at nearly sixty thousand degrees. So the atoms should have formed at sixty thousand degrees, not three thousand.”

It’s the right objection, and the answer is the one the third journey found for deuterium: how many lumps of light there are. In any glow, most lumps carry about the average amount of energy, but a few carry much more: the far side of Red Hot, White Hot’s curve, where it trails away towards short waves. At ten thousand degrees, only about one lump in sixty thousand can pay an electron’s price. That sounds like few. But there were more than a billion lumps of light for every atom, so there were still tens of thousands of big notes for every atom there was. Every atom was broken up as soon as it formed. The glow had to cool a very long way, until even the rarest big notes became too rare, before the atoms could keep their electrons. In the end hydrogen was half made of atoms by about 3,500 degrees, and by about 3,000 degrees, with only about one electron in seven still free, the fog had thinned enough for the light to go straight. The universe was about 380,000 years old.

A wall in every direction

That’s why there’s a wall.

Looking out into space is looking back in time, because light takes time to arrive: the last item in the detectives’ kit of the first journey. We see Andromeda as it was two and a half million years ago, and the most distant galaxies as they were more than thirteen billion years ago. Look farther still, back to before there were any galaxies at all, and in every direction you reach the fog. You can’t see beyond it, any more than you can see the coast through the sea of clouds. What you see is the fog’s last glow, as you see the sunlit top of the clouds from the plateau.

That glow is the oldest light. Every photon of it last bounced off an electron about 380,000 years after the beginning, about 13.8 billion years ago, and has come straight here since. The matter it came from is now about 45 billion light-years away. And since the light reaches us from every direction, from the fog all round us, the wall surrounds us like the inside of a sphere: not because we’re at the middle of anything, but because anyone, anywhere, looking back to that time, would see the same. Astronomers call the wall the surface of last scattering: the place where each lump of light last bounced off an electron, before it came straight to us.

Surface of last scattering

The wall of the oldest light: the fog’s edge, as we see it from here, all round us, where the light of the cosmic microwave background last bounced off an electron, about 380,000 years after the beginning, as the fog cleared. Light from anything beyond it, or earlier, never reaches us. It isn’t a thing in space but how far back we can see: anyone, anywhere, would see a wall of their own.

Cosmic microwave background

The oldest light in the universe: the glow of the hot, young universe, given out when it was a fog of free electrons and set free about 380,000 years after the beginning, when the fog cleared. It set out at about 3,000 degrees, and the expansion of the universe has since stretched its waves about 1,100 times, so it now arrives as microwaves, the glow of something about 2.7 degrees above absolute zero, from every direction at once. It is the most perfect glow of a hot body ever measured, and it is very slightly lumpy, by about one part in a hundred thousand.

“And that’s in the television?” said John.

“About one speck in a hundred,” said Albert. “The rest is the set and the galaxy and the ground. Penzias and Wilson’s horn was built to hear almost nothing of its own, and it listened at seven centimetres, where the oldest light outshines our galaxy. Your television listens at about half a metre, where the galaxy hisses louder than the oldest light, and its own hiss is louder than both.” He considered the set. “Much louder.”

The most perfect glow

The way to be sure the hiss is the young universe’s glow, and not starlight knocked about, is to measure how bright it is at every wavelength, and see whether the spread has the shape a hot glow has. That’s hard from the ground, because the air glows and absorbs at the wavelengths that matter, so NASA sent an instrument into space. The Cosmic Background Explorer, COBE, was launched in November 1989. Its Far Infrared Absolute Spectrophotometer, built by a team led by John Mather, compared the sky with a carefully made hot body of its own, wavelength by wavelength, from half a millimetre to a centimetre. Within the first nine minutes of measuring, it had a spread of colours. In January 1990 Mather showed it to a meeting of the American Astronomical Society: a smooth curve, with the measurements sitting exactly on it. The audience stood up and applauded.

The oldest light has the spread of colours of a hot body, the same curve as the glow from the hole in Red Hot, White Hot’s oven, at a temperature of 2.725 degrees above absolute zero. When all of COBE’s measurements were in, it matched the curve to within fifty parts in a million. Nothing in nature, or in any laboratory, has ever been measured to be a more perfect glow. Mather shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for it in 2006, with George Smoot, whose instrument on the same spacecraft found its ripples, which come later in this journey.

Teide has its share in it. The observatory up the road has listened to the oldest light since 2000: first with the Very Small Array, fourteen small dishes from Cambridge, Manchester and the Canary Islands’ own institute, which measured its ripples, and since 2012 with QUIJOTE, two telescopes measuring how its light is polarised. Driving up the observatory road that afternoon, past the white domes of the solar telescopes, Cal pointed out their enclosure.

The figure below is The Van’s receiver, made far better than it is. Then, in the microwaves, turn up the contrast: a thousand times, and one side of the sky is slightly warmer than the other; a hundred thousand times, with that taken away, and the ripples appear.

Fred’s needles

Fred had finished his tea and had another. Now he put the cup down on The Van’s step, very precisely, and began.

“I’ll tell you what I say. The universe never had a beginning and it’ll never have an end. It expands, I grant you, but new matter turns up to fill the gaps, an atom of hydrogen here and there, far too little to notice, so it looks the same at every time. Stars are born, and live, and die, for ever. And stars make light. That glow of yours is starlight.”

“It’s a hot body’s glow,” said John. “Perfectly.”

“Starlight doesn’t stay as it’s born,” said Fred. “Exploding stars throw out iron, and iron cooling in space can grow into needles as fine as threads, which are very good at catching long waves and giving them out again. Fill space thinly with them, give them time, and they’ll knock the starlight about until it comes out as the glow of something at the temperature of space. A perfect glow.” He picked up the cup again. “And the beauty of it is this. Suppose the stars made all the helium in the universe, as I say they did. Making helium sets energy free: that’s what stars run on. Spread all that energy evenly through space and work out the temperature of a glow with that much in it.” He drank. “About three degrees.”

There was a pause.

“Is that right?” said Cal.

“It is,” said Albert. “It is a very good sum.”

“A coincidence, they say.” Fred smiled. “I don’t much care for coincidences.”

It was a good argument, and it deserved better than to be laughed at. The sum is real, and dust can turn starlight into a glow. From the 1970s on, Fred and a few friends worked hard on needles of one kind and another, and the idea was honestly tested.

What killed it was evidence.

The first piece is that the glow was hotter in the past. In Fred’s universe nothing changes, so the glow ought to have been 2.7 degrees ten billion years ago, as it is today. In the expanding universe it was hotter, in exactly the proportion its waves have since been stretched. And that can be measured, because molecules in a cloud of gas are a thermometer, as McKellar’s were: the glow bathing them nudges some into their next level up, and their fingerprint lines say how many. In 2008 astronomers found carbon monoxide in a cloud so far away that its light set out about eleven billion years ago, seen against a quasar behind it. The universe has grown 3.4 times since, so the glow then should have been about 9.3 degrees. The molecules said 9.15, give or take three quarters of a degree, and clouds at other distances agree.

The second is the perfection itself. To smooth starlight into a glow as perfect as COBE’s, the needles would have to soak up nearly all of it, and then they’d be a fog themselves, at these wavelengths: radio telescopes looking out through it would find the distant galaxies dimmed, and the farthest hidden altogether. They see them clearly.

And the third is the ripples, which ring with a note only a hot, young fog could have played. Fred was waiting for those.

“Not bad,” he said. “I’ve lost arguments to worse.” He looked at the snow on the television for a while. “You looked at my novel last time,” he said to John, who went slightly red. “A cloud of gas that turns out to be a good deal cleverer than the astronomers watching it. I’m beginning to think this one is.”

Hotter ahead

In his search for the fault, John had left the television’s aerial lead plugged, by way of a short length of copper, into the back of the speedometer. The speedometer had been mended in Exactly Seventeen Miles an Hour, and since then it had told the truth, except when it had nothing to measure against, when it fell back on its old habit and said 17 mph. Parked in the car park, it ought to have said nothing at all.

It said:

861,000 MPH

Cal looked at it, and then at the car park, and The Van, which was quite definitely parked.

“Relative to what?” he said.

“Everything,” said John.

“Nearly,” said Albert.

The Van had found something to measure against at last: the oldest light. With the contrast turned up a thousand times, as in the figure, the glow is about a thousandth warmer towards one part of the sky and a thousandth cooler in the opposite direction. That’s the Doppler effect of Chasing a Beam of Light: anything moving through a sea of light finds the light ahead squeezed, its waves shorter, so it looks warmer, and the light behind stretched and cooler. The difference says the Sun and everything with it are moving through the oldest light at about 370 kilometres a second. That afternoon the Earth’s yearly circle round the Sun was carrying them partly the same way, which added a little, and the Earth’s turning made the last figures on the speedometer flicker.

It’s the speed John and Cal met at the very start of the first series, in The Day the Mountain Moved: about 1.3 million kilometres an hour, relative to “the faint microwave glow left over from the early universe, which fills the whole of space”.

That was Albert’s “nearly”. The oldest light gives everyone in the universe a common backdrop, the nearest thing to a universal speedometer there will ever be, but it isn’t absolute rest: physics works exactly the same for something at rest in the glow as for something rushing through it, as the first journey of all found. It’s just the most convenient thing to move relative to, because it’s everywhere.

Ripples

With the warm side and the cool side taken away, the oldest light is smooth to about one part in a hundred thousand. Then the ripples begin.

If the glow’s temperature were Teide’s height, about 3,700 metres from the sea to the summit, the ripples would be about four centimetres high: a pebble on the summit. George Smoot’s instrument on COBE found them in 1992, and they’ve since been mapped across the whole sky by NASA’s WMAP satellite, from 2001, and in finer detail still by the European Space Agency’s Planck satellite, from 2009. Planck’s map is mottled all over with warm and cool spots, in every direction.

The spots are places where the fog was very slightly denser or very slightly thinner than average when it cleared. They’re tiny, but they’re the most important thing in the picture. Gravity pulls a little harder where there’s a little more, so a slightly denser place gathers more matter to it, which makes it denser still. Every galaxy in the sky grew from ripples like these. How they did it, given how small the ripples were and how little time there was, needs something the next journey has to find.

But the ripples have more to say first, because they aren’t just random lumps. Before the fog cleared, it was ringing.

The fog rang like a bell

A sound, Chasing a Beam of Light found, is a wave of squeezing passing through something springy, and its speed is set by how springy and how heavy the thing is. The young fog was both. The light trapped in it, knocking against the electrons, pushed outwards wherever it was squeezed, like air in a bicycle pump: that was the springiness. The matter dragged along with the electrons was the weight.

So wherever the fog was a little denser, gravity pulled matter in, the light trapped with it was squeezed, and its pressure pushed back, overshot, and the fog sprang outwards again: a sound wave, travelling out at about half the speed of light. The whole young universe rang with sound waves of every size, the way a bell rings when it’s struck, until the fog cleared. Then the light flew free and stopped pushing, and the ringing stopped. The pattern of the sound at that moment was frozen into the oldest light, and it’s the pattern of the spots.

That gives the spots a size. The biggest common spots are the distance a sound wave could have travelled by the time the fog cleared, which, knowing the speed of sound in that fog and the time it had, works out at a few hundred thousand light-years. That’s a known size. And the distance to the wall is known too. So here is a ruler of known length, laid across the sky at a known distance, and how big it looks says something remarkable: the shape of space itself.

Start with a triangle. On a flat table its three angles add up to 180 degrees, every time. On a ball they add up to more: run two lines from the North Pole straight down to the equator, a quarter of the way round from each other, join them along it, and all three corners are right angles. On a saddle, or one of those curved crisps stacked in a tube, they add up to less. In The Apple That Fell in a Straight Line, John and Cal found that lines as straight as the Earth’s surface allows, starting out parallel at the equator, meet at the poles. Space as a whole could curve like a ball, so that lines spreading out from us bend back towards each other; it could curve like a saddle, so that they spread apart faster than straight lines; or it could be flat.

Teleport to the Relativity moonThe Apple That Fell in a Straight LineStraight lines on a curved surface, and how they meet

Now follow the two lines of light from us back to a spot’s two edges. If space curves like a ball, lines spreading out from us spread more slowly than straight lines would, so to reach both edges they must leave us at a wider angle, and the spot looks bigger than it would in flat space. If space curves like a saddle, they spread faster, need only a narrower angle, and the spot looks smaller. Only in flat space does it look the size ordinary geometry says.

The commonest spots look about a degree across, about twice the width of the full Moon. That’s what flat space gives. It was first measured well by BOOMERanG, a telescope carried by a balloon right round Antarctica in eleven days at the turn of 1999, whose results came out in 2000, and Planck has since measured it so precisely that space can’t be curved by more than a fraction of a per cent either way. On the largest scales we can see, the universe is flat.

The recipe

Flat space says how much there is in the universe, because in general relativity what’s in space sets how it curves. More than a certain amount of matter and energy curves it like a ball; less, like a saddle; exactly that amount, the critical amount, makes it flat. So the universe holds, in all, the critical amount, to within a fraction of a per cent.

The spots say what it’s made of, too. A bell’s note depends on what it’s made of: brass rings differently from glass. The fog’s ringing depended on what was in it, and the pattern of smaller spots, how strong each size is against the others, is a recording of the note.

Ordinary matter, the stuff of atoms, felt the light’s push, so it rang, and its weight mattered. Hang a ball on a spring and set it bouncing: the heavier the ball, the further it sags, and the further its swings go down compared with up. In the same way, with gravity pulling the fog in, the more ordinary matter there was, the deeper its squeezes went compared with its stretches, so that spots of one size come out strong and the next weaker, alternately. From how much they alternate, the ordinary matter can be weighed: about 5 per cent of the critical amount. That’s what the deuterium of the third journey said, from completely different physics, 380,000 years earlier.

But the pattern needs something else as well, about five times as much of it. It pulls, as matter does, but it never felt the light, and didn’t ring. It sat in its clumps, pulling, while the ordinary matter sloshed in and out of them, and without it the pattern comes out wrong. It comes to about 27 per cent. Astronomers call it dark matter, and it’s the next journey’s subject.

“So that’s 32,” said Cal, on the napkin. “And it has to come to a hundred.”

“Then there is 68 per cent of something,” said Albert, “that is not matter at all.”

It doesn’t clump, or it would have rung or pulled, and the spots would show it: it’s spread smoothly through space. And John and Cal had met it before, in Further Apart Every Day, from a completely different direction. In 1998 two teams measuring exploding stars found the universe’s expansion speeding up, and called whatever drives it dark energy. The oldest light, from the shape of space and the ringing, says the same amount of it. Two measurements with nothing to do with each other agree.

Dark energy

Whatever is making the universe’s expansion speed up. It isn’t matter: it doesn’t clump or pull things together, but is spread evenly through space, and it makes up about 68 per cent of everything in the universe. In the simplest description it is an energy belonging to empty space itself, the same in every cubic metre, which is what Einstein’s “cosmological constant” describes. Nobody knows what it really is. It was found in 1998, from exploding stars, and the oldest light confirms how much of it there is.

Albert looked at the napkin for a long time.

“I put that term into my equations once,” he said, “to hold the universe still. And when the universe turned out to be expanding, I took it out again.”

“And now it’s back,” said Fred, “doing the opposite job.”

“So it seems,” said Albert, a little sheepishly.

So the recipe for the universe, from its baby picture, is about 5 per cent ordinary matter, about 27 per cent dark matter and about 68 per cent dark energy. Everything John and Cal have ever seen, every star and planet and doughnut, is in the five.

The ripples carry one more message. Their strength is almost the same at every size, but not quite: each time the size halves, they’re about two and a half per cent weaker. That’s what cosmic inflation predicts, the brief, enormous stretching that, as the second journey found, may have come before everything else. It’s evidence for inflation, not proof: other ideas might yet give the same pattern. The simulator was suitably cautious.

Back to Teide

By evening the clouds below the plateau had broken up, and the sun went down behind La Palma in a long orange glow. Fred stood by his car, keys in his hand, looking up as the first stars came out.

“Ninety-five per cent of your universe,” he said, “is something nobody has ever seen. And you call my needles far-fetched.”

“The needles were very good needles,” said Albert.

“I’ll be back,” said Fred. “And when I am, there’s a story I’ll tell you about carbon.” He got into the car, wound the window down, and pointed at the sky. “Mind you,” he said, “the old fog’s a pretty thing. I’ll give you that.” Then he drove off down the mountain, his exhaust rattling all the way to the first bend.

John and Cal sat on the terrace wall. Albert brought out the last three doughnuts on his cake stand and set it between them, and the napkin with Cal’s sums on it, which said, in three columns:

5, 27, 68

Cal looked at it for a long time. He was thinking of the 27: something five times as heavy as everything in the sky, which pulled and never shone, and had sat in the young fog without ringing.

“What is it?” he said. “The twenty-seven. What is it actually made of?”

Albert turned the cake stand slowly round, so that the doughnuts on it went round too, and watched them.

“Nobody knows what it is,” he said. “But I can show you how we know it is there.”

Out in the car park, The Van’s television was still showing its snow, and about one speck in every hundred was older than any star.
