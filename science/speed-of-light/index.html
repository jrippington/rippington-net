The Van has perfectly serviceable headlights, a defective speedometer, and a passenger who believes that a sufficiently fast vehicle ought to be able to chase its own light. One of these things turns out to be the real problem.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 2 of 14

The headlights question

John was at the café early the next morning, looking like a man who had spent most of the night thinking about headlights.

Albert had put out the last two lemon doughnuts. They were magnificent: a faint crispness to the pastry, a thick coat of lemon sugar that glittered in the low sun, and an outline sufficiently unfortunate that Cal, when he arrived, chose to approach his with a knife and fork.

“Could you stop inspecting it?” John asked.

“I’m considering the safest angle of attack.”

Albert appeared with two coffees. “You have come back with a question.”

“We have,” said John. “The one from yesterday. Suppose The Van is travelling at ninety-nine per cent of the speed of light, and I switch on its headlights. How fast does the light move away from us?”

“About one per cent of the speed of light,” said Cal. “Surely. Light goes at the speed of light, and we’re already doing ninety-nine per cent of it. It would creep out in front of us, like one lorry overtaking another on the motorway.”

Albert smiled. “Surely is a dangerous word in physics.”

“I thought that too, at first,” said John. “But think about the train. If I throw a ball forwards from a moving train, someone standing beside the track sees the ball going at the train’s speed plus the speed of my throw. And yesterday we decided that the inside of The Van is as good a place to do physics as anywhere else. So inside The Van, the headlights ought to work exactly as they do in the car park: the light should leave us at the full speed of light. Which means that someone outside would see it going at the speed of light plus The Van’s speed. Nearly twice the speed of light.”

Cal put down his knife. “So it’s either one per cent of the speed of light, or nearly two hundred per cent.”

“Yes.”

“That’s quite a wide range.”

“Between you,” said Albert, “you have found both of the sensible answers. Unfortunately, the universe has chosen a third.”

John looked at him. “Could The Van ever catch up with its own light, then? If it went fast enough?”

For a moment Albert had a faraway look. Then he took a teaspoon from the counter, dipped its tip into John’s coffee, and drew it slowly across the cup, tapping the surface as he went. Rings of ripples spread out from each tap. In front of the spoon they crowded together; behind it, they spread apart.

“You’ve put your spoon in his coffee,” said Cal.

“Physics requires sacrifices.” Albert laid the spoon on John’s saucer. “Before you ask how fast light moves, you should decide what it is moving through.”

How fast is light?

Nothing in everyday life comes close to the speed of light. Light travels at very nearly 300,000 kilometres every second: in one second, it could go round the Earth about seven and a half times. Sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to cross the 150 million kilometres from the Sun to Teide. Physicists write this speed as c, and it will turn up in almost every journey of this series.

The Van, which has a fantastical engine and very little respect for its handbook, can reach a large fraction of c. It can never reach c itself (we’ll see why later in the series), and that turns out to be the least of John’s problems.

How waves travel

Albert’s question, what light is moving through, sounds like the sort of thing people ask to avoid answering. To see why it is a real question, we need to look at how waves work, starting with the ones we know.

What a wave is

Think of a stadium wave at a football match. Each person stands up, raises their arms and sits down again, a moment after their neighbour does. Nobody leaves their seat. Yet something clearly travels round the stadium, and fast: the pattern of standing up. What goes round the ground is the disturbance the people pass from one to the next, while the people themselves stay put.

Drop a pebble into a still pond and the same thing happens. Rings spread outwards. A leaf floating on the surface bobs up and down as each ring passes, but it doesn’t travel outwards with the rings: when they have gone by, the leaf is more or less where it started. The water in each place only moves up and down a little. The rings are the disturbance being handed on.

Sound works the same way, though you can’t see it. When a siren sounds, it pushes on the air next to it, squashing it slightly. That squashed air pushes on the air beyond it, and so on outwards. Each bit of air only shuffles back and forth a tiny distance, but the squeeze travels outwards through the air, and when it reaches your ear, it pushes on your eardrum and you hear it.

Wave

A disturbance that travels, while the stuff it travels through stays more or less where it is. In a stadium wave, the people only stand and sit; on a pond, the water only bobs up and down; in sound, the air only shuffles back and forth. What moves from place to place is the pattern. Most waves repeat: the distance from one crest to the next is the wavelength, and the number of crests passing a point each second is the frequency. For sound, the frequency is the pitch you hear.

Each of these waves needs something to travel through: the crowd, the water, the air. Take away the crowd and there is no stadium wave. Pump the air out of a jar with a ringing bell inside it, as people have done since the seventeenth century, and the ringing fades to nothing, though you can see the hammer still striking the bell.

Medium

The material a wave travels through: the water for ripples, the air for sound, the string of a guitar for its vibrations. The medium doesn’t travel with the wave; it passes the disturbance along. A wave’s speed is set by its medium (how springy and how heavy it is), and it is measured relative to that medium.

Why a wave doesn’t take its source’s speed

Because a wave’s speed is measured relative to its medium, a wave behaves quite differently from a thrown ball.

In dry air at 20 °C, sound travels at about 343 metres a second, whatever made it: a whisper and a jet engine both send their sound out at 343 metres a second. More surprisingly, it doesn’t matter how fast the source is moving. If a train blows its whistle as it rushes along at 50 metres a second, the sound doesn’t set off at 343 plus 50. Once the whistle has squeezed the air, the squeeze travels through the air at the air’s own speed, and the train’s speed has nothing more to do with it.

A ball is different. A ball thrown forwards from that train does carry the train’s speed with it, as the doughnut on the first journey carried The Van’s. A ball keeps whatever motion it had when it was let go, by its inertia; a wave is handed on through its medium at the medium’s own pace.

And because sound’s speed is measured relative to the air, moving air carries sound along with it. On a windy day, sound travels faster downwind, relative to the ground, and slower upwind. If you are in the air’s frame of reference (in a balloon drifting with the wind, say), sound goes at 343 metres a second in every direction. Stand on the ground and it doesn’t.

Crowding ahead, stretching behind

You will have heard an ambulance’s siren drop in pitch as it passes you: higher as it approaches, lower as it goes away. The siren itself plays the same note throughout. What changes is how often its crests reach you.

Picture the siren sending out one crest of sound, then another a moment later. Each crest spreads out through the air as a growing sphere, centred on the point in the air where the siren was when it sent it. But between one crest and the next, the siren has moved on. So in front of it, each new crest starts out a little closer to the one before; behind it, a little further away. The crests crowd together ahead and stretch apart behind, just like the ripples in John’s coffee.

If you stand ahead of the siren, the crowded crests arrive more often than the siren sends them: you hear a higher pitch. Behind it, they arrive less often: a lower one. An ambulance at 30 metres a second (about 108 kilometres an hour) sounds nearly 10 per cent higher as it approaches and about 8 per cent lower as it goes away, which is a drop of about three semitones as it passes.

The Doppler effect

The change in the frequency of a wave when its source and its receiver are moving towards or away from each other. Moving closer, the crests arrive more often than they are sent, so the frequency rises (a higher pitch, for sound); moving apart, they arrive less often, and it falls. It is named after Christian Doppler, who described it in 1842.

Doppler himself was thinking about light: he suggested that the colours of some double stars might come from their motion. The idea was first tested with sound: in 1845 the Dutch scientist Christophorus Buys Ballot put horn players on a train out of Utrecht, and musicians beside the track heard the note rise as the train approached and fall as it went away.

Who is moving through the air?

Suppose a siren races towards you at half the speed of sound, while you and the air keep still. The crests crowd up so much that you hear the pitch doubled: an octave higher. Now swap. The siren stays still in still air, and you run towards it at half the speed of sound. This time the crests are spaced just as the siren sends them, and you run into them faster: the pitch goes up by only a half. You and the siren approach each other at the same speed both times, yet the pitch differs, because once the siren is moving through the air and once you are.

So the Doppler effect can tell you how fast you are moving through the air: with a good enough ear and a siren you trust, you could work out your speed relative to the medium without looking at anything else. A medium gives you something to measure your speed against.

Each crest spreads out from the point in the air where it was sent (the small dots mark those points), so you can see them crowd ahead and stretch behind. The second panel is the same scene relative to the siren itself, as if you were riding on it. Near 0.9 of the speed of sound, the crests ahead pile up almost on top of each other. What is light a wave of?

In the first decades of the nineteenth century, experiments with light passing through narrow slits convinced most physicists that light, too, is a wave. Light spreading out from two slits makes stripes of bright and dark where the two sets of waves reinforce and cancel each other, just as ripples from two pebbles do on a pond. Thrown particles don’t do that.

But a wave of what? The answer came from somewhere nobody expected: a laboratory bench with no light on it at all.

Two numbers from a laboratory bench

You have met electric and magnetic forces, even if you have never thought of them that way. Rub a balloon on your jumper and it will lift your hair: that’s an electric force, between electric charges. Bring a magnet near a compass and the needle swings: that’s a magnetic force. During the nineteenth century, physicists found that the two are closely related. An electric current, which is just electric charge on the move, behaves like a magnet: it swings a compass needle, and two wires carrying currents pull on each other, or push each other apart.

How strong are these forces? That comes down to two numbers, and you can measure both on a laboratory bench. One says how hard two electric charges push or pull on each other at a given distance. The other says how hard two electric currents pull on each other through their magnetism. These numbers are as much a part of the laws of nature as the strength of gravity. Every charge and every current in the universe obeys the same two, whatever battery or magnet it comes from.

In 1856, two German physicists, Wilhelm Weber and Rudolf Kohlrausch, measured them, with a charged glass jar and a coil of wire. When they put the two numbers together, out came something peculiar for a pair of measurements of forces: a speed, about 310,740 kilometres a second.

Nobody knew what to make of it. Then the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell worked out the equations that tie electricity and magnetism together, published in full in 1865. They showed that a changing electric force makes a magnetic one, and a changing magnetic force makes an electric one, so the two can keep each other going and travel off through space together as a wave, with nothing else needed to carry them. And the speed of that wave, Maxwell’s equations said, is set by the two numbers from the bench: it is exactly the speed Weber and Kohlrausch had found. The best measurements of the speed of light at the time were about 315,000 and 298,000 kilometres a second. The match was far too close to be a coincidence, and Maxwell concluded that light is itself one of these waves. He was right. Radio waves, X-rays and the warmth you feel from a fire are the same kind of wave, at different frequencies.

Electromagnetic wave

A wave of electric and magnetic forces, in which each change in one makes the other, so that the pair keep each other going and travel through space with nothing else to carry them. Light is one; so are radio waves, microwaves, X-rays and the warmth of a fire, which differ only in their frequency. In empty space they all travel at the same speed, c, and that speed is fixed by how strong electric and magnetic forces are.

Look at what went into that speed: a charged jar, a coil of wire and two forces measured on a bench, with no lamp, no star and nobody watching a beam go by. The speed of light isn’t a separate fact about light. It is the speed that the laws of electricity and magnetism give any such wave, so wherever those laws are the same, the speed of light must be the same too.

Relative to what?

Still, a speed has to be relative to something. Sound’s 343 metres a second is relative to the air. Maxwell’s equations give one speed for light, c. Relative to what? The obvious answer, and almost everyone’s answer at the time, was that light must be a wave in some medium, even if nobody could see it, feel it or bottle it, and that c is light’s speed relative to that medium.

The luminiferous ether

The medium that nineteenth-century physicists believed must carry light, as air carries sound (“luminiferous” means light-bearing). It would have to fill all of space, even the emptiest vacuum, and be stiff enough to carry waves at c, yet offer no resistance at all to the planets moving through it. Light’s speed would be c relative to the ether. No experiment ever found it, and modern physics has no place for it.

The ether was odd, but it promised more than something for light to wave in. Remember what a medium does: it gives you something to measure your speed against. If space is full of ether, and light goes at c relative to the ether, then the ether is the universe’s ultimate frame of reference. Measure light’s speed in different directions, and you would find your own speed through the ether: your absolute speed, which John and Cal went looking for on the first journey and couldn’t find.

The Earth goes round the Sun at about 30 kilometres a second, about one ten-thousandth of c. Even if the Sun happened to be at rest in the ether, the Earth couldn’t be, at least not all year round. Somewhere on Earth, at some time of year, there should be an “ether wind”, and light should go slightly slower into it and slightly faster with it.

Why light’s speed must be the same for everyone

“That,” said John, “would fix our speedometer.”

“The speedometer,” said Cal, “has never once measured anything.”

“Exactly. It needs something to measure our speed against. If there’s an ether, the headlights will tell us how fast we’re going through it.”

Albert took a small brass compass from his apron pocket and set it on the counter, beside the sugar bowl. The needle swung, settled, and pointed firmly at the coffee machine, which said more about the coffee machine than about north.

“Before you go,” he said, “tell me about your first journey. You wanted to know whether The Van was moving, without looking out of the window. What did you try?”

“Throwing a doughnut,” said Cal. “Dropping a screw. Pouring the coffee.”

“And?”

“Nothing. Everything in The Van behaved the same, however fast it was going.”

“Did you try one of these?” Albert tapped the compass. “Or a battery, or a magnet? Anything electric?”

“The kettle’s electric,” said John. “It boiled exactly as slowly as it always does.”

“Then you have done Maxwell’s experiment without knowing it.” Albert slid the compass towards him. “Think about what your kettle just told you.”

Albert’s argument has four steps, and you already know each of them.

First: nothing inside The Van can tell you that it’s moving. That was the lesson of the first journey, the principle of relativity. In a closed room moving steadily, without speeding up, slowing down or turning, every experiment comes out exactly as it would at rest. John and Cal tested it with doughnuts and screws, but the principle covers every experiment there is, electric and magnetic ones included. And we have tested that part on an enormous scale, because we live on a moving planet. The Earth carries us round the Sun at about 30 kilometres a second, and six months later it is going just as fast in the opposite direction: between March and September your velocity through space changes by about 60 kilometres a second, which is dozens of times faster than a rifle bullet. Yet every compass, battery, electric motor and radio on Earth works in exactly the same way all year round. If moving changed how electricity and magnetism behave, someone would have noticed long ago.

Second: so the two numbers from the bench are the same in The Van. Suppose John took a charged jar and a coil of wire into The Van and measured Weber and Kohlrausch’s two numbers there. He must get the same two numbers as in the café car park, however fast The Van is going. If he got different ones, he would have built exactly what the first journey showed can’t exist: a speedometer that works with the curtains closed. He could read off how fast he was moving from an experiment on a bench, without ever looking outside.

Third: so the speed of light is the same in The Van. Those two numbers fix the speed of light. The same numbers give the same speed. So light travels at c relative to The Van: measured with The Van’s own rulers and clocks, The Van’s headlight beam leaves it at the speed of light, however fast The Van is going, just as it would in the car park. Cal’s light creeping away at one per cent of its speed can’t happen.

Fourth: and the same for everyone. Nothing in that argument was special to The Van. The probe, drifting outside, can do the same experiment, and must get the same two numbers, so light travels at c relative to the probe as well. So John’s light going at nearly twice the speed of light can’t happen either. And the same goes for anyone and anything else moving steadily. Light’s speed isn’t c relative to the air, or the ether, or the lamp that sent it. It is c relative to whoever is measuring it, whatever their own motion.

There is only one way out. It is to say that the principle of relativity is wrong for electricity and magnetism: that the two numbers have their true values in just one special frame of reference, the ether’s, and that moving through the ether changes them, so a careful enough experiment would reveal your speed through it. It is a reasonable idea, and exactly what the ether would mean. But it makes a prediction: somewhere, at some time of year, a careful experiment on the moving Earth should find the ether wind. People looked for it, very carefully indeed, as we’ll see.

So the choice is stark. Either there is a special frame of reference, which electricity and magnetism can detect and nothing else ever has, or the speed of light is the same for everyone. There is no third possibility.

Cal looked at the compass for a while.

“So the light will leave us at the speed of light,” he said, “however fast we’re going.”

“Yes,” said Albert.

“And the probe will see the same light going at the speed of light.”

“Yes.”

“But we’ll be moving, relative to the probe.”

“You will.”

“That,” said Cal, “can’t be right.”

“Then go and measure it,” said Albert, and took his compass back.

Ninety per cent of light speed

They were back in The Van, which was hanging in the same quiet stretch of empty space as before. The engines were off and the kettle was, for once, behaving itself.

Cal opened the cupboard behind the kettle, where they kept the probe, and where, it turned out, there were also two identical instruments, each in a dusty box labelled LIGHT SPEED (EXPERIMENTAL). Each was a slim bar with a tiny light sensor at either end, thirty centimetres apart, both wired to the same very fast clock, so that it could time how long a flash of light took to go from one sensor to the other. Light covers thirty centimetres in about a billionth of a second, which seemed ambitious, but the boxes looked confident.

They clamped one instrument to the bar that stuck out in front of The Van, which Cal had always assumed was for bumping into asteroids. It pointed straight ahead, so that a flash from the headlights would run along it. They clamped the other along the probe’s painted ruler.

“So that’s what the ruler was for,” said Cal.

Then they fed the probe out through the airlock, as before, and watched it drift away, fire its little thruster once and stop, its green light blinking, a few metres off. It was going to be the outside observer again: the person standing beside the track.

First, a small experiment. John pressed The Van’s horn. Inside the cabin, which had air in it, the horn was deafening. The probe, which had a microphone, heard nothing at all. Between The Van and the probe there was no air to carry the sound.

“No medium, no sound,” said Cal, when his ears had recovered.

John flicked the headlights. The probe saw them perfectly. One of the headlights worked; the other only worked when it felt like it, but one was enough.

The plan was simple, at least by The Van’s standards. The Van would back off a long way, turn round, and race back past the probe as fast as it could go. At the moment it passed the probe, John would flash the headlights. The flash would race ahead along the bar in front of The Van, and The Van’s instrument would time it. A moment later the same flash would run along the probe’s ruler, and the probe’s instrument would time it too. Two measurements of one flash of light: one by The Van, which was moving very fast, and one by the probe, which was keeping still. Or, as Cal would have insisted, which The Van was moving very fast relative to.

The Van backed away until the probe was a speck. John turned it round and opened up the engine. The cabin filled with a rising whine and a smell like a toaster thinking about something else.

The dashboard said:

MAXIMUM SPEED: 90 PER CENT OF LIGHT SPEED. FOR HIGHER SPEEDS, PLEASE CONSULT YOUR DEALER.

“Ninety per cent will do,” said Cal.

The speedometer, with great confidence, said seventeen miles an hour.

The Van flashed past the probe in rather less time than it takes to describe it, and John flicked the headlights.

For a moment nothing seemed to happen. Then the navigation computer, which had been working things out, put up a message:

HEADLIGHT BEAM SPEED: 569,605 KM/S

WARNING: BEAM EXCEEDS THE SPEED OF LIGHT. PLEASE CONTACT YOUR DEALER.

“It’s added the speeds,” said John. “Our speed plus the light’s. Like the ball on the train.”

“It has also,” said Cal, “not measured anything. It’s just done a sum.”

They looked at what the instrument on the front bar had actually measured. The flash from their own headlight had left them at 299,792 kilometres a second: the full speed of light, exactly as it would have in the café car park.

The probe’s report came through a few seconds later. Its instrument had timed the same flash, as it ran along the painted ruler: 299,792 kilometres a second.

Cal read it twice.

“We were travelling at nine-tenths of the speed of light when the headlights came on.”

“Yes,” said John.

“And the light still left us at c.”

“According to our measurement.”

“And the probe, which we were rushing past, also measured c. For the same flash.”

John stared at the display for some time.

“Albert,” said Cal, “is going to be unbearable about this.”

“Then somebody’s ruler or clock must be doing something strange,” said John.

“Possibly both,” said Cal.

What the experiments found

John and Cal’s experiment is imaginary. The one way out of Albert’s argument, a special frame of reference that electricity and magnetism can detect, could be tested, because it predicts an ether wind. So could the other guess John made at the café: that light from a moving lamp goes faster, like a ball from a train. Both were tested, for real.

Looking for the ether wind

In 1887, in the basement of a building at what is now Case Western Reserve University in Cleveland, Ohio, Albert Michelson and Edward Morley set out to measure the ether wind. Their idea is easiest to see with a river.

Imagine two equally good swimmers in a river 100 metres wide, flowing at 1 metre a second. Each can swim at 2 metres a second through the water. One swims 100 metres downstream and back; the other swims straight across and back. Downstream, the current helps (3 metres a second); coming back, it hinders (1 metre a second). That trip takes about 33 seconds plus 100 seconds: 133 seconds. The swimmer going across has to aim a little upstream to avoid being swept away, and makes about 1.7 metres a second across: there and back takes about 115 seconds. The swimmer going across and back wins, and only because the river is flowing. In still water, they would tie.

Michelson and Morley’s swimmers were two beams of light, split from one, sent along two arms at right angles to each other, bounced back off mirrors, and brought together again. If the Earth were moving through the ether, one beam would be swimming “along the river” and the other “across” it, and they would get back at slightly different times. When the beams are brought together, their waves add up into a pattern of bright and dark stripes, and the pattern depends very sensitively on the difference in their travel times. Turn the apparatus round, so that the arms swap roles, and the stripes should shift.

To make the effect big enough to see, they folded each beam back and forth between mirrors, giving it a path of about 11 metres, and mounted everything on a heavy slab of sandstone floating on a pool of mercury, so that it could be turned smoothly without being disturbed. With the Earth moving at 30 kilometres a second through the ether, they expected the stripes to shift by about four-tenths of a stripe as they turned it.

They saw a shift of no more than two-hundredths of a stripe, and on average much less: nothing that couldn’t be put down to the apparatus itself. Michelson called the result “decidedly negative”. Later experiments, repeated at different times of year and, in modern times, with lasers and far greater precision, have found no ether wind either. No experiment has ever found that special frame.

(It took a while for everyone to accept that. Some physicists suggested that the Earth drags the ether along with it near the ground; two others, George FitzGerald and Hendrik Lorentz, independently suggested that moving through the ether squashes the apparatus along its direction of motion, by just enough to hide the wind. That suggestion will come back, transformed, in the fifth journey.)

Does a moving lamp’s light go faster?

The second test is of John’s guess. If light from a moving lamp went faster, light would behave like a thrown ball, and the probe should have measured nearly twice the speed of light.

In 1913 the Dutch astronomer Willem de Sitter pointed out that the sky had already answered this. In a pair of stars orbiting each other, each star spends half its time coming towards us and half going away; if its light came faster on the way towards us, then over the many light-years (the distance light travels in a year) to Earth, fast light would overtake slow light sent out earlier, and the pair’s orbits would look impossibly jumbled. Nothing of the kind is seen.

Fifty years later, the experiment was done more directly. In 1964, at CERN in Geneva, a team of physicists made particles called neutral pions travelling at more than 99.97 per cent of the speed of light. A neutral pion lives for a tiny fraction of a second and then turns into two flashes of gamma rays, which are light of a very high frequency. Here, then, was a lamp moving at almost the speed of light, sending light out forwards: The Van’s headlights, done for real. The team timed the gamma rays over a known distance. They travelled at 299,770,000 metres a second, give or take 40,000, which is the ordinary speed of light. The lamp’s enormous speed had added nothing at all.

Einstein’s second rule

A few years after Michelson and Morley’s experiment, and long before the pions, a teenager in Switzerland was worrying about the same thing. At about sixteen, Einstein, then at school in Aarau, imagined chasing a beam of light at the speed of light itself. If you could keep up with it, he reasoned, you would see the wave standing still beside you: a frozen pattern of electric and magnetic forces, going nowhere. Nobody had ever seen such a thing, and Maxwell’s equations don’t allow one. In other words, for someone riding alongside the light, the laws of electricity and magnetism would have to be different: the same argument Albert gave John and Cal at the café. Einstein said much later that this puzzle was where his thinking about relativity began.

In 1905, at twenty-six, he published the answer, in a paper called “On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies”. He built it on two rules.

The first was the principle of relativity, from the first journey, extended from mechanics to all of physics, electricity, magnetism and light included: the laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame of reference, so no experiment in a closed, steadily moving laboratory can reveal its motion.

The second was about light: in empty space, light always travels at the same speed, c, however fast the thing that sent it out is moving. That is what Maxwell’s equations say, and what de Sitter’s double stars and, later, the CERN pions showed.

Put the two together and you have the conclusion John and Cal reached at the café: everyone moving steadily, whatever their speed, measures the same flash moving at c. There is no room left for an ether wind, and no use for the ether at all. Einstein’s paper said so in as many words: the ether would prove to be “superfluous”.

The constancy of the speed of light

Einstein’s second postulate: light in empty space travels at the same speed, c, whatever the motion of the lamp that sent it. Combined with the principle of relativity, it means that every observer moving steadily, in any inertial frame of reference, measures the same speed c for any flash of light, whatever their own motion. Since 1983 the speed has been fixed by definition at exactly 299,792,458 metres a second, and the metre is defined from it.

The Doppler effect for light

Now go back to the figure of the siren and press Light in empty space. It is the same idea, a source sending out a flash at every tick of its own clock, now done with light, with the probe and two watchers where the air and the listeners were.

Look first at the panel relative to the lamp itself. With sound, riding on the siren meant feeling the air stream past, and the crests were swept back by that wind. With light there is no wind, however fast the probe flies past. Every flash spreads out at c, in every direction, centred on the lamp. Then look at the panel relative to the probe. Each flash spreads at c from the place where the lamp was when it sent it, so the flashes still crowd ahead and stretch behind: light has a Doppler effect too, and it is how astronomers measure how fast stars and galaxies are moving towards or away from us. Ahead the light is bluer; behind, redder.

Both panels are right, which ought to feel very strange indeed. The same flash is a circle centred on one point relative to the probe, and a circle centred on a different point relative to the lamp. With sound, both panels agreed on where each crest came from: a fixed point in the air. With light there is no air, and each frame finds light spreading at c from wherever the lamp was, in that frame, when it flashed.

And try the swap. With sound, a listener running towards a still siren heard a smaller rise than a siren racing towards a still listener. With light, a watcher flying towards a still lamp sees exactly the same rise as when the lamp flies towards the watcher. Only their speed relative to each other matters. The Doppler effect, which for sound could tell you your speed through the air, can’t tell you your speed through anything at all when it comes to light. Light gives you no medium to measure your speed against: the speedometer is still broken.

(Look closely at the numbers in the words under the figure, and you’ll find something else. At 60 per cent of the speed of light, the crowding alone would make the flashes arrive two and a half times as often, but the watcher ahead sees them only twice as often. The difference is the lamp’s own clock, which, measured by the probe’s clocks, runs slow. That is the subject of the next journey.)

For the mathematically inclinedThe Doppler effect in numbers

The universe refuses to add

In ordinary life, if a train travels at 30 metres a second and a passenger throws a ball forwards at 10 metres a second, relative to the train, the person beside the track measures about 40 metres a second. Adding speeds like this is so natural that it hardly seems to need a name, but it has one: Galilean addition. It works splendidly at everyday speeds.

It cannot work for light. Everyday addition says that The Van and the probe, moving relative to each other, must disagree about the speed of the same flash: the probe should get 1.9 c if light behaves like a ball from a train, or The Van should get 0.1 c if it behaves like sound in air. They both get c.

A second figure puts all three to the test. It shows the flash from The Van’s headlights, in two panels again: as the probe sees it, and as The Van sees it. Each frame has laid out its own ruler, marked every 100,000 kilometres, with a clock at every mark. The clocks in each frame have been set going together, so that they all read zero at the moment of the flash. As the light passes each mark, the clock there records the time, and distance divided by time is the light’s speed, as that frame measures it. (The Van and the probe are drawn far larger than they are, or you wouldn’t see them at all on a ruler hundreds of thousands of kilometres long. And how you set clocks a hundred thousand kilometres apart going together is a much more interesting question than it sounds. It gets a journey of its own.)

Like a thrown ball is John’s guess: the probe’s clocks would record the light arriving far too early, at nearly twice the speed of light. Like sound in air is Cal’s: here The Van sees its own light creep away. As measured is what nature does: each frame’s clocks record the light at c. Change The Van’s speed, all the way up to 0.95 c (rather more than the dashboard allows), and nothing about the measured speed changes. And watch the gap between The Van’s nose and its own light. Relative to the probe, at 0.9 c, The Van is chasing its own flash, and the gap grows at only a tenth of c. Relative to The Van, the same gap grows at the full speed of light.

Relative to the probe, The Van is almost keeping up with its light; yet The Van sees the same light pull away at the full speed of light. A speed is a distance divided by a time. If two people measure the same flash and get the same speed, when everyday reasoning says they can’t, then the distances or the times they measure, or both, can’t be what everyday reasoning assumes. Somebody’s ruler or clock, as John said, must be doing something strange.

So the useful question isn’t “How does the light know how fast the lamp is moving?” It is “Why did we ever assume that people moving relative to each other must agree about distances and times?” At the speeds of trains and ships, nobody had needed to check. The next few journeys check, one at a time, starting with clocks.

For the mathematically inclinedAdding velocities without breaking light

Can The Van catch its own light?

One detail of “the speed of light” can mislead. Light slows down in glass and water: in water it travels at about 225,000 kilometres a second, and in ordinary glass at about 200,000, which is what makes a lens bend light and a straw look broken in a glass of water. But a material, like the air for sound, has a frame of its own, in which that slower speed is measured. The speed that is the same for everyone is c, light’s speed in empty space.

And c turns out to be more than the speed of light. It is built into the way space and time fit together, as the next few journeys will show, and it is the fastest that anything at all, a particle, a signal or a cause, can go. Light travels at it. The Van, for all its fantastical engine, can only creep closer and closer to it, for reasons the seventh journey explains. And so, to answer John’s question at the café, no: The Van can never catch up with its own light. However fast it goes, its light leaves it at c.

Back to Teide

The Van came home before sunset, with the same number of doors it had left with. On the way down, the speedometer had briefly shown a small picture of a lighthouse, and then gone dark.

Albert was wiping the counter with an air of deliberate innocence.

“Well?” he asked.

“The light left us at c,” said John. “And the probe measured it at c too. Same flash.”

“And the ether?”

“Superfluous,” said Cal.

Albert looked, for a moment, rather pleased with someone.

“The headlights aren’t the problem,” said John slowly. “It’s our idea of space and time.”

Albert nodded once.

Outside, sunlight broke through a small gap in the clouds and lay across the dark rock of the plateau. It had left the Sun about eight minutes and twenty seconds earlier and crossed 150 million kilometres of almost nothing, without needing air, or an ether, or anything else to carry it. Anyone at all, flying past the Earth at any speed they liked, would have measured it going at exactly the same speed.

Cal watched it move across the table. “If light always goes at c,” he said, “and we and the probe were both right…”

“…then our clocks can’t both be behaving the way we thought,” John finished.

Albert reached below the counter and brought out a little wooden box. Inside, facing each other, were two small mirrors.

“I wondered,” he said, “when you would ask about clocks.”
