How much a mass bends light

For a ray passing a distance bbb from the centre of a mass MMM (not too close, and not too massive: what’s called the weak-field limit), general relativity predicts a deflection, in radians, of

α=4GMbc2,\alpha = \frac{4GM}{bc^2},α=bc24GM​,

where GGG is Newton’s gravitational constant and ccc the speed of light. A larger mass bends light more; light passing twice as far away is bent half as much.

For light grazing the Sun, M=1.99×1030M = 1.99 \times 10^{30}M=1.99×1030 kilograms and bbb is the Sun’s radius, 6.96×1086.96 \times 10^{8}6.96×108 metres. Then

α=4×6.67×10−11×1.99×10306.96×108×(3.00×108)2≈8.5×10−6 radians.\alpha = \frac{4 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.99 \times 10^{30}}{6.96 \times 10^{8} \times (3.00 \times 10^{8})^2} \approx 8.5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{radians}.α=6.96×108×(3.00×108)24×6.67×10−11×1.99×1030​≈8.5×10−6 radians.

A radian is about 206,265 arcseconds, so this is about 1.751.751.75 arcseconds. The calculation Soldner made with Newton’s gravity, and Einstein’s of 1911, give 2GM/bc22GM/bc^22GM/bc2: exactly half.

The article’s other numbers. The Sun’s gravity at its surface is GM/R2=6.67×10−11×1.99×1030/(6.96×108)2≈274GM/R^2 = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.99 \times 10^{30} / (6.96 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 274GM/R2=6.67×10−11×1.99×1030/(6.96×108)2≈274 metres per second per second, about 28 times the Earth’s 9.8. And since an arcsecond is 1/206,2651/206{,}2651/206,265 of a radian, a two-pence coin, 25.9 millimetres across, covers one arcsecond from 25.9 mm×206,265≈5.325.9\ \text{mm} \times 206{,}265 \approx 5.325.9 mm×206,265≈5.3 kilometres away, and 1.75 arcseconds from about 3 kilometres.

Why twice

Where gravity is weak, general relativity’s rule for clocks and rulers, the metric, is very nearly

dτ2=(1+2Φc2)dt2−(1−2Φc2)dℓ2c2,\begin{aligned} d\tau^2 = {} &\left(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) dt^2 \\ &- \left(1 - \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) \frac{d\ell^2}{c^2}, \end{aligned}dτ2=​(1+c22Φ​)dt2−(1−c22Φ​)c2dℓ2​,​

where dℓ2=dx2+dy2+dz2d\ell^2 = dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2dℓ2=dx2+dy2+dz2 is the square of a small distance and Φ=−GM/r\Phi = -GM/rΦ=−GM/r is the gravitational potential, the depth of the well of Time Runs Faster Upstairs. The first part says how fast clocks run; the second, how gravity stretches space. Light records no time at all, dτ=0d\tau = 0dτ=0, so by the clocks and rulers of someone far away it covers

dℓdt=c 1+2Φ/c21−2Φ/c2≈c(1+2Φc2)\begin{aligned} \frac{d\ell}{dt} &= c\,\sqrt{\frac{1 + 2\Phi/c^2}{1 - 2\Phi/c^2}} \\ &\approx c\left(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) \end{aligned}dtdℓ​​=c1−2Φ/c21+2Φ/c2​​≈c(1+c22Φ​)​

each second. Φ\PhiΦ is negative, so by this faraway accounting light near the Sun is slightly slower, and the two parts contribute equally: one Φ/c2\Phi/c^2Φ/c2 from the slower clocks, one from the stretched space. (Anyone near the Sun, timing the light with their own ruler and clock, still finds ccc.)

That is the marching line. A wavefront that is slower on one side turns towards that side, as light does entering glass at a slant, as though space had a refractive index n≈1−2Φ/c2=1+2GM/rc2n \approx 1 - 2\Phi/c^2 = 1 + 2GM/rc^2n≈1−2Φ/c2=1+2GM/rc2. For a ray passing at a distance bbb, with lll measured along the ray from its closest approach, r=b2+l2r = \sqrt{b^2 + l^2}r=b2+l2​, and the ray turns towards the mass by

α=∫−∞∞∣∂n∂b∣dl=2GMc2∫−∞∞b dl(b2+l2)3/2=2GMc2×2b=4GMbc2.\begin{aligned} \alpha &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \left|\frac{\partial n}{\partial b}\right| dl \\ &= \frac{2GM}{c^2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{b\, dl}{\left(b^2 + l^2\right)^{3/2}} \\ &= \frac{2GM}{c^2} \times \frac{2}{b} = \frac{4GM}{bc^2}. \end{aligned}α​=∫−∞∞​​∂b∂n​​dl=c22GM​∫−∞∞​(b2+l2)3/2bdl​=c22GM​×b2​=bc24GM​.​

With the clocks alone, n≈1−Φ/c2n \approx 1 - \Phi/c^2n≈1−Φ/c2, and the same sum gives 2GM/bc22GM/bc^22GM/bc2: the half that the torch in the lift gives.

The size of an Einstein ring

The size of an Einstein ring needs the distances as well. For a lens of mass MMM at a distance DLD_LDL​, a source at a distance DSD_SDS​ behind it, and DLSD_{LS}DLS​ between them, the ring’s radius on the sky is the Einstein radius

θE=4GMc2 DLSDLDS.\theta_E = \sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2} \, \frac{D_{LS}}{D_L D_S}}.θE​=c24GM​DL​DS​DLS​​​.

For a galaxy of about two million million Suns, counting its dark matter, halfway to a source 6 billion light-years away, this comes to about 3 arcseconds. (In an expanding universe the distances have to be the right kind of cosmological distance, which changes the number somewhat but not the idea.) And when the source isn’t perfectly lined up, but sits an angle β\betaβ off the line through a point-like lens, its two images appear at angles

θ=β±β2+4θE22\theta = \frac{\beta \pm \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\theta_E^2}}{2}θ=2β±β2+4θE2​​​

from the lens’s centre, one on each side. With θE=2.97\theta_E = 2.97θE​=2.97 arcseconds and β=1\beta = 1β=1 arcsecond, that gives images 3.51 arcseconds out on the source’s side and 2.51 arcseconds out on the other, which is what the figure in the article shows at its start.

A star as the lens. For one Sun, 4GM/c24GM/c^24GM/c2 is about 5.9 kilometres. Put the lens star 1,000 light-years away, halfway to a star behind it, so that DLS/(DLDS)=1/(2DL)D_{LS}/(D_L D_S) = 1/(2D_L)DLS​/(DL​DS​)=1/(2DL​):

θE=5,9002×9.5×1018≈1.8×10−8 radians,\begin{aligned} \theta_E &= \sqrt{\frac{5{,}900}{2 \times 9.5 \times 10^{18}}} \\ &\approx 1.8 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{radians}, \end{aligned}θE​​=2×9.5×10185,900​​≈1.8×10−8 radians,​

about 0.004 arcseconds: hundreds of times smaller than the arcsecond or so to which the Earth’s air blurs every star, which is why Einstein thought no telescope would show one. The ring grows with the square root of the mass, and a galaxy has about a million million times a star’s, so even at the far greater distances of galaxies its ring is a few arcseconds in radius, as Zwicky saw.
