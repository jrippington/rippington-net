A galaxy seen twice

The next night, the sky over the plateau was as clear as it ever gets, which on Teide is very clear indeed.

The café had closed. Its last light made a small yellow square on the car park, and beyond it the ground fell away into a darkness so complete that the stars seemed to come right down to the rocks. The Milky Way stood up out of the caldera like smoke from a fire that had gone out a long time ago.

On The Van’s roof, where a less ambitious vehicle would have kept a ladder, sat a telescope. John had bolted it there himself, in the face of considerable advice. It had a camera at the back, which took long, patient pictures of faint things, and beside the camera a narrow slot of glass, a spectrograph, which could take the light from any one object and spread it out into a rainbow. The rainbow from a star or a galaxy is crossed by fine dark and bright lines, at colours set by the atoms that made or absorbed the light, and the pattern is as particular as a barcode: two objects with the same lines in the same places are, very nearly always, made of the same things, and moving in the same way.

That night the telescope was behaving suspiciously. It was pointed at a faint, distant galaxy, so far away that its light had set out long before the Earth existed, and it showed two of them. Two tiny, slightly stretched smudges, a few seconds of arc apart, each looking very much like the other.

John examined the lens cover. “Perhaps we’ve installed two lenses by mistake.”

“That’s not how telescopes work,” said Cal.

“Have you checked?”

“Yes. Also, the two pictures have matching features and spectra.”

He had pointed the spectrograph at each smudge in turn. The two rainbows lay one above the other on the screen, and every line in one had its partner in the other, at the same colour.

“Then it’s the software,” said John. “It’s doubled the picture. Like the wing mirror.”

The Van’s wing mirror had been cracked for as long as anyone could remember, and the crack gave every following car a twin, so that a single lorry behind them looked like two.

“The wing mirror doubles everything,” said Cal. “Every other star on the screen is single. Only the galaxy has a twin.”

The door of the café opened, and Albert came out with two coffees, which he had apparently made after closing especially. He glanced at the display and immediately looked interested.

“Perhaps,” he said, “you are seeing one galaxy by two roads.”

John looked at him. “Light doesn’t choose roads.”

“Then,” said Albert, “you have forgotten yesterday’s apple.”

He set the coffees down on The Van’s bonnet and looked up at the sky for a while.

“Two roads is a lot to begin with,” he said. “Begin with one road and one bend. Last night you told me that light bends by the same amount as everything else.”

“The torch you put in The Van,” said John. “Its beam curved, so light falls like the apple.”

“Then work out how much it ought to bend if it passes very close to the Sun,” said Albert. “And then find a star that is sitting right beside the Sun, and see whether it is where your sum says it ought to be.”

“You can’t see stars next to the Sun,” said Cal. “The Sun’s in the way. And it’s daytime.”

“Not from here,” Albert agreed. “Not by day. Not unless something gets between you and the Sun.”

John’s eyes went, slowly, to The Van.

An eclipse to order

Every so often the Moon passes directly in front of the Sun, and for a few minutes, along a narrow strip of the Earth, day turns to a strange, deep twilight. The sky darkens enough for the brighter stars to come out, and around the black disc of the Moon a pale, wispy glow appears: the corona, the Sun’s thin outer atmosphere, which is always there but normally drowned out by the glare. It happens because of a coincidence of sizes. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, and about 400 times further away, so from the Earth the two look almost the same size, and the Moon can cover the Sun precisely, leaving everything around it visible.

The trouble with total eclipses is that you have to wait for one, and then go to wherever its strip happens to fall, and then hope it isn’t cloudy. Astronomers have done all three for centuries. The Van, on the other hand, could simply go and stand in the Moon’s shadow.

First, though, John did the sum Albert had set him, on the back of an envelope, using the argument from the torch beam: light passing the Sun falls towards it, as anything else would, for as long as it is close. His answer was that light just grazing the Sun’s edge should be bent by a little under one second of arc, and light passing further out by less.

The next day, The Van climbed out of the atmosphere with a noise like somebody emptying a cutlery drawer into a bath, and spent the afternoon getting to the right place: on the far side of the Moon from the Sun, in the long, narrowing cone of the Moon’s shadow. John steered until the Moon sat squarely between The Van and the Sun, and then crept along the line, the Moon growing slowly larger ahead of them, until the last bright sliver of sunlight vanished behind its edge.

The cabin went dark. Around the Moon’s black disc, the corona spread out in soft streamers. And beside it, close in to the hidden Sun, there were stars.

Cal had brought the star catalogue: a long list of where every star in that part of the sky ought to be, measured over many years, at night, when the Sun was somewhere else entirely. The telescope took its pictures. Cal laid the catalogue’s positions over them.

They didn’t match.

Every star near the Sun had moved, very slightly, away from it. The stars nearest the Sun had moved the most. A star twice as far out had moved half as much, and a star four times as far out a quarter as much. The nearest star they could see sat a little less than twice as far from the Sun’s centre as the Sun’s own edge, and it was very nearly one second of arc further from the Sun than the catalogue said it should be. By John’s envelope, it should have moved half a second of arc.

A second of arc, or arcsecond, is a very small angle. A full circle is 360 degrees, a degree is 60 minutes of arc, and a minute is 60 seconds of arc, so an arcsecond is a 3,600th of a degree. The full Moon, for comparison, is about half a degree across: about 1,800 arcseconds. One arcsecond is roughly the width of a two-pence coin seen from five kilometres away.

A light came on on the dashboard.

“It’s had that one ready for years,” said Cal. “It was waiting for a reason.”

“The camera’s wobbling,” said John. “Or the catalogue’s wrong.”

“If the camera wobbled, every star would be off in the same direction,” said Cal. “These are all pushed straight out from the Sun, like the spokes of a wheel. And the catalogue’s been checked for a hundred years.” He held up John’s envelope. “Your sum’s got the pattern right. Every star, out from the Sun, less and less further out. It’s just that every one of them has moved twice as far as you said.”

John looked at the picture for a long time: the dark Moon, the pale corona, and the stars standing slightly back from it all, as though the Sun had gently pushed them away.

“It’s the light,” he said at last. “The stars are where they always were, but their light is coming in at a slant. And the torch beam only accounts for half of it.”

Why light should bend

To see why John was right about the light, and where the other half came from, start with something we all take for granted: that light travels in straight lines.

Does light always go straight?

Everything in ordinary life says that it does. Shadows have sharp edges, a torch beam in fog is a straight rod, and you can see round a corner only with a mirror. When a carpenter wants to know whether a plank is straight, they close one eye and sight along it, using light as the ruler; surveyors and builders do the same with telescopes and lasers. For most practical purposes, light is how we define straight.

And there’s a second, deeper reason to expect light to ignore gravity. In Newton’s picture, gravity is a pull, and the size of the pull depends on the mass of the thing being pulled: twice the mass, twice the pull. Light has no mass, so it seems there should be nothing for gravity to pull on.

Both of these instincts turn out to be wrong, and, as John had realised at the end of the tenth journey, the reasons were already in hand.

The torch in the lift

In the eighth journey, Albert had John imagine a torch shining straight across The Van in empty space, while its engine pushed it upwards at one g. While the light crossed the cabin, The Van sped up around it, so inside the cabin the beam curved down towards the floor: far too little to see, because the light is across the cabin almost before The Van has moved, but not zero. A room standing on the ground behaves in every way like a room pushed at one g, so light crossing a room on the ground must curve down in just the same way.

That argument never mentions the mass of the light. The light isn’t being pulled at all. It goes as straight as spacetime allows, along its geodesic, as a thrown ball does, and the room is pushed (or, on the ground, held up) off it. Light really does have no rest mass, as the seventh journey found when it met the photon; what gravity works on is its energy and momentum.

Why you need the Sun

Across a room, the bend is hopelessly small. But the bending adds up the longer light spends near a mass, and the stronger that mass’s gravity. Starlight passing the Sun spends its whole approach falling towards it: the Sun’s gravity at its surface is 28 times the Earth’s (only 28, for something a third of a million times as massive, because its surface is so far from its centre), and the light is in the Sun’s grip, more or less, for the several seconds it takes to cross a few million kilometres around it. The result is still a tiny angle, but now a measurable one. Worked out from the torch-in-the-lift argument, as John did on his envelope, light just grazing the Sun’s edge should be bent by about 0.88 arcseconds. Measured, it is bent by twice that: about 1.75 arcseconds, the width of a two-pence coin seen from three kilometres.

The bending is always towards the Sun. So when the light finally reaches a telescope, it is arriving from a direction slightly further out from the Sun than the star really is, and the telescope, which has no way of knowing that the light has bent on the way, reports the star where the light seems to come from. The star appears to have stepped back from the Sun.

Deflection of light

The bending of a ray of light as it passes a mass. Light passing a star, a galaxy or anything else massive follows a path curved slightly towards it, so it arrives from a different direction than it would have if the mass weren’t there. The bending is bigger for a bigger mass and for light passing closer: twice as close, twice the bending. For light grazing the edge of the Sun it is about 1.75 arcseconds, or about a 2,000th of a degree. It is the same for every colour of light, and for radio waves, X-rays and every other kind of light alike.

A glass prism bends blue light more than red, which is how it makes a rainbow: the glass slows each colour by a different amount. Gravity bends every colour by exactly the same amount, because it changes the paths that everything follows, light of every colour included.

Why the bend is twice as big

John’s envelope had plenty of distinguished company. The idea that gravity might bend starlight is older than relativity. In 1801 the German astronomer Johann Georg von Soldner, treating light as a stream of tiny Newtonian particles, worked out how much the Sun should deflect a grazing ray, and published the answer in 1804. In 1911 Einstein worked it out again, from the equivalence principle alone: the torch-in-the-lift argument, John’s argument. Both arrived at almost the same number as John, which today we would put at about 0.88 arcseconds.

In November 1915, with general relativity finally complete, Einstein did the calculation properly, and got twice as much: 1.75 arcseconds.

The reason for the doubling connects two journeys we’ve already made. The torch-in-the-lift argument captures how gravity affects time: as the ninth journey found, clocks closer to a mass run slower than clocks further away. Treat that alone, and you get the first half of the bending. But general relativity says that near a mass space is curved as well as time. Near the Sun, the geometry of space itself isn’t quite the flat geometry of a school geometry book, and light crossing that curved space is turned by the same amount again. Time gives half the bending, and space the other half.

One way to picture it is a long line of people marching side by side across a field, all keeping perfectly in step. Now suppose the people on the left of the line take very slightly shorter strides than the people on the right. The line doesn’t break, and nobody changes their pace, but the whole line slowly swings round to the left, towards the short-striders. A beam of light is a little like that line: its front edge, its wavefront, is a line of light all moving forward together. Measured with the clocks and rulers of someone far away from the Sun, the side of the wavefront nearer the Sun covers slightly less ground each second than the side further out, partly because clocks run slower there and partly because space there is stretched. So the wavefront turns, towards the Sun.

The picture has its limits. Nobody near the Sun, timing light as it passes with their own ruler and clock, ever finds it going slower than c (the ninth journey insisted on that); the “slower” is an accounting made from far away. And no glass or other material is involved, so every colour turns alike. But the faraway accounting can be measured. Radar echoes from planets, and radio signals from spacecraft, that pass close to the Sun come back slightly late: a delay Irwin Shapiro predicted in 1964, measured most precisely with the Cassini spacecraft in 2002.

Then switch on “The 1919 measurements”, and see what the first people to manage it found.

The 1919 eclipse

The first people to manage the measurement were British, but Einstein’s 1911 prediction, the half value, had already sent one expedition out. In the summer of 1914 the German astronomer Erwin Freundlich travelled to the Crimea to photograph a total eclipse due there on 21 August. He never got the chance. The First World War broke out while he was on his way, his party were arrested as enemy aliens, and their equipment was seized. In hindsight, it may have been a lucky escape for the theory: had Freundlich measured the true deflection that August, he would have found twice what Einstein had then predicted.

By 1917 the prediction had doubled, and Frank Dyson, the Astronomer Royal, had noticed something about the eclipse due on 29 May 1919. On that day the Sun would be standing in front of the Hyades, a cluster of bright stars in the constellation of Taurus: an unusually rich field of stars to measure, close to the Sun, during totality. Dyson and Arthur Eddington, the Cambridge astronomer who had become general relativity’s leading champion in Britain, organised two expeditions to the strip of the Earth where the eclipse would be total. Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson of the Royal Greenwich Observatory went to Sobral, in north-eastern Brazil. Eddington and Edwin Cottingham went to Príncipe, a small island off the west coast of Africa.

The plan was simple to state and fiendishly hard to carry out. Photograph the stars round the eclipsed Sun. Photograph the same stars again, months apart, at night, when the Sun was nowhere near them. Compare the two sets of plates and measure how far each star had moved. On the plates from Sobral’s best telescope, a 4-inch lens that brought the starlight to a focus 19 feet behind it, even the largest shifts came to only a few hundredths of a millimetre.

At Príncipe, the morning of the eclipse brought heavy rain, and cloud was still passing in front of the Sun during totality; only a couple of Eddington’s plates had enough stars on them to use. At Sobral the sky cleared, but the star images on the many plates from the main telescope came out blurred and out of focus, probably because the Sun’s heat had warped the mirror that fed it. The small 4-inch telescope, which had been taken along as a backup, gave the clearest plates of all. The Sobral team stayed on in Brazil until July to take the comparison plates at night.

The results were announced in London on 6 November 1919, at a joint meeting of the Royal Society and the Royal Astronomical Society. Scaled to the Sun’s edge, the Sobral 4-inch plates gave a deflection of 1.98 arcseconds, with an uncertainty of 0.12. The Príncipe plates gave 1.61 arcseconds, with an uncertainty of 0.30. Both were close to Einstein’s 1.75, and both clearly ruled out the half value. The next morning The Times ran the headline “Revolution in Science: New Theory of the Universe: Newtonian Ideas Overthrown”. Within weeks, Einstein was famous all over the world.

The measurements were rather less clean than the headlines suggested. The blurred plates from Sobral’s main telescope gave a lower value, close to the half value, and the team set them aside, judging that the blurring made them unreliable. People have argued about that decision ever since: in 1980 two philosophers of science, John Earman and Clark Glymour, suggested that the data had been chosen to fit the theory. But a re-measurement of the Sobral plates in 1979, with a modern machine, had already shown that even the blurred ones, properly handled, agreed with Einstein, and historians who have gone back through the original notebooks have generally found the team’s judgement sound. The fair summary is that 1919 was a difficult, marginal measurement that happened to come out right, not a crushing proof.

The crushing proofs came later, and they came from radio astronomy. Distant quasars, the brilliant centres of far-off galaxies, give out radio waves, and every year the Sun passes close to some of them in the sky. Radio telescopes thousands of kilometres apart, working together, can measure the directions of those quasars to a tiny fraction of an arcsecond, in daylight and with no eclipse needed. Measurements of this kind, made over decades, now agree with Einstein’s full value to within a few hundredths of a per cent.

General relativity had already passed one test before anyone measured the bending of starlight. Each time Mercury goes round the Sun, the point where it comes closest creeps forward a little, and Newton’s gravity, with every other planet’s tug included, left about 43 arcseconds a century of that creep unexplained. In November 1915 Einstein found that his theory predicted the extra creep, with nothing adjusted to make it fit: a planet’s path and starlight’s, two quite different checks, from the same equations.

Two roads to one galaxy

On the way home, Cal turned the telescope back to the doubled galaxy. Out above the atmosphere, with no air to blur them, the two smudges were sharper than they had been from the car park: two thin, curved slivers of light, a few arcseconds apart, either side of a fainter, rounder glow.

“There’s something in between,” said Cal. “Another galaxy. Much nearer.”

John looked at it, and at the slivers either side of it, and then at the star field round the Moon, still up on the other screen.

“It’s the Sun again,” he said slowly. “Only bigger. And further away.”

A galaxy as a lens

A galaxy is an enormous mass: hundreds of billions of stars, together with gas, dust and a great deal of dark matter, the invisible material that makes up most of a galaxy’s mass and that we know about chiefly through its gravity. Light passing near it is bent, just as starlight passing the Sun is, only far more, because the mass is so much greater.

Now suppose that, by chance, there is a more distant galaxy almost directly behind it. Light from that distant galaxy sets out in every direction. Most of it goes nowhere near us. But some of it passes the nearer galaxy on its left-hand side, and is bent to the right, towards the middle; and some passes it on the right-hand side, and is bent to the left. If the geometry is just so, both of those bent paths can arrive at the same telescope. The telescope receives light from the distant galaxy from two different directions, and, as with the star beside the Sun, it reports the galaxy wherever its light seems to come from. So it shows two galaxies, one on each side of the nearer one. Neither is where the distant galaxy really is.

Gravitational lensing

The bending of light from a distant object by the gravity of a massive object between it and us: a star, a galaxy or a whole cluster of galaxies, which acts as the “lens”. The lens can make the distant object look brighter (magnified), stretch it into arcs, and, when the alignment is close enough, show it to us several times over, because its light reaches us along more than one path. Unlike a glass lens, a gravitational lens bends every colour alike.

Cal found the simulator, three menus down, beneath a game of patience that The Van appeared to prefer. It could model a lens. He gave it a nearer galaxy and a more distant one, and adjusted their positions. He watched two luminous arcs separate into distinct blobs.

“Are they really the same galaxy?” he asked.

“Yes,” said John. “By different roads.”

“So light can bring us several pictures of the same thing? From different directions, and possibly at different times?”

“Yes.”

“Then our sky isn’t a simple map of where things are.”

“Not even close.”

Three quite different things are involved here, which in ordinary life always coincide and here come apart. There is where the distant galaxy actually is. There are the paths its light takes on the way to us. And there are the directions from which that light arrives at the telescope, which is all a telescope can ever measure. With nothing in the way, those three line up: the light comes straight, so it arrives from where the galaxy is. With a lens in the way, the paths bend, and the directions of arrival no longer point at the galaxy at all.

Lining them up

Cal moved the distant galaxy in the simulator, sliding it sideways until it sat exactly behind the nearer one.

The two arcs stretched, reached round towards each other, and joined. Where there had been two pictures of the galaxy, there was now a complete, glowing ring.

When the distant galaxy is exactly behind the lens, there is nothing to choose between left and right, or up and down: its light can pass the lens on any side at all, and be bent inwards by the same amount, and reach the telescope. So it arrives from every direction round the lens at once, at the same angle from the lens’s centre. A point of light becomes a circle.

Einstein ring

The ring of light seen when a distant source, a massive lens and the observer lie almost exactly in a straight line: the source’s light reaches the observer equally from every side of the lens, so its image is spread into a circle round the lens. The ring’s size, its angle on the sky, depends on the lens’s mass and on the distances involved, so measuring it weighs the lens. Einstein worked out its properties in 1936; the Russian physicist Orest Khvolson had described it in 1924.

Einstein himself was not optimistic about seeing one. When he published his calculation in 1936, he was thinking of a star as the lens, and he concluded that the ring would be far too small for any telescope to show, and the alignment far too unlikely. The next year the Swiss astronomer Fritz Zwicky pointed out that a whole galaxy, being so much more massive, would make a ring big enough to see, and would have a far better chance of finding something behind it. Zwicky was right. The first lens of any kind was found in 1979: a distant quasar seen twice, the two images about six arcseconds apart, by Dennis Walsh, Robert Carswell and Ray Weymann at Kitt Peak in Arizona. Rings followed, the first of them found with radio telescopes. In 2025 the European Space Agency’s Euclid telescope found an almost perfect one round NGC 6505, a galaxy about 590 million light-years away, made by the light of a galaxy far behind it that nobody had seen before.

Making a lens for yourself

Cal’s simulator is in the figure below, simplified to its bones. At the top, the view from the side, again hugely out of scale: the telescope on the right, the lens galaxy halfway, and the distant galaxy on the left, with the light’s paths from one to the other. Each path is bent where it passes the lens. The amber rings at the left show where the distant galaxy appears to be, looking straight back along each path as it arrives. Below it, the view through the telescope: the lens galaxy in the middle, a dashed circle showing its Einstein radius, the distant galaxy’s images in amber, and, if you ask for it, a faint dashed outline of where the distant galaxy really is. The model behind it is the standard one astronomers start with: all the bending happens where the light passes the lens, and the lens is either a single point of mass or a simple galaxy with its mass spread out.

Slide the distant galaxy sideways, and watch its two images, A and B, move, always one on each side of the lens and neither where the galaxy really is. Then press “Line them up”, and watch them join into a ring. Make the lens heavier, and the ring grows. Switch to “A spread-out galaxy” and slide the distant galaxy well off to one side: past the ring, the second image vanishes, which is closer to how many real galaxy lenses behave. And notice the brightness: the images together are always brighter than the distant galaxy would be on its own, because the lens gathers light from a wider area of the sky and turns it towards the telescope.

Why one image runs late

There’s one more thing the figure shows, beside the view through the telescope: a record of how bright each image is, day by day. The distant galaxy in the figure has a bright, flickering centre, as many real ones do, and every so often it flares. Watch the record. The flare shows up in image A, and then, some weeks or months later, the same flare shows up in image B.

The two roads aren’t the same length. The light that forms one image travels a slightly longer path than the light that forms the other, and passes through a slightly different depth of the lens’s gravity, where (as the marching line showed) time and space are stretched differently. So the two images show the same galaxy at different moments in its history, one running behind the other.

Real astronomers have measured this. The twin quasar found in 1979 flickers, and its two images flicker in the same pattern, about 417 days apart. In November 2014 the Hubble Space Telescope caught a supernova, an exploding star, in a galaxy behind a massive cluster of galaxies; the cluster’s lensing showed it four times at once. Astronomers worked out from their models of the cluster that another of the galaxy’s images, elsewhere in the picture, lagged behind the others by about a year, so the supernova ought to appear there too. They predicted where and roughly when, and pointed Hubble at the spot. Between November and December 2015, it appeared. The supernova is called Refsdal, after the Norwegian astronomer Sjur Refsdal, who had suggested in 1964 that the delays between lensed images could be used to measure how fast the universe is expanding. (The expansion itself is the subject of the fourteenth journey, the last of the series.)

Weighing a lens

Because the size of an Einstein ring depends on the lens’s mass, measuring the ring weighs the lens, including all the parts of it that give out no light at all. That makes lensing one of the most direct ways we have of finding dark matter: astronomers map how much a galaxy or a cluster of galaxies bends the light behind it, and the bending reveals far more mass than the stars alone could provide. Very massive clusters bend the light of galaxies behind them into great arcs, and magnify them, so that some of the most distant galaxies ever seen have been found through these natural telescopes. Even a single star can act as a lens, brightening a star behind it for a few weeks as the two drift into line; a planet round the lens star adds a brief flicker of its own, and many planets round other stars have been found that way.

The rubber sheet or the lens?

That evening, back at the café, Albert listened to the whole story, all the way from the Moon’s shadow to the ring, and to John’s account of Eddington, the clouds and the blurred plates, polishing a cup as he listened.

“Imagine waiting for that result,” said John. “Years of it.”

“I expect,” said Albert, “that if it had come out the other way, Einstein would have felt rather sorry for the universe.”

Then he took a paper napkin and drew a lens on it: an ordinary glass lens, thick in the middle, with lines of light passing through it and coming together on the other side. Next to it he drew a much larger galaxy.

“Glass refracts light,” he said. “Gravity changes the geometry of the paths.”

John leaned over the napkin. “Could I use the rubber-sheet picture to explain that? The heavy ball on the stretched sheet, and the light rolling round the dip?”

“You could begin there,” said Albert. “But remember what the sheet leaves out.”

It leaves out time, as the tenth journey found, and for light the omission can be measured. Curved space alone gives only half of the 1.75 arcseconds. The other half comes from time, from the slower clocks near the Sun, which the sheet doesn’t show; the torch in the lift is all time and no space, and gets only that other half. Nature needs both, and the 1919 eclipse, for all its difficulties, was the first time anyone checked.

The glass lens on the napkin is a useful picture too, within limits, and it’s where the name comes from. Like a glass lens, a galaxy can gather light, magnify, and form images. But a glass lens bends light more the further from its middle the light passes, which is what it takes to bring it all to one sharp focus. A star or a galaxy does the opposite, at least for light passing outside most of its mass: it bends light most when it passes close, and less and less further out. So the light it gathers is smeared into arcs and rings round it. And glass bends each colour differently; gravity bends them all alike.

“So which one is right?” said John. “The sheet or the lens?”

“Neither,” said Albert. “They are pictures. The explanation is the geometry. The pictures are how you find your way into it.”

Back to Teide

The stars were out when John and Cal went to put The Van’s telescope away. It had not failed at all; it had given them an honest record of the arriving light. The mistake had been theirs: the assumption, so deep that neither of them had ever noticed making it, that every point of light in the sky sits at the far end of one straight line through empty space.

Cal stood by The Van’s open door, looking at the arcs, which were still on the dashboard screen.

“If this is what ordinary galaxies can do to light,” he said, “what happens near something much more compact?”

“Much more compact than what?” John asked.

“A black hole.”

The Van’s navigation system, which had still not withdrawn its resignation, made a small, worried noise anyway, and opened a file called:

LAST WILL AND TESTAMENT

They left it to its paperwork and sat out on the café’s terrace, under a sky that had seemed familiar their entire lives. The Milky Way stood over the caldera, and Taurus was rising in the east with the Hyades in it: every one of those stars, they now knew, had once been seen to step aside from the Sun.

“The stars are still there,” John said. “But the paths their light takes are part of the story.”

Cal nodded. “We aren’t looking through empty nothing. We’re looking through a universe with geometry.”

Albert placed their coffees down.

“The universe,” he said, “does not guarantee that a distant object sends you only one picture.”

“And next?” asked John.

Albert watched The Van’s warning lights flicker across the car park.

“Next, perhaps,” he said, “we should discuss places from which no picture can ever escape.”
