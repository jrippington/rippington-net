Albert spins his cake stand until the doughnuts fly off. Then the simulator spins a galaxy, and its stars go round far too fast for everything anyone can see to hold them. By evening John and Cal have weighed galaxies, clusters and a collision between two clusters, and found the same missing weight every time: about five times as much as all the atoms in the universe, and nobody knows what it is.

The cake stand

Albert’s cake stand was the kind that turns on its base, made for icing wedding cakes. He had bought it, he said, for the doughnuts, though nobody had ever seen him ice anything. In the morning it stood on the counter with seven fresh lemon doughnuts on it, one in the middle and six round the rim, and Albert was waiting for John and Cal beside it, as a man waits who has promised to show somebody something.

“Watch,” he said, and gave it a gentle push.

The stand turned slowly, and the doughnuts went round with it, the six on the rim travelling a long way on each turn and the one in the middle hardly moving at all. Albert pushed again, harder. The stand spun faster, and the doughnuts on the rim began to shuffle outwards, and then, as he gave it one more push, two of them slid to the edge and off. Cal caught one. The other rolled along the counter and stopped against the till.

“What was holding them on?” said Albert.

“The plate,” said John. “The grip of the plate.”

“And when it went faster?”

“It wasn’t enough. Anything going round needs something pulling it in, or it carries on in a straight line. Spin it faster and it needs a harder pull.” John picked up the doughnut by the till. “The plate couldn’t grip hard enough.”

“So if I spun it fast,” said Albert, “and the doughnuts stayed on, you would say…”

“That something was holding them harder than the plate could.”

Albert nodded and put the doughnuts back.

“The galaxies are moving too quickly,” he said.

“Too quickly for what?” said John.

“For the amount of matter we can see.”

“An interesting distinction,” said Cal.

“What is?” said John.

“Between what is there,” said Cal, “and what you can see.”

The night before, the napkin had said 5, 27, 68. Everything that shines, every star and planet and cloud of gas, was in the five. The oldest light had said there was more than five times as much again of something else, which pulled and never shone. That’s a remarkable thing to claim on the evidence of one picture, however perfect, and the first question to ask of a remarkable claim is whether anything else says the same.

Something does. Long before anyone measured the oldest light’s ripples, astronomers had found the same missing weight in galaxy after galaxy, by timing how fast things go round.

How fast things should go round

Start where gravity is simplest, in the Solar System.

Anything going round in a circle is being pulled in. The Earth goes round the Sun because the Sun’s gravity pulls on it; without that pull, it would carry on in a straight line, like a doughnut off a cake stand. How fast something has to go to stay on a circle depends on two things: how hard it’s pulled, and how big the circle is. And the pull, in turn, depends on how much mass is inside the circle.

Whirl a conker round on a string and you can feel it. The faster you whirl it, the harder you have to pull on the string; let go, and it flies off in a straight line. Whirl it twice as fast and you have to pull four times as hard, because there’s twice as much speed to turn aside on every circle, and only half as long to do it in. Make the string twice as long and keep the conker going at the same speed, and you need only pull half as hard, because a wider circle turns more gently. In the sky, gravity is the string, and it gets weaker with distance: twice as far from the Sun, a quarter of the pull.

In the Solar System nearly all the mass is in the Sun: everything else together is less than a seventh of one per cent of it. So the farther a planet is from the Sun, the weaker the pull it feels, and the slower it needs to go to stay in its orbit. Mercury, closest in, goes round at about 47 kilometres a second. The Earth goes at about 30. Jupiter, five times farther out, at about 13. Neptune, thirty times farther than the Earth, at only about 5. The planets slow down with distance, exactly as they must if nearly all the mass is in the middle.

John wanted to see what would happen to a planet going too fast for its orbit. The simulator rebuilt the Solar System on the dashboard screen, the planets drawn far larger than they are, and at John’s request it gave Neptune five times the speed it has, about 27 kilometres a second, nearly as fast as the Earth goes. Neptune’s circle opened out at once into a long curve, and it left. It crossed the edge of the screen and kept going, out past the comets, and the simulator followed it for a while and then gave up.

PLANET NOT FOUND

“The Sun can’t hold it at that speed,” said Cal. “Not out there. Not with only the Sun’s mass inside its orbit.”

“And if it had stayed?” said Albert.

“Then there’d have to be more mass inside its orbit than the Sun,” said Cal. “Five times as fast needs twenty-five times the pull. Twenty-five Suns’ worth.”

That gives astronomers a way of weighing things. Turn the rule round: if you know how fast something goes round and how far it is from the centre, you know how much mass is inside its orbit. The Earth’s speed and its distance weigh the Sun at about two thousand trillion trillion tonnes, without anyone going near it. The same rule works for anything held in orbit by gravity, a moon round a planet, a star round the middle of a galaxy, or a galaxy in a cluster of galaxies.

For the mathematically inclinedOrbital speed and the mass inside

A galaxy isn’t quite like the Solar System, because its mass isn’t all in the middle. A spiral galaxy is brightest in its bulge at the centre and grows fainter outwards, along its arms, until the light fades into the dark. If the light shows where the mass is, a star some way out has a good deal of the galaxy’s mass inside its orbit, and a star farther out has a little more; but beyond the point where the light runs out, there’s nothing more to add, and from there on the stars should slow down with distance, as the planets do.

So in a galaxy the speeds should rise from the middle, peak somewhere in the bright part, and then fall away. Plotting how fast things go round against how far out they are is the way to see it.

Rotation curve

A graph of how fast the stars and gas in a galaxy go round its centre, against how far out they are. Since the speed needed to stay in orbit depends on how much mass is inside the orbit, a rotation curve weighs the galaxy, distance by distance. Where nearly all the mass is in the middle, as in the Solar System, the curve falls with distance. Measured rotation curves of galaxies stay nearly flat far out, beyond where their light fades.

Timing Andromeda

In the 1960s, the Andromeda Galaxy’s speeds were the obvious ones to measure. It’s the nearest big galaxy to our own, about two and a half million light-years away, a great spiral tilted so steeply that we see it nearly edge on, and John had photographed its middle from his garden.

The red knots in John’s picture are the key to measuring it. Each is a cloud of hydrogen, lit from inside by young, hot stars, and glowing in hydrogen’s red line, the sharp colour of Why the Atom Survives and the first journey’s fingerprints. A sharp line is the perfect speedometer. If the cloud is coming towards us, its red line arrives a little bluer; if it’s going away, a little redder: the Doppler effect of Chasing a Beam of Light, and the shift in the first journey’s detectives’ kit. Measure the shift, and you have the cloud’s speed along the line of sight. Since the galaxy is turning, the clouds on one side of the centre come towards us and those on the other side go away, and once Andromeda’s approach as a whole (about 300 kilometres a second) is taken off, what’s left is how fast each cloud goes round.

Vera Rubin, at the Carnegie Institution’s Department of Terrestrial Magnetism in Washington, had already measured a galaxy’s spin this way: in 1965, with Margaret and Geoffrey Burbidge and others, she had measured NGC 7331, which is also in one of John’s pictures. For Andromeda she worked with Kent Ford, who had built a new spectrograph with an image tube, a device that amplified faint light electronically, so that faint spectra could be taken far faster than before. In the late 1960s they took the spectra of 67 glowing clouds of hydrogen, from about 10,000 light-years from Andromeda’s centre to nearly 80,000, out where the galaxy’s light grows faint, and they published the result in 1970.

Teleport to NyxNGC 7331 and Stephan's QuintetJohn’s picture of NGC 7331, the galaxy whose spin Vera Rubin measured in 1965

The clouds far out weren’t slowing down. They were going round at about the same speed as the clouds much farther in, over 200 kilometres a second, where the falling light said they ought to be going much slower.

At first it was one galaxy, and the outer clouds were few. In 1975 Morton Roberts and Robert Whitehurst mapped Andromeda’s hydrogen by its radio note, the 21-centimetre line of Three Pairs of Sunglasses, which comes from cold, dark hydrogen, too cold to glow in visible light at all, and so reaches beyond the edge of the visible disc. The speeds were still flat, farther out than any starlight. Radio astronomers were finding the same in other galaxies, and from 1978 Rubin, Ford and Norbert Thonnard measured spiral after spiral by their light, dozens of them over the next few years, and found the same in every one: speeds rising from the centre and then staying flat, out to the edge of what they could see.

John asked the simulator to show them one of those measurements, made as Rubin, Ford and Thonnard made theirs, in a single exposure. It drew a long, thin strip of a galaxy’s light, taken through a slit laid across it from one side of its centre to the other, with hydrogen’s red line running along it. If the galaxy had stood still, the line would have been straight. It wasn’t. On one side of the centre it was pushed towards the blue, on the other towards the red, steeply near the middle, where the spin builds up. And then, out towards both ends of the slit, where the galaxy’s light was thinning to nothing, the line didn’t drift back towards the middle, as it would have if the outer clouds were slowing down. It stayed where it was.

John asked the simulator to grade it.

Too fast to hold

Put the measurements and the rule together, one step at a time.

  • To stay in an orbit at a given distance, a star must be pulled in by enough mass inside its orbit; the faster it goes, the more mass that takes.
  • The stars and gas far out in Andromeda go round as fast as those farther in. Twice as far out, at the same speed, a star needs only half the pull, because its wider circle turns more gently; but the same mass would pull only a quarter as hard from twice as far. So twice as far out there must be twice as much mass inside: the mass must still be growing, far out, in step with the distance.
  • But the light isn’t growing there. Out where the clouds are, almost all of Andromeda’s starlight is already inside their orbits, so almost everything that shines is inside them too.
  • So there must be mass out there that doesn’t shine, and a great deal of it.

Is there a way out? The measurement could be wrong, but it has been made with visible light and with radio waves, by different people, with different instruments, in hundreds of galaxies, and they all agree. The stars and gas could be flying off, not orbiting at all, but then galaxies wouldn’t last, and they’re billions of years old. Or the rule could be wrong, and gravity might work differently over such enormous distances. That’s a serious possibility, and it comes back later in this journey.

If the rule is right, the only way left is mass nobody can see, spread through and round each galaxy far beyond its stars, in a great unseen ball astronomers call a halo. In a galaxy like Andromeda, within the edge of its starlight, it outweighs everything that shines two or three times over, and it goes on far beyond.

With none, the visible matter’s curve falls away far below the measurements; only with a halo more than twice as heavy as everything that shines does the curve come up to them. Dark matter

Matter that pulls, by gravity, as all matter does, but gives out no light, absorbs none and reflects none, so it can’t be seen at all, only weighed. Its pull shows in how fast stars go round galaxies and galaxies move in clusters, in how much clusters bend light, and in the oldest light’s ripples. There is about five times as much of it as of all the ordinary matter, the stuff of atoms. Nobody knows what it is: it isn’t made of atoms, and most physicists think it’s a kind of particle not yet found.

Our own galaxy is the same. The Sun goes round the Milky Way at about 230 kilometres a second, and stars and gas twice as far out from the centre go round nearly as fast, so the Milky Way, too, sits in a halo of matter nobody can see. John asked the simulator to run it for a billion years with everything that shines and nothing else, the stars keeping the speeds they have. It drew the galaxy’s arms unwinding, its outer stars streaming off into the dark, and a thinned-out, shapeless cloud of stars where the spiral had been.

GALAXY NOT FOUND

It had said something very like that once before, in A Grain of Sugar, about an atom built by the rules of 1911. Both times it was right about the rules it had been given, and both times the rules were missing something.

Why the halo is round

If the halo is matter, why isn’t it a flat disc, like the stars? The answer says something about what dark matter can’t do.

Ordinary matter, falling together, can get rid of its energy. Gas clouds falling into a galaxy collide with one another, heat up, and glow, and the glow carries the energy away, so the gas can settle further in, into the flat, spinning disc where a spiral galaxy’s stars are born. (How falling gas sheds its heat is the next journey’s story, and why spinning things settle into flat discs a later one’s.)

Dark matter can’t do any of that. It doesn’t collide with itself, and it gives out no light, so it has no way of shedding its energy. Whatever it had when it fell together, it keeps, and it goes on swinging in and out on long, looping orbits through a huge, rounded cloud, never settling. So the halo is big, round and puffed out, and the flat disc of stars sits in the middle of it like the yolk of a fried egg. Measured by how its stars and gas move, the Milky Way’s halo reaches out hundreds of thousands of light-years, far beyond its disc.

The halo may be what keeps the disc in one piece. In 1973 Jeremiah Ostriker and Jim Peebles, the same Peebles who had been looking for the oldest light at Princeton, ran computer models of flat discs of stars spinning on their own, and found them unstable: within a few turns they buckled into a bar. A heavy, round halo round the disc steadied it. Spiral galaxies, it seemed, could only have lasted if they sat inside something heavier than themselves.

The smallest galaxies are the most extreme. Some of the faint little galaxies that orbit our own shine with no more light than a few thousand Suns, and their stars move as if the galaxy weighs hundreds or even thousands of times as much as they do: almost nothing but dark matter, with a sprinkling of light.

Clusters too heavy to see

The galaxies weren’t the first place the missing weight turned up. That was in clusters of galaxies, forty years earlier.

In 1933 Fritz Zwicky, a Swiss astronomer at the California Institute of Technology, measured the speeds of galaxies in the Coma Cluster, a great swarm of them hundreds of millions of light-years away, by the shifts of their lines. A cluster is held together by its own gravity, as the Solar System is, and its galaxies move about inside it, falling through the middle and out again. The faster they move, the more mass it takes to keep them from flying off. Zwicky’s galaxies were moving at around a thousand kilometres a second, relative to one another. The mass he could see, from the light of the galaxies, could hold them in only if they moved at about eighty. He wrote that if the result held, there must be “dark matter” (“dunkle Materie”, in his German) present in far greater amounts than the matter that shines. He wasn’t the first to use the words, which earlier astronomers had used for whatever unseen matter there might be among the stars of our own galaxy; he was the first to find so much of it.

In 1936 Sinclair Smith did the same for the Virgo Cluster, the nearest big cluster of galaxies to us, and found its galaxies needed about two hundred times as much mass each as astronomers then thought a galaxy had. He wondered whether the difference might be in some unseen material between the galaxies. John has a picture of the Virgo Cluster’s heart.

For decades the clusters’ missing weight was a curiosity, and plenty of astronomers assumed it would turn out to be ordinary matter they hadn’t found yet. Some of it was. In the early 1970s, X-ray telescopes in space found the big clusters filled with gas so hot, tens of millions of degrees and more, that it glows in X-rays, and there turned out to be several times more of it, by mass, than of all the clusters’ stars together. But the gas only made things worse. Gas that hot should boil away into space unless something holds it in, and its galaxies’ gravity isn’t nearly enough. Weighed by its galaxies’ speeds or by its own heat, a cluster comes out at six or seven times the mass of all its ordinary matter, stars and gas together.

Weighed by its light

There’s a third way to weigh a cluster, which doesn’t depend on how anything moves at all.

In Starlight Bent by the Sun, John and Cal found that mass bends light. The Sun bends the light of stars passing close to it, and a galaxy or a cluster of galaxies, far heavier, bends the light of more distant galaxies behind it into arcs and rings. How much it bends them depends only on its mass, all of it, shining or not. So astronomers can map the mass of a cluster by how it distorts the galaxies behind it, faint background shapes stretched slightly sideways round the mass in the foreground, like the pattern on a tiled floor seen through a curved glass.

The lensing weighs the clusters at the same six or seven times their ordinary matter. Three completely different measurements, the speeds of the galaxies, the heat of the gas, and the bending of light, agree.

The Bullet Cluster

All of that weighs the missing mass. None of it finds the missing mass anywhere apart from the ordinary matter. Inside a cluster the dark matter and the ordinary matter sit in the same place, so anyone who doubts the dark matter can always say that the ordinary matter’s gravity is simply stronger than we think. What would settle it is a cluster in which the two have been pulled apart.

Nature obliged. About 3.8 billion light-years away is a pair of clusters of galaxies that collided: a smaller one passed straight through a larger one, about 150 million years before the light we see now set out. It’s called the Bullet Cluster, after the shape of its gas.

A cluster has three parts, and in a collision each behaves differently.

  • Its galaxies are small and far apart, like the few people in a field, and when two clusters pass through each other, hardly any of the galaxies hit anything. They sail through and out the other side.
  • Its hot gas fills the whole cluster, and it holds most of the cluster’s ordinary matter. When two clusters meet, their gases can’t pass through each other. They crash together, are slowed, and are heated further. In the Bullet Cluster the smaller cluster’s gas, swept back into the shape of a bullet, has been left behind its galaxies, and drives a shock wave ahead of it through the bigger cluster’s gas at about 4,700 kilometres a second.
  • And its dark matter, if it exists, and if it’s something that passes through itself as easily as the galaxies pass through each other, sails through with the galaxies.

So the collision makes a prediction. If there’s no dark matter, and the clusters’ mass is just their ordinary matter, then most of the mass is in the gas, and it should now be in the middle, with the gas, slowed and left behind. If there is dark matter, most of the mass should have sailed on with the galaxies, out ahead of the gas on either side.

The gas can be seen in X-rays, and a team led by Maxim Markevitch had mapped it with NASA’s Chandra X-ray telescope. The mass can be found by lensing, from how the clusters distort the galaxies behind them, and in 2006 a team led by Douglas Clowe mapped it. The mass wasn’t with the gas. It was out ahead of it, on both sides, with the galaxies, by a margin far too large for chance. Clowe’s team called their paper “A direct empirical proof of the existence of dark matter”.

The figure below runs the collision forward, a simple drawing of it, not a simulation of the real one. The bigger cluster stays put, and the smaller one comes through it from the left. Each cluster’s galaxies are dots, its hot gas is the red glow, and the blue lines, like the contours of hills on a map, are where its mass is, as lensing finds it. Scrub through the collision, and watch the gas crash and stay in the middle while the galaxies sail on. Then choose: with dark matter, the mass goes with the galaxies; without it, the mass stays with the gas. Only one of the two matches the measurements.

The real picture is one of the best-known in astronomy: a photograph of the two clusters’ galaxies, with the X-ray gas laid over it in pink, two bright lumps between them, the smaller one bullet-shaped, and the mass from lensing laid over it in blue, two clouds of it, each on its own cluster’s galaxies, well clear of the pink. Nothing could show more plainly that most of the mass is somewhere the ordinary matter isn’t.

What it isn’t

“So it’s a lot of something dark,” said John. “Dust. Dead stars. Black holes.”

It’s the natural first guess, and astronomers spent a long time on it. Dust would glow in the infrared, warmed by starlight, and gas would glow, or absorb, at its own fingerprint colours; neither turns up in anything like the amounts needed. Faint stars, burnt-out stars, planets wandering on their own, and black holes made by dying stars would all be dark. But they would still bend light. If the halo of our own galaxy were made of them, they would now and then drift in front of a star behind them, and the star would brighten for a few weeks as their gravity focused its light, the way Starlight Bent by the Sun described a single star acting as a lens. From the 1990s, two teams, MACHO and EROS, watched millions of stars in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy next to ours, for years, looking for exactly that. They found far too few to make up most of the halo.

Teleport to NyxThe Large Magellanic CloudJohn’s picture of the Large Magellanic Cloud, whose stars were watched for years for dark objects drifting in front

And there’s a stronger reason, which rules out anything made of atoms at all. The first minutes of the third journey and the ringing of the oldest light in the fourth, two completely different pieces of physics, both say that ordinary matter is only about 5 per cent of the universe, about a sixth of all its matter. However the ordinary matter is hidden, as gas or dust or dead stars or black holes made from stars, there isn’t enough of it. The dark matter can’t be made of atoms. It has to be something else.

There’s one more reason it matters, which the next journey takes up. Before the fog cleared, ordinary matter couldn’t clump at all: the light trapped with it pushed back every time gravity pulled it together, and set it ringing instead. So when the fog cleared, at the surface of last scattering, ordinary matter’s ripples were still only one part in a hundred thousand, and from a start that small, by gravity alone, there wouldn’t have been time to build a single galaxy by now. Dark matter never felt the light. It had been gathering into clumps since long before the fog cleared, in the dark, and when the ordinary matter was set free, the clumps were waiting for it.

What it might be

If it isn’t atoms, it’s probably a kind of particle nobody has yet found: something with mass, which feels gravity, but which feels neither light nor the strong force, and so passes through ordinary matter, and through itself, almost without a trace. Billions of them may be streaming through the café every second, through the counter, the doughnuts and John.

Physicists have been looking for them for forty years, since the first search of all, in 1986, in a gold mine in South Dakota. The most sensitive searches are still deep underground, shielded from the cosmic rays that rain down on the surface. In the same mine, now a laboratory, nearly a mile down, an experiment called LZ waits with ten tonnes of xenon, cooled to about a hundred degrees below freezing until it’s a clear liquid, for the faint flash a dark-matter particle would make if it ever knocked into a xenon nucleus. Others wait under the mountains of the Gran Sasso in Italy and at Jinping in China. Between them they’ve ruled out much of the range where the simplest kinds of particle were expected. At the start of September 2026 the LZ team reported a single flash they couldn’t explain, which was intriguing, and they were careful to say they weren’t claiming to have seen dark matter: one flash is still a long way from a discovery. Other physicists search for much lighter particles, with names like axions, by trying to turn them into radio waves in strong magnets. Nothing yet.

The simulator was suitably honest.

Or is gravity wrong?

“Isn’t it simpler,” said Cal, “to say gravity’s different, far out? Then you don’t need a whole new kind of matter nobody can find.”

It’s a fair question, and some very good physicists have answered yes.

In 1983 the Israeli physicist Mordehai Milgrom suggested that gravity might weaken more slowly than Newton’s rule says where it’s extremely weak: at pulls below about a hundred-billionth of the one we feel at the Earth’s surface, which is how weak gravity is in the outskirts of galaxies. He called it Modified Newtonian Dynamics, MOND for short. It was designed to make rotation curves flat, but it does more than that. Given only where a galaxy’s stars and gas are, it predicts its whole rotation curve, galaxy after galaxy, often remarkably well, and it explains a pattern dark matter has to be tuned to match: that a galaxy’s speed far out goes closely with how much ordinary matter it has. Its supporters argue its case well, and the arguments over its newest test, in pairs of stars orbiting each other very widely, where the pull between them is just as weak, are still going on.

“A good idea,” said Albert, “deserves its day in court.”

But MOND has had trouble wherever galaxies aren’t alone. In clusters it still needs extra unseen mass, a good deal of it. In the Bullet Cluster, the pull is where the ordinary matter isn’t, which no rule for the ordinary matter’s own gravity can easily arrange. The oldest light’s ringing, as the last journey found, needs matter that pulls without ringing. And on 17 August 2017, as John and Cal found in Echoes of Spacetime, gravitational waves and light from two colliding neutron stars arrived together after 130 million years, which ruled out a whole family of the theories built to extend MOND to the whole universe, because they had gravity’s ripples travelling by a different road from the light.

So most physicists favour dark matter, not because anyone has caught it, but because a single kind of unseen mass explains galaxies, clusters, a collision and the oldest light at once, while changing gravity explains the galaxies and struggles with the rest. It isn’t settled for everyone, and that’s as it should be until someone catches a particle, or finds a better rule.

Back to Teide

That night the sky was clear, and from the terrace wall the Milky Way rose over the mountain, its pale band crossed by dark lanes of dust. Albert brought out the cake stand with the last of the doughnuts, and set it turning, very slowly, between John and Cal.

“We’re on it,” said Cal, looking up. “A doughnut on the rim.”

They were. The Sun goes round the Milky Way’s centre at about 230 kilometres a second, and its whole galaxy is held together by something nobody can see. Even here, it streams through everything: in the whole of the Earth, at any moment, there’s perhaps half a kilogram to a kilogram of it, about a bag of sugar’s worth, passing through and out the other side at hundreds of kilometres a second, and then more of it, and more, without a trace.

“Five times as much of it as all the atoms there are,” said John. “And all of it in the dark.”

“That is where it has always been,” said Albert. “It was there before the first star. Before the fog cleared, even. Gathering.”

John went out to The Van, set the simulator going from the moment the fog cleared, and asked it to show them what happened next. The screen filled with the oldest light, warm yellow-white and the same in every direction, and as they watched, very slowly, it began to fade.

NOTHING TO SEE FOR SOME TIME. PLEASE WAIT

They waited. Somewhere in the dark, unseen even by the simulator, the dark matter was gathering into clumps, and pulling the gas in after it.
