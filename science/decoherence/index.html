Cal wants to know why he has never seen a doughnut in two states at once. Albert puts one in a box with a radioactive atom, the simulator lets a little air in among the molecules, and The Van's kettle refuses to boil while anyone is watching it. The answer turns out to be all around them, and it explains everything except the one thing Cal most wanted to know.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Field Guide to the Very Small, part 14 of 16

A doughnut in a box

Albert had paid his bet the evening before, with a dozen doughnuts from his own counter and the dignity of a man settling a debt of honour. Then, as the plateau went dark outside, Cal had asked a question that had been building in him for some time. If a photon could be in two states at once, and a pair of them could be linked across the distance to the Moon, why had he never once seen a doughnut in two states?

Albert had taken an empty doughnut box down from the shelf and said that it was a question for a box. In the morning.

He was as good as his word. When John and Cal came in the next morning, there was a small white cardboard box on the counter, beside the grain of sugar under its upturned glass. It was the box Albert used for doughnuts to take away, and it had already done service in more than one thought experiment. This morning it had a round hole cut in one side, just big enough for a hand, and a label on its lid in Albert’s handwriting:

ONE DOUGHNUT. ONE ATOM. DO NOT OPEN

“What’s in it?” said John.

“A doughnut,” said Albert. “A single radioactive atom, of a kind that has an even chance of breaking apart in the next hour. A Geiger counter, which clicks if it does. And a buzzer, which sounds when the counter clicks.” He turned the box round so that the hole faced Cal. “And the means of eating.”

Cal looked at the hole. “Me.”

“You are the most reliable part of the apparatus.”

“Do I have to put my hand in?”

“Not yet,” said Albert. “First, think.”

The arrangement was simple. Cal would put his hand through the hole and wait. If the atom broke apart, the counter would click, the buzzer would sound, and Cal would eat the doughnut. If the atom stayed whole, the buzzer would stay silent, and so would Cal. After an hour, they would open the box and see.

“So the atom decides whether I get breakfast,” said Cal.

“Nobody decides,” said Albert. “That is rather the point.”

A cat in a steel chamber

Albert’s box is a kinder version of the most famous thought experiment in quantum physics.

In 1935 Erwin Schrödinger, whose equation for the electron’s waves turned the eighth journey’s orbits into clouds, wrote a long paper on what he called the present situation in quantum mechanics. It was partly a reply to the paper Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen had published a few months earlier, the one the twelfth journey followed. In the middle of it, to show where the rules seemed to lead, he described one of what he called “quite ridiculous cases”.

A cat is shut in a steel chamber with a device it can’t interfere with: a Geiger counter, and beside it a tiny amount of a radioactive substance, so little that in the course of an hour perhaps one of its atoms breaks apart, and perhaps, just as likely, none does. If one does, the counter clicks, a hammer falls, and the hammer breaks a small flask of poison. The chamber is left alone for an hour.

Quantum mechanics, followed to the letter, describes what is in the chamber after that hour. The atom is in a superposition of having broken apart and of not having done so: both, with an arrow for each, as the fifth journey would put it (its little turning arrows, one for every possibility, whose lengths set the chances). The counter’s click depends on the atom, so the counter is in a superposition of clicked and not clicked. The hammer, the flask and the cat follow along. By the rules, the chamber holds a living cat and a dead one at once, in equal parts.

Schrödinger didn’t believe for a moment that a cat could be like that, and that was his point. The blurred, doubled states of the very small can’t simply be scaled up into a description of the things we can see, and if the rules seem to say they can, something in our understanding of the rules is missing. He meant the cat as an absurdity, and physicists have been arguing about it ever since.

“I always felt sorry for the cat,” said Albert. “Hence the doughnut.”

The doughnut’s box works in exactly the same way. Follow the rules link by link, as Schrödinger did, and after an hour the box holds the atom whole, the counter silent and the doughnut uneaten, and also the atom broken, the counter clicked and the doughnut eaten, both at once. And since Cal’s hand is part of the arrangement, the rules put Cal in it too.

“I’d know,” said Cal. “I would remember whether I’d eaten it.”

“Hold on to that,” said Albert. “It is a better question than you think, and it will keep until this evening. First, the doughnut.”

Both, or one we don’t know?

The trouble with the doughnut in the box is that it looks like any other uncertainty. Until you open it, you don’t know whether it has been eaten. When you open it, you find out. That is exactly how an ordinary uncertainty behaves: a coin under a cup is heads or tails, and lifting the cup tells you which. So what would be different about a doughnut that really was eaten and uneaten at once?

The fifth journey found the difference, and it found that it shows itself in only one way. Ordinary chances only ever add: open the café’s second door, and no fewer customers get in. The arrows of a superposition can cancel. Where “both” really is both, its two arrows can be brought together, so that they cancel in some places and add in others, and stripes appear, as they did behind the two slits. Where it’s only “one or the other, and we don’t know which”, there is nothing to cancel, and there are never any stripes.

So to show that the doughnut was really eaten and uneaten together, you’d have to do with the doughnut what the fourth journey did with electrons: bring its two possibilities back together, and look for stripes.

And the sixth journey found what stops that. When the probe watched the slits, the stripes vanished, and not because the probe’s light pushed the electrons about: a real experiment marked which way atoms went without kicking them at all, and the stripes went just the same. The arrows of two paths can be added only if the paths end in exactly the same way. If anything, anywhere, keeps a record of which way the particle went, the two paths end differently, one with the record saying A and one with it saying B, and their arrows can’t be added. The chances add instead, and the stripes are gone. It doesn’t matter whether anyone ever reads the record. It only has to exist.

“So the question is whether anything keeps a record of the doughnut,” said John.

“Everything does,” said Albert. “Go and see.”

Footballs in a perfect vacuum

They went out to The Van. Its dashboard screen was showing a kettle again, for reasons nobody had ever discovered. Cal hit it firmly in the place that usually worked, and the kettle went away.

A doughnut through two slits was out of the question, as John had found in the fourth journey, throwing them through The Van’s doors. John asked the simulator for the next best thing. In the fourth journey they had met footballs of sixty carbon atoms, sent through slits in Vienna in 1999. The simulator gave them those footballs’ slightly larger cousins, molecules of seventy carbon atoms, a little longer in shape, more like a rugby ball than a football. Each is about one and a half million times heavier than an electron.

The simulator built the experiment in a perfect vacuum. On the left was a little oven, in which the molecules were heated until they boiled off into a thin beam. In the middle was a wall with two slits in it, less than a thousandth of a millimetre apart. On the right was a screen, where each molecule that arrived left a single dot. The molecules came one at a time, often enough to see the screen fill but far too seldom to meet one another on the way.

The dots built stripes, slowly, as the electrons’ dots had built them in the fourth journey. Each of those rugby balls of seventy atoms had come through both slits, its two arrows had met at the screen, and where they cancelled no dots fell.

VACUUM: PERFECT. STRIPES: PRESENT

“Now,” said Albert, who had come out with his coffee, “let some air in.”

Letting the air in

Cal found the simulator’s valve and opened it as little as it would go.

The air is molecules too, mostly of nitrogen and oxygen, flying about at about half a kilometre a second and bumping into everything. When one of them bumps into a carbon molecule on its way to the screen, it bounces off. Where it goes afterwards depends on where it was struck, and so on which slit the carbon molecule came through: from the upper slit, it bounces one way; from the lower slit, a slightly different way. The air molecule flies off carrying a record of which slit. It is the probe of the sixth journey, made of air.

The difference in the bounce can be tiny. It only has to exist. Once that air molecule ends up somewhere different in the two cases, the two paths no longer end in the same way, their arrows can’t be added, and that carbon molecule’s share of the stripes is gone, as surely as if the probe had watched it.

On the screen, the dark bands began to collect dots. Cal opened the valve a little further, and they filled in further, until the screen showed one broad, smooth hump and no stripes at all: the pattern of particles that had each come through one slit or the other.

RECORDS LEFT IN THE AIR: MANY. STRIPES: GONE

“But nobody’s watching the air,” said John. “Nobody’s looked at anything.”

“Nobody needs to,” said Cal. “The record’s there. That was the whole of the sixth journey.”

This was done for real, in Vienna, in 2003. Klaus Hornberger, Markus Arndt and their colleagues sent molecules of seventy carbon atoms through an arrangement of very fine slits in a vacuum chamber, and let in tiny, measured amounts of gas. As the pressure rose, the stripes faded steadily, in just the way the sums said they should if every collision left a record. A single collision was enough to wipe out a molecule’s share of the stripes. And it took remarkably little gas. Air’s pressure is measured in millibars, and at sea level it is about a thousand of them. At about a millionth of a millibar, around a billionth of the pressure of the air in the café, the stripes were all but gone.

Put that billionth another way. The molecules took a few thousandths of a second to fly through the experiment, and at a millionth of a millibar about one air molecule struck each of them on the way. The air in the café is about a billion times denser. In it, a molecule like this would be struck every few trillionths of a second, and every strike would be a record.

Given away by their own glow

Albert asked Cal to close the valve again, so that the vacuum was perfect, and to turn up the oven.

In the second journey John and Cal found that everything warm glows, a fresh doughnut included, and that the hotter a thing is, the more light it gives out and the shorter its waves. Molecules glow too. A hot molecule gives out light a photon at a time, and each photon sets off from wherever the molecule is. If the photon’s wavelength is shorter than the gap between the two paths, then the photon, wherever it ends up, carries a picture of where it came from, just as the light in a microscope does. It is another record.

At first, nothing changed. The molecules were already hot from the oven, but their glow was in the infrared, whose waves are longer than the gap between the paths, and a photon that long can’t tell one path from the other. As Cal turned the heat up, the glow moved towards shorter waves, as the second journey’s had, and there was more of it. The stripes began to fade. When the molecules were as hot as the filament of a light bulb, they were giving out visible light, whose waves are shorter than the gap, and the stripes were gone. The molecules had given themselves away by their own glow.

That was done for real too, in Vienna, in 2004. Lucia Hackermüller and her colleagues heated their molecules with lasers just before they reached the slits. Up to about twelve hundred degrees Celsius, the stripes were untouched. Above that they faded, and by about two thousand seven hundred degrees, as hot as a light bulb’s filament, they had gone. One or two photons of visible light from each molecule, on average, were enough to halve them. The team noted that the same thing should happen to “all macroscopic bodies”. Anything big and warm glows, all the time.

A probe nobody can switch off

The probe in the sixth journey could be switched off, and when it was, the stripes came back. The air, the light and the warmth round everything are a probe that can’t be switched off, and for anything bigger than a molecule they are watching all the time.

That is the whole of the idea, and it has a name.

Decoherence

What happens when something in a superposition leaves records of itself in its surroundings: air molecules that bounce off it, light it reflects or gives out, anything at all that ends up differently depending on which possibility is real. The possibilities can then never be brought back together to interfere, so the superposition behaves, in every experiment anyone could do, exactly like an ordinary “one or the other, and we don’t know which”. For anything big and warm it happens almost at once, which is why everyday things never seem to be in two states. It doesn’t say which possibility you will find.

The idea was worked out from 1970 onwards, first by H. Dieter Zeh in Heidelberg, then by Wojciech Zurek in the United States and others, and in 1985 Zeh and Erich Joos did the sums for specks of dust and other everyday things. What they found can be followed for the doughnut one step at a time:

  • “Both” shows itself, rather than “one or the other”, only by interference: by arrows adding in some places and cancelling in others.
  • Arrows can be added only for possibilities that end in exactly the same way, with no record anywhere of which happened.
  • A doughnut on a counter is struck by about forty million billion billion air molecules every second, bathed in the café’s light, and glowing in the infrared. Every air molecule that bounces off it, and every photon that leaves it, goes somewhere slightly different depending on exactly where the doughnut is and what shape it’s in. Every one is a record.
  • So “eaten” and “uneaten” never end in the same way. Their arrows can never be added, and there can never be stripes between them.
  • So the doughnut in the box, by every test anyone could ever make, behaves exactly as if it were simply eaten or uneaten, and we hadn’t yet looked.

Is there a way out? One is that doughnuts aren’t really in two states at all: perhaps the rules themselves change for big things. That may be true, and it is one of the answers the next journey takes seriously. But nobody needs it to explain why a doughnut never looks doubled. Even if the rules hold for doughnuts exactly as they hold for electrons, the air and the light make certain that nobody could ever see the difference.

What becomes of “both”, then? It doesn’t disappear. It spreads. Before the air arrives, there is the doughnut, in two states. After a single air molecule has bounced off it, there are two whole arrangements, the doughnut uneaten with that air molecule flying off one way, and the doughnut eaten with it flying off another. The doughnut and the air have become entangled, to use the twelfth journey’s word. A moment later it’s the doughnut, the counter, the box, a trillion trillion air molecules and every photon that has left them. To see the stripes now, you’d have to bring both arrangements back together exactly, every air molecule and every photon, which is a good deal harder than stirring the milk back out of a cup of coffee. In principle the two arrows are still there. In practice nothing in the universe will ever add them again.

Cal asked the simulator to try it with The Van. He switched it to quantum mode, in which Planck’s constant is turned up until The Van behaves like an electron:

PLANCK’S CONSTANT: INCREASED. PLEASE DRIVE CAREFULLY

In empty space the simulator was happy to put The Van in two places at once, a hand’s breadth apart, and to keep it there. Then Cal gave it the car park’s air.

VAN IN ONE PLACE. TIME TAKEN: NUMBER TOO SMALL FOR DISPLAY FONT

Even in quantum mode, the simulator couldn’t keep The Van in two places for longer than it took the air to notice. With the real world’s Planck’s constant, the air notices even sooner.

How long would a doughnut last?

How quickly, exactly? The air’s part is easy to estimate: it is simply how often an air molecule strikes the thing, since every strike is a record. For a doughnut in the café, it comes to less than a tenth of a trillionth of a trillionth of a second. Light, the fastest thing there is, would take far longer than that to cross a single proton.

Out in deep space, far from any star, the air has gone, but not everything has. Space is still filled with the faint microwave glow left over from the early universe, the oldest light there is, which John and Cal met in Further Apart Every Day. Even a doughnut frozen to its temperature, three degrees above absolute zero, would be found by it in about a millionth of a billionth of a second. A warm doughnut would give itself away faster still, by its own infrared glow. Only the smallest things, alone in the dark, can keep a superposition for long. An electron in deep space would last a few thousand years, and a molecule of seventy carbon atoms, frozen, would outlast the universe.

That is why experiments putting larger and larger things into superpositions are done in the best vacuums anyone can make, often close to absolute zero: they are trying to keep the surroundings from taking notes.

A kettle that won’t boil

Niels was coming for tea that afternoon, and the café’s tea was not what it might have been. So Albert sent them out to boil water in The Van’s kettle, which was at least reliable.

It was not reliable that afternoon. Cal filled it, switched it on, and stood waiting. After five minutes the water wasn’t even warm, and the dashboard explained why:

KETTLE OBSERVED. BOILING SUSPENDED

John opened the cupboard behind the kettle. The probe was inside, as always, its green light blinking steadily, and one of the cameras on its many faces was pointing straight at the kettle.

“A watched pot never boils,” said Cal.

“There’s a real effect like that,” said John. “It has a name.”

There is, and it’s called the quantum Zeno effect, after Zeno of Elea, the Greek philosopher who argued that an arrow can never move: at every instant it is exactly where it is, and an arrow that is always exactly where it is never goes anywhere. Two physicists, Baidyanath Misra and George Sudarshan, gave the effect that name in 1977, when they showed that, by the rules, looking at a quantum system often enough could stop it changing.

The reason is in the arrows. Think of an atom that has started to change from one state to another. At first, its arrow for “changed” grows steadily from nothing. But an arrow half as long gives only a quarter of the chance (the fifth journey’s maths page has the rule), so at first the chance of finding the atom changed creeps up very slowly: in half the time, a quarter of the chance; in a tenth of the time, a hundredth. Now look, and ask the atom which state it’s in. Nearly always it says “not changed yet”, and looking sets that answer, as the tenth journey found that measuring always does, wiping out the little arrow for “changed” that had grown. The change has to start again from the beginning. Look again a moment later, and the same thing happens. Look often enough, and the change never gets going.

In 1990 Wayne Itano, David Wineland and their colleagues at the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, in Boulder, Colorado, saw it happen. They held about five thousand beryllium atoms, each missing an electron, in a trap, and used radio waves to drive each of them slowly from one state to another. During the change, they flashed laser light at the atoms, a few times or many times, and each flash asked every atom which state it was in. The more often they asked, the fewer of the atoms ever completed the change. Physicists still argue about what exactly the experiment shows: whether it is “looking” that freezes the atoms, or simply the light disturbing them, and whether the difference means anything. The team’s answer was that every way of describing it predicts the same results.

What the effect can’t do is stop a kettle. A kettle boiling isn’t a single delicate change that starts from nothing; it’s heat pouring from the element into some thirty trillion trillion jostling molecules of water, each of them bumping into its neighbours about ten trillion times a second, and every bump a record. There is nothing left for a glance to freeze. Watching a kettle adds nothing to the records the kettle is already making about itself.

The Van’s kettle, as Cal pointed out, was simply broken in a new way. He shut the cupboard door on the probe. A minute later the water was boiling.

Watching a record being made

Decoherence is usually far too fast to see. It is over before anything can watch it happen. In Paris, in the 1990s, Serge Haroche and his colleagues found a way to slow it down enough to watch.

They trapped microwaves, light with waves a few millimetres long, between two mirrors of superconducting metal about three centimetres apart, cooled to within a degree of absolute zero. By the 2000s their mirrors were so good that a single photon could bounce between them for more than a tenth of a second, which doesn’t sound long until you work out that in that time it travels about forty thousand kilometres, once round the Earth. And they could look at the light without destroying it. They sent atoms through the gap one at a time, each atom puffed up to about a thousand times its usual size so that it felt the light strongly, and the light turned each atom’s own arrows a little without ever being absorbed. Each atom carried away a reading.

In 1996, with an earlier and leakier pair of mirrors, Michel Brune, Haroche and their colleagues used one atom to put a field of a few photons into a superposition, a field pointing two ways at once, a “meter”, in their words, “pointing simultaneously towards two different directions”. Then they sent more atoms through, to see how long it lasted. They watched the superposition fade as it happened, as photons leaked through the mirrors and carried records of the field away into the surroundings, and the further apart its two states were, the faster it faded, just as the sums said it should. It was decoherence, measured while it unfolded.

Haroche shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2012 with David Wineland, of the Zeno experiment, “for ground-breaking experimental methods that enable measuring and manipulation of individual quantum systems”.

What decoherence doesn’t explain

So the air explains why Cal never sees a doughnut in two states. It explains why the world looks ordinary: why chairs, doughnuts and vans are always somewhere, why nothing big ever shows stripes, and why superpositions of large things can only be made, briefly, in the dark and the cold. It is not a new law. It follows from the rules of the fifth and sixth journeys, applied honestly to things that are never alone.

But look at what it leaves.

After the air has done its work, the rules still describe the box in two ways at once: the doughnut uneaten, the air arranged one way, the counter silent and Cal waiting; and the doughnut eaten, the air arranged another way, the counter clicked and Cal full. Decoherence guarantees that these two can never meet again, so that each looks, from the inside, like a perfectly ordinary outcome. It doesn’t remove either of them. Yet when Cal opens the box, he finds one. Why that one? And what has become of the other?

Decoherence says nothing about that. It turns “both” into something that looks exactly like “one or the other, and we don’t know which”, but it doesn’t say which, and it doesn’t say what happens to the one we don’t find. For some years physicists hoped it might settle the question completely. Most now agree that it doesn’t, on its own. It explains why the stripes go, and on why one result happens it is silent.

Back to Teide

By late afternoon the coaches had gone back down the mountain, and the light on the plateau had turned long and golden. The box was still on the counter.

“It’s been more than an hour,” said John.

Cal looked at the box, which nobody had touched all day. “There’s no atom in there, is there?”

“Nor a Geiger counter,” said Albert. “Nor a buzzer. The atom was the thought, and the experiment was the thought. The doughnut is real.”

Cal opened the box. The doughnut was there, whole and slightly stale.

“Uneaten,” said John.

“For now,” said Cal, and ate it.

He wiped the sugar from his fingers, and looked at the empty box.

“So the air explains why I’ve never seen a doughnut in two states,” he said. “Everything round it keeps a record, so the two states can never meet again, and each looks like an ordinary outcome.”

“Yes,” said Albert.

“But it doesn’t explain why I found this one. Why it was uneaten, and not eaten.”

“No.”

“Then what happened to the other one?”

Albert looked out of the window. Someone was coming up the terrace steps, unhurried, pausing at the top to look at the mountain before he came in.

“That,” said Albert, “is the question my friend has come to argue about. He has been arguing about it with me for a very long time.” He reached under the counter for the tin of tea he kept for only one customer. “Cal, would you boil The Van’s kettle again? And this time, nobody watch it.”

Niels and Albert were about to spend the whole evening on it.
