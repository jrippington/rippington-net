How fast the air keeps records

How the stripes fade

One record. A molecule that has passed both slits is in the superposition (ψA+ψB)/2(\psi_A + \psi_B)/\sqrt{2}(ψA​+ψB​)/2​ of the two ways through. Suppose an air molecule bounces off it and ends up in the state ∣eA⟩|e_A\rangle∣eA​⟩ if the molecule came through slit A, and ∣eB⟩|e_B\rangle∣eB​⟩ if through B. The two together are in the state

12(ψA ∣eA⟩+ψB ∣eB⟩),\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\big(\psi_A\, |e_A\rangle + \psi_B\, |e_B\rangle\big),2​1​(ψA​∣eA​⟩+ψB​∣eB​⟩),

and the chance of the molecule arriving at a place xxx on the screen, whatever the air molecule does, is

P(x)=12∣ψA(x)∣2+12∣ψB(x)∣2+Re⁡ ⁣[ψA∗(x) ψB(x) ⟨eA∣eB⟩].P(x) = \tfrac12 |\psi_A(x)|^2 + \tfrac12 |\psi_B(x)|^2 + \operatorname{Re}\!\big[\psi_A^*(x)\, \psi_B(x)\, \langle e_A | e_B \rangle\big].P(x)=21​∣ψA​(x)∣2+21​∣ψB​(x)∣2+Re[ψA∗​(x)ψB​(x)⟨eA​∣eB​⟩].

The first two terms are the two one-slit patterns. The last is the stripes, and it is multiplied by ⟨eA∣eB⟩\langle e_A | e_B \rangle⟨eA​∣eB​⟩, how much the air molecule’s two possible states overlap. If they are the same, nothing has been recorded and the stripes are whole. If they are completely different, ⟨eA∣eB⟩=0\langle e_A | e_B \rangle = 0⟨eA​∣eB​⟩=0, the record is complete and the stripes are gone: this is the sixth journey’s rule, that the hands of paths ending differently can’t be added.

Many records, at random. If each record is complete, a molecule shows stripes only if it leaves none. Records come at random, so if a molecule leaves NNN on average, the chance it leaves none is e−Ne^{-N}e−N, and the stripes’ strength, their visibility, falls as

V=V0 e−N,V = V_0\, e^{-N},V=V0​e−N,

as Vienna measured. The screen’s pattern is the mixture

P(x)=e−NPboth(x)+(1−e−N)Pone slit(x).P(x) = e^{-N} P_\text{both}(x) + \left(1 - e^{-N}\right) P_\text{one slit}(x).P(x)=e−NPboth​(x)+(1−e−N)Pone slit​(x).

In the article’s first figure, N=p/p0N = p/p_0N=p/p0​ from the air: at three millionths of a millibar, e−3≈5e^{-3} \approx 5e−3≈5 per cent of the stripes are left.

Records made by glowing. A photon of wavelength λ\lambdaλ given out by a molecule whose two paths are a distance ddd apart overlaps itself in the two cases by an amount that falls from 1 when λ≫d\lambda \gg dλ≫d (no record: the photon can’t tell the paths apart) to nearly 0 when λ≪d\lambda \ll dλ≪d (a complete record, like a microscope’s light). In Vienna the paths were about a micrometre apart. Molecules from the oven at about 900 K glow most strongly at

λpeak=2.9×10−3 m K900 K≈3 μm,\lambda_\text{peak} = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}}{900\ \text{K}} \approx 3\ \mu\text{m},λpeak​=900 K2.9×10−3 m K​≈3 μm,

too long to tell the paths apart, and give out few photons. Heated towards 3,000 K, they give out many more, and visible ones, under a micrometre long. In 2004 Hackermüller and colleagues found the stripes untouched below about 1,500 K (about 1,200 °C), halved at about 2,500 K, where one or two visible photons a molecule do it, and gone by about 3,000 K (about 2,700 °C). The figure uses a round fit to those three numbers, counting the photons’ records as whole records:

How long a superposition lasts

When one record is enough. Light or a gas molecule of wavelength λ\lambdaλ bouncing off something in two places Δx\Delta xΔx apart can record which only if Δx\Delta xΔx is larger than λ\lambdaλ. Then a single bounce is a complete record, and the superposition lasts about as long as the wait for the first:

τ≈1Γ,\tau \approx \frac{1}{\Gamma},τ≈Γ1​,

The figure’s things are in two places a hand’s breadth apart, ten centimetres, which is far longer than the wavelength of air molecules (about 10−1110^{-11}10−11 m) or of the faint glow left over from the early universe (about 2 mm), so τ≈1/Γ\tau \approx 1/\Gammaτ≈1/Γ throughout.

Why looking often freezes a change

How a change begins. Take something with two states, 1 and 2, pushed steadily from one to the other, as radio waves push an atom: its arrow for being in state 2 grows as sin⁡(Ωt/2)\sin(\Omega t/2)sin(Ωt/2), so the chance of finding it there is

P2(t)=sin⁡2 ⁣(Ωt2)≈(Ωt2)2when t is small.P_2(t) = \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right) \approx \left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right)^2 \quad\text{when } t \text{ is small}.P2​(t)=sin2(2Ωt​)≈(2Ωt​)2when t is small.

It has changed completely at T=π/ΩT = \pi/\OmegaT=π/Ω. At first the chance grows as the square of the time: in half the time, a quarter of the chance.

Looking. Interrupt the change nnn times, at equal intervals T/nT/nT/n, each time asking which state it is in. Each look sets the answer and wipes out the coherence between the two states, so that between looks the system starts afresh from wherever the look left it. In terms of the difference w=P1−P2w = P_1 - P_2w=P1​−P2​, a push through the angle θ=π/n\theta = \pi/nθ=π/n on the way between looks multiplies www by cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ, and the look keeps only that. Starting from w=1w = 1w=1, after the whole push

w=cos⁡n ⁣(πn),P2(T)=12[1−cos⁡n ⁣(πn)].w = \cos^n\!\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right), \qquad P_2(T) = \tfrac12\left[1 - \cos^n\!\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)\right].w=cosn(nπ​),P2​(T)=21​[1−cosn(nπ​)].

With no looks in between (n=1n = 1n=1), P2=1P_2 = 1P2​=1: the change is complete. With more:

This is the formula for the experiment of Itano, Heinzen, Bollinger and Wineland (1990), whose beryllium ions were driven by radio waves and looked at with pulses of laser light, and their measurements agreed with it.

Why it does nothing to kettles. The freezing needs a change that starts as a single coherent push, slowly, as t2t^2t2, and looks much more often than the change takes. Water coming to the boil is heat flowing into about 102510^{25}1025 molecules that are each recorded by their neighbours far more often than anyone could look: there is no coherent beginning left to freeze.
