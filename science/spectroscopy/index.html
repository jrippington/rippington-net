John wants to know where everything came from, and Albert tells him to find out first what it is. In 1835 a philosopher declared that nobody could ever know what the stars are made of. Up the observatory road at dusk, with a square of plastic from Albert's cupboard and a simulator that has suddenly become honest, John and Cal find out how wrong he was, and what nearly everything is made of.

A question about the past

Albert didn’t answer John’s question that night. He didn’t answer it the next morning either, or at lunchtime, though John asked it again over his coffee, and again over his soup, in slightly different words each time.

“Where did it all come from?” he said, for the fourth time, as the last of the afternoon coaches pulled out of the car park. “The protons in the grain of sugar, and the carbon, and the stars. You said it wasn’t a question about the very small any more. Then what is it a question about?”

Cal patted his shirt pocket. The grain of sugar was still there, folded into Albert’s napkin, with the words on the outside where he could see them:

DO NOT EAT

“It is a question about the past,” said Albert. He was wiping down the counter in long, slow strokes. “And there is a difficulty with the past. Nobody can go there.”

“Not even in The Van?” said John.

“The Van can visit the future, if you are prepared to go very fast and come home to find everyone older than you. You found that out a long time ago. Nothing can visit the past. Everything that happened before you arrived, you have to work out from what it left behind.”

“Like a detective,” said Cal.

“Exactly like a detective. A detective always arrives after the event, and all he has is the evidence.” Albert folded his cloth. “In this case nearly all the evidence is light. So before you ask where the stars came from, you must ask what their light can tell you about them now. Start with the simplest question of all. What are they made of?”

“Nobody can know that,” said John at once. “You’d need a piece of one.”

Albert looked pleased, the way he did when someone said exactly the wrong thing for exactly the right reasons.

“A very clever man said precisely that,” he said, “in 1835.” He hung the cloth over the tap. “I shall close early tonight. We are going up the road.”

Never, by any means

John’s objection is the obvious one, and it was once the considered view of one of the cleverest men in France.

In 1835 the philosopher Auguste Comte published the second volume of his great course of lectures on what science is and what it can know. He believed that knowledge should rest only on what can be observed, and he wanted to draw a line round the questions science could ever answer. The stars were his example of where the line fell. Their shapes, their distances, their sizes and their motions could all be worked out, he wrote, because those come from watching where they are and how they move. But what they’re made of was another matter: in his words, we would “never know how to study by any means their chemical composition”.

He wasn’t being foolish. To find out what something is made of, you need a piece of it. That’s how chemistry had always worked, and how it mostly still works: you take a sample, and you heat it, dissolve it, burn it and weigh what’s left. Burn sugar in a pan, and the black crust it leaves is carbon and the steam coming off it is water, so sugar contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.

Nobody was ever going to bring back a piece of a star. The nearest one after the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 40 trillion kilometres away: light takes more than four years to cross the distance. In 1835 Comte’s line looked as safe as any line could be. The light from a star was a point of brightness, perhaps tinted a little red or blue, and what could a point of brightness tell you about chemistry?

Comte died in 1857. Within two years he had been proved wrong, by two men in Heidelberg, with clues that had been sitting in ordinary sunlight for half a century.

Up the observatory road

Albert closed the café at six, which nobody could remember him doing before. He came out to The Van in his coat with a paper bag of doughnuts, a flask, and something flat wrapped in a napkin, which he had taken from his cupboard, from somewhere between the tray of currant dough and the oven gloves.

The observatory road leaves the plateau to the north-east and climbs through old lava, black and brown and rust-red, in long bends. The Van climbed it at its own pace, which was the pace of a vehicle that had once been several other vehicles and remembered all of them. Near the top the domes of the observatory came into view along the ridge, white, with their shutters closed. Most of them watch the Sun by day, and a later journey goes to look at them properly.

They stopped at a wide place by the road where the ground fell away to the west. The sun had just gone down into the sea, and the sky above where it had been was still bright, gold low down and pale green higher up. Out on the horizon, past the shoulder of the mountain, lay the long dark shape of La Palma.

Its night is kept dark on purpose. Its mountain, the Roque de los Muchachos, carries some of the world’s best telescopes, and since 1988 a law has limited the lights on La Palma, and on the side of Tenerife facing it, to keep the sky dark for them. For years that meant streetlights glowing in almost a single colour, sodium’s yellow, which the astronomers could simply filter out. John and Cal had found out why one kind of atom gives out only one colour; tonight’s journey turns that fact round.

Cal switched on the dashboard, so that they could use the simulator later. The screen lit up and, instead of the usual kettle, said something new.

“What was it before?” said Cal.

“Confident,” said John.

The simulator had always been able to rebuild anything that had ever been measured. Now, it turned out, it said how sure it was. Everything it showed came with one of three grades. OBSERVED meant that the thing’s light, or some other signal from it, was actually here to be measured. RECONSTRUCTED meant that it had been worked out, from the laws of physics and from evidence. SPECULATION meant that nobody knew.

Cal tried it out on a doughnut from Albert’s bag, holding it up to the camera on the dashboard.

ORIGIN OF THIS DOUGHNUT: RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: LOW

“I made it this morning,” said Albert. “In front of witnesses.”

WITNESSES: NOT FOUND

It’s the distinction every detective has to keep: between what you’ve seen, what you’ve worked out from what you’ve seen, and what you’re guessing. In astronomy, where almost nothing can be touched, the journeys ahead lean on it at every step. The simulator was going to be insufferable about it.

Gaps in the sunlight

Albert unwrapped his napkin. Inside was a square of thin plastic in a cardboard frame, the kind of thing given away at science museums. It was a diffraction grating: a sheet ruled with hundreds of fine grooves to every millimetre, which spreads light into its colours as the tracks on a CD do, only far more cleanly.

He took two of the café’s menu cards from his coat, held them edge to edge with a gap between them no wider than a line drawn in pencil, and put the grating behind the gap. Then he gave the whole thing to Cal.

“Look at the bright sky, low down, where the sun went down,” he said. “Not at the sun. It has gone, but never at the sun, and never through this.”

The glow over the sea was sunlight, scattered by the air, and through the slit and the grating it spread into a long thin rainbow: red at one end, then orange, yellow, green, blue and violet. Cal held it still against the rim of the window and looked for a long time.

“There are gaps in it,” he said. “Thin dark lines. One in the yellow, I think. A few in the green.”

“There are,” said Albert. “Nobody put them there.”

The lines had been in sunlight all along, but it took a while to see them. When Isaac Newton spread sunlight into a rainbow with a glass prism, in 1666, he let it in through a round hole, and the colours from different parts of the hole overlapped and blurred out anything so fine. In 1802 the English chemist William Wollaston let the light in through a narrow slit instead, as Albert had, and saw seven dark lines across the colours. He took them for the boundaries between one colour and the next, and thought no more about them.

Mapping them fell to a glassmaker. Joseph von Fraunhofer was orphaned at eleven and apprenticed to a glass-cutter in Munich, and in 1801, when he was fourteen, his master’s house collapsed on top of him. He was dug out alive after hours in the rubble, and the Elector of Bavaria, who had come to see the rescue, gave him a purse of money. It bought books and his freedom, and within a few years he was making the best lenses in the world, at a workshop in an old monastery at Benediktbeuern, south of Munich.

To make better lenses, Fraunhofer needed to know exactly how much each kind of glass bent each colour, and for that he needed colours sharp enough to measure by. A rainbow’s colours blend into one another with no edges. So he looked at sunlight through a narrow slit and a prism, with a small telescope to magnify the result, and found the dark lines Wollaston had seen, and many more. By 1814 he had mapped 574 of them, each in its exact place among the colours. He labelled the darkest with capital letters, starting with A deep in the red, and used them for the rest of his life as the marks on a ruler.

He found the same lines in moonlight, and in the light of the planets, which is only sunlight bounced back. When he turned his telescope on bright stars, some showed patterns of lines like the Sun’s, and some, Sirius among them, showed different patterns altogether. He noticed something else, and wrote it down: his double dark line in the yellow, which he had labelled D, sat exactly where a lamp’s flame showed a bright yellow line. He never found out what any of it meant. He died in 1826, at thirty-nine.

Cal asked the simulator to show them Fraunhofer’s workshop. It rebuilt a long room with tall windows, a brass telescope on a stand, and a prism on a table in a shaft of sunlight.

Try it

You can see a few of Fraunhofer’s lines for yourself, with a slit and something that spreads light into colours: a diffraction grating like Albert’s, from a science shop, or an old CD or, better, a DVD, whose tracks are closer together. Make the slit from two pieces of card, or cut it in the end of a cardboard tube, as narrow as you can manage. Look through it at a bright white cloud, or at the bright sky, with the grating or the disc held close to your eye, tilted until the colours spread out across the slit’s image. With a narrow enough slit and a steady hand, you may make out a dark line in the yellow and two or three in the green and blue.

Warning

Never look at the Sun, directly or through a slit, a grating, a disc or anything else. Point the slit at a white cloud or the sky well away from the Sun. Looking at the Sun, even for a moment, can permanently damage your eyes.

Bright lines and dark lines

The clue Fraunhofer wrote down was picked up in Heidelberg, more than thirty years later, by a chemist and a physicist who worked in neighbouring laboratories.

The chemist was Robert Bunsen. In 1855 he and his instrument maker, Peter Desaga, had built a new gas burner, with a hole at its base to let air in, whose flame was very hot and almost colourless. (It’s the Bunsen burner of every school laboratory.) A pinch of any substance held in that flame showed its own colour cleanly, without the yellow smoky light of the old burners swamping it. The physicist, Gustav Kirchhoff, suggested that instead of judging the colours by eye, Bunsen spread each flame’s light into its colours with a prism. From 1859, together, they did it with element after element.

Every element gave a few bright, sharp lines of its own, always in the same places, and no two elements gave the same set: the fingerprints of the last series, which John and Cal now knew the reason for. An electron in an atom can only have certain energies. It gives out light only when it drops from one of them to another, and then in a single lump carrying exactly the difference, which sets the colour. So each kind of atom has its own few colours and nothing between them. And sodium’s two bright yellow lines sat exactly where Fraunhofer’s dark D lines were.

Was that a coincidence? In the autumn of 1859 Kirchhoff tried something. He let sunlight pass through a flame with salt in it on its way into his prism. The flame was glowing in sodium’s yellow, so he expected its bright yellow light to fill in the dark D lines. They got darker.

So he took the Sun out of the experiment. He replaced it with limelight, a block of lime heated white-hot in a gas flame, whose light spreads into a smooth rainbow with no lines in it at all. Through the salted flame, the limelight showed two dark lines in its yellow, exactly where sodium’s bright ones would be. Kirchhoff had made Fraunhofer’s D lines on his laboratory bench.

Why a gas makes dark lines

Step by step, from what the last series established:

  • An atom’s electron can climb from one energy level to a higher one only by taking in exactly the energy of the climb. Light comes in lumps, and the lump it takes in must be exactly the right size, which means exactly the right colour: the same colour it gives out when it drops back down.
  • Behind the salted flame is a glow of every colour, the white-hot lime. The sodium atoms in the flame take in that glow’s lumps of sodium yellow, and only those. Every other colour passes through them untouched.
  • An atom that has taken in a lump soon drops back and gives out a lump of the same colour, but in any direction at all. Almost none of them happen to go on in the direction the light was travelling.
  • So the light coming straight through the gas is short of sodium’s colours: two dark lines, exactly where sodium’s bright lines are.
  • Look at the same gas from the side, against the dark, and those re-emitted lumps are all you see: bright lines, in exactly the same places.

Dark lines and bright lines are the same fingerprint, read from two directions. A gas in front of something hotter and brighter takes its own colours out of the light; on its own, against the dark, it shines in them. (The flame is hot itself, of course, and glows yellow on its own account. But the lime behind it is hotter still and far brighter in that colour, so the flame takes out much more yellow than it adds. That’s why the D lines in sunlight got darker: the Sun’s surface is far hotter than a flame.)

Absorption line

A dark line in the colours of a glowing object, where a gas between it and you has taken a colour out of its light. Each kind of atom can take in only the lumps of light whose energy matches a climb between its own energy levels, which are exactly the colours it gives out when its electrons drop back, so its dark lines fall in exactly the places of its bright ones. The Sun’s light is crossed by thousands of them, made by atoms in its outer layers.

The dots are lumps of light. Watch the ones of the gas’s own colours: they’re taken in, and given out again in any direction. Below are two views, one above the other, with their colours lined up. The first is from straight through the gas, looking at the glow: a rainbow with dark lines. The second is from the side, looking at the gas against the dark: bright lines alone. Try each element and see that its dark lines and bright lines always fall in the same places, and that each element’s set is its own. Then take the gas away, and the dark lines go with it.

Is there a way out? Perhaps the dark lines in sunlight are made nearer home, in the Earth’s own air. A few of them are. Fraunhofer’s A and B, deep in the red, are made by the oxygen in our atmosphere, and astronomers can tell them apart: they grow darker when the Sun is low and its light comes to us through more air, and lighter seen from a high mountain or a balloon. But almost all of the lines stay exactly the same however high the Sun is, so they’re made before the light reaches the Earth at all. Or perhaps the matches are coincidences? One line in the right place might be. But when Kirchhoff mapped sunlight against the elements’ lines, line by line, he found iron’s lines by the dozen, and every one of them sat on a dark line in the Sun. A coincidence can’t do that dozens of times over. There’s only one explanation left: the dark lines are made by atoms in the Sun’s own outer layers, cooler than the glowing surface beneath them, taking their colours out of its light on its way to us.

So the pattern of dark lines in a star’s light is a list of what’s between us and its glowing surface. Nobody needs to go there.

Spectroscopy

Finding out about something by spreading its light into its colours, its spectrum, and reading the lines in it: dark lines where a gas has taken its own colours out, bright lines where a gas is shining in them. Since every element has its own set of lines, the lines say what elements are there, and how strong they are, and where they’ve shifted to, say much more besides. It’s how nearly everything known about the stars has been learned.

Chemistry at a hundred and fifty million kilometres

There’s a story told about how the idea came to Bunsen and Kirchhoff, and whether or not it happened just so, it’s the right shape. One evening, from their laboratory in Heidelberg, they could see a fire burning in Mannheim, about twenty kilometres away down the valley. They pointed their prism at it, and in its light they found the lines of barium and strontium. If you can tell what’s burning in Mannheim from Heidelberg, Bunsen is supposed to have asked on a walk afterwards, why not the Sun? People will say we’ve gone mad.

They didn’t go mad. Over the next three years Kirchhoff measured the Sun’s dark lines more carefully than anyone had, and laid each element’s bright lines against them. Sodium was there, as the D lines had said. So were iron, calcium, magnesium, nickel and chromium, and more as the work went on. The Sun, which nobody would ever touch, was made of the same elements as the Earth. Comte had been dead for two years.

The same flames found two elements nobody had ever seen. In 1860, in what was left after boiling down mineral water from the spa at Dürkheim, Bunsen and Kirchhoff found two bright blue lines that belonged to no known element, and named the element caesium, from the Latin for sky-blue. The next year, in a mineral, they found deep red lines and named rubidium, from the Latin for red. For the first time, elements had been discovered by their light alone, before anyone had held a grain of them.

Then the stars. In London, in an observatory he had built onto his house, William Huggins fitted a spectroscope to his telescope, and in 1863 and 1864, with his friend the chemist William Allen Miller, he found the dark lines of sodium, iron, calcium, magnesium and hydrogen in the light of bright stars such as Aldebaran, Betelgeuse and Sirius. The stars were made of the same stuff as the Sun and the Earth. And on the evening of 29 August 1864 he turned his spectroscope on a faint glowing patch in the constellation of Draco, a nebula, half expecting it to be a crowd of stars too far away to see one by one. Instead of a rainbow crossed by dark lines, he saw a few bright lines on the dark. It was glowing gas.

Bright lines in John’s pictures

That’s the reason some of John’s pictures look the way they do. A nebula, a cloud of thin gas lit up by hot stars, is gas seen against the dark of space, and it shines in its bright lines: hydrogen’s deep red, oxygen’s blue-green, sulphur’s other red, each a narrow slot of colour. A filter that lets through only one of those slots lets through only one element’s light.

John’s picture of Thor’s Helmet, made with a telescope in Chile that he used remotely, was taken through three such filters, one for hydrogen, one for oxygen and one for sulphur, and it shows each element in a colour of its own: in this picture, blue is oxygen.

Oxygen’s line had a strange history. The brightest line Huggins saw in his nebula in 1864 was green, at a place where no element on Earth had any line, and for sixty years nobody could match it. It was given an element of its own, “nebulium”. Then, in 1927, the American physicist Ira Bowen showed that it was oxygen after all: oxygen that has lost two of its electrons, giving out a colour it can only give out in gas far thinner than any laboratory could make, where an atom can go undisturbed for long enough. Henry Norris Russell, the most influential astronomer in America, put it neatly: “Nebulium has vanished into thin air.” That doubly stripped oxygen is what John’s oxygen filter lets through. It takes the hard ultraviolet light of a star as hot as the one inside Thor’s Helmet to strip oxygen of two electrons, which is why the bubble round it glows blue.

His picture of the Eastern Veil, from his garden in Oxfordshire, is in colours close to natural, and the same atoms show there too. It’s the bright edge of the blast from a star that exploded about 20,000 years ago, still ploughing into the gas round it. Where the blast slows in a denser cloud, the gas it has heated glows as it cools, and oxygen glows first, at about 80,000 degrees, then hydrogen, at about 7,000. So all along the arc a thin edge of oxygen’s blue-green runs just ahead of hydrogen’s red.

An element from the Sun

On 18 August 1868, a total eclipse of the Sun crossed India, and the French astronomer Jules Janssen watched it from Guntur, with a spectroscope. When the Moon covers the Sun’s bright face, the flames of glowing gas round its edge, the prominences, can be seen, and Janssen spread their light into its colours. As a gas against the dark sky, they shone in bright lines: hydrogen’s red, and others, and among them a bright yellow line very close to sodium’s but not quite on it, a little further towards the green.

Janssen saw the lines so clearly that he realised he needed no eclipse at all to see them, and he spent the following days studying the prominences in full sunlight. That October, in London, Norman Lockyer saw the yellow line too, without knowing of Janssen’s work, and concluded that it belonged to no element known on Earth. He named the element helium, from the Greek for the Sun. Many chemists doubted it for a generation. Then, in 1895, the Scottish chemist William Ramsay treated a uranium mineral with acid and collected the gas it gave off, and when the gas was made to glow, it shone in the same yellow line. Helium was found in the sky twenty-seven years before it was found on the ground.

“So nebulium was a mistake,” said Cal, “and helium wasn’t.”

“The light is always honest,” said Albert. “It is the reading that needs care.”

The helium story hides a puzzle. Helium’s lines show in the prominences, but they don’t show among the dark lines of the Sun’s ordinary light, though the Sun is full of helium. Why not?

Mostly hydrogen

By now it was dark. The last of the glow had gone from the western sky, and the stars were out in their thousands, bright and steady in the thin, still air. High in the west was a brilliant blue-white star.

“Vega,” said Albert. “Twenty-five light-years away. Try it with the probe.”

The probe came out of its cupboard behind the kettle, looking, as it always did, slightly surprised to be needed. It was the only thing on board that had never broken down. Cal fixed Albert’s grating over one of its cameras with a piece of tape, set the probe on The Van’s roof and pointed it at Vega, and the probe sat perfectly still and took a long exposure. On the dashboard screen appeared a short streak of colour: Vega’s light, spread out. Across it were four dark lines, strong and wide, and hardly anything else.

VEGA’S LIGHT: OBSERVED. SET OUT 25 YEARS AGO

John knew those four. They were hydrogen’s: its deep red, its blue-green and its two violets, the four colours of the fingerprint from the last series, dark here instead of bright.

“So Vega’s made of hydrogen,” he said. “And the Sun isn’t. Its colours had dozens of lines and none of them were much like these.”

“Hydrogen’s lines are in the Sun’s light too,” said Albert, “but they are not strong.”

“Then the Sun’s mostly iron and sodium and calcium and so on.” John was warming to it. “Like the Earth. It makes sense. The strong lines are the elements there’s most of.”

It’s what nearly every astronomer believed until the 1920s. The Sun’s light is crossed by the lines of dozens of elements, iron’s most of all, and in 1914 Russell compared the elements in the Sun’s lines with the elements in the Earth’s rocks and found them much the same. It was natural to suppose that the Sun was made of roughly what the Earth is. It was also wrong, and when a young astronomer showed why, she was told she must be mistaken.

Payne’s twist

Cecilia Payne came from Cambridge, in England, to the observatory at Harvard in 1923, at twenty-three. Harvard had hundreds of thousands of photographs of stars’ spectra on glass plates, sorted into a sequence by the strength of their lines, and nobody yet understood why the lines changed from one star to the next. In 1920 the Indian physicist Meghnad Saha had worked out how heat strips atoms of their electrons. Payne put the two together.

The key is that a dark line needs atoms ready to make it. Hydrogen’s four visible lines are all climbs from the second rung of its ladder of levels, as the last series found: they’re the drops onto the second level, run backwards. So a hydrogen atom can take in one of those colours only if its electron is already on the second rung. Left alone, its electron sits on the bottom rung, and getting it up to the second takes a hard knock, far harder than a typical knock from a neighbouring atom in the Sun’s outer layers, at about 5,500 °C. In the Sun only about one hydrogen atom in two hundred million is on the second rung at any moment. The rest can’t take in hydrogen’s visible colours at all. So hydrogen’s lines in the Sun are modest, though the hydrogen is there.

At Vega’s surface, at about 9,300 °C, the knocks are harder, and about one hydrogen atom in a hundred thousand is on the second rung, about seventeen hundred times as many as in the Sun. Its lines are dark and wide. Go hotter still, to a star like Bellatrix, in Orion, at about 22,000 °C, and the knocks are so hard that most of the hydrogen loses its electron altogether. An atom with no electron has no ladder to climb, and hydrogen’s lines fade again. Hydrogen’s visible lines are strongest at about 10,000 degrees, and how strong they are in a star says more about its temperature than about how much hydrogen it has.

Every element works the same way, each with its own best temperature. Helium’s visible lines need a climb from a rung so high that only the knocks in stars far hotter than the Sun can put enough helium atoms on it. That’s why the Sun’s ordinary light shows no helium, while the hotter gas of the prominences, and the light of hot stars, do. The darkest lines in the whole of the Sun’s visible light are calcium’s, two at the violet end, made by calcium atoms that have lost one electron and sit on their lowest rung, where the Sun’s temperature suits them perfectly. The Sun has about one calcium atom for every half million of hydrogen, and yet, at its temperature, it has about four hundred times as many calcium atoms ready to make calcium’s dark lines as hydrogen atoms ready to make hydrogen’s.

Allow for all that, star by star, as Payne did, and the stars turn out to be made of nearly the same mixture, hot or cool, blue or red. And the mixture is astonishing. Her numbers made hydrogen about a million times as common as all the metals, iron and the rest, and helium about a thousand times. The stars weren’t made of what the Earth is made of at all.

Russell read her thesis in 1925 and wrote to her that it was “clearly impossible” for hydrogen to be a million times more common than the metals. Payne was a young researcher with her career ahead of her, and Russell was the man whose word counted most. When the thesis was published, it said that the enormous abundance she had found for hydrogen and helium was “almost certainly not real”.

It was real. Over the next four years Russell came at the question by a different route, and in 1929 he published his own result, crediting hers: the Sun’s atmosphere is mostly hydrogen. Decades later, the astronomer Otto Struve called her thesis the most brilliant ever written in astronomy.

Today’s measurements say that the Sun is about three quarters hydrogen and a quarter helium, by weight, with less than two per cent left over for everything else: the oxygen, carbon, iron and all the rest. Counting atoms instead of weighing them, about ninety-two in every hundred are hydrogen, and nearly all the others are helium. The Earth, by contrast, is mostly iron, oxygen, silicon and magnesium, with hardly any hydrogen or helium at all. The stars are made, almost entirely, of the two lightest elements there are.

There’s one more piece of honesty to add, and the simulator added it without being asked.

VEGA’S SURFACE: OBSERVED. VEGA’S INSIDE: RECONSTRUCTED

A star’s lines are made in its outer layers, the last few hundred kilometres of gas its light passes through on its way out. They say nothing directly about its core, where it shines, and which has been changing for as long as the star has lived. What’s inside a star is worked out from what’s outside it and the laws of physics, and those working-outs come in later journeys. What the light reads directly is the skin.

Cal sat with his hand on his shirt pocket.

“So the hydrogen in this grain,” he said, “is the commonest stuff there is.”

“By a very long way,” said Albert.

Where it came from is the question of the third journey. First, John wanted to know what else the light could tell them.

The detectives’ kit

They sat in The Van with the door open and the flask of Albert’s coffee going round, and John wrote a list on the back of the bag the doughnuts had come in. It was everything a detective can read from a star’s light without ever going near it.

The first is colour, which gives the temperature. Hot things glow, and the hotter they are, the further their glow moves from red, through yellow and white, to blue: the last series found it in the lava under the café and in an oven with a hole in its door. A star’s colour is a thermometer reading. Vega’s blue-white says about 9,300 °C; the Sun’s white, about 5,500.

The second is the lines, which give what it’s made of: this journey’s discovery, with Payne’s warning about temperature attached.

The third is where the lines are, which gives the motion. When a source of waves comes towards you, its waves arrive crowded together, and when it goes away, stretched out: the siren of a passing ambulance drops in pitch, and light does the same thing in colour, bluer coming, redder going. It’s the Doppler effect of Chasing a Beam of Light. On its own, a star’s colour couldn’t show it, because a star could simply be a slightly bluer star. But its lines are a fingerprint whose exact places are known from the laboratory, so if every one of them has moved a little towards the blue, the star is coming closer, and by how much says how fast. Vega’s lines are shifted very slightly towards the blue: it’s coming towards us at about fourteen kilometres a second.

The fourth is brightness, which, with the distance, gives the power. A lamp twice as far away looks a quarter as bright, because its light has spread out over four times the area by the time it reaches you, as the paint from a spray can covers four times the wall from twice as far away, and more thinly. So a star’s faintness alone can’t say whether it’s a feeble star nearby or a mighty one far away. Know its distance, though, and its brightness says how much light it’s giving out in all. Vega gives out about fifty times as much visible light as the Sun. How astronomers measure the distances, a much harder problem, is part of the next journey.

The fifth is the light’s travel time, which gives the age of the view. Light takes time to arrive, so a star is seen as it was when its light set out: Vega as it was twenty-five years ago, Thor’s Helmet as it was about 13,400 years ago, when Britain was warming at the end of the last ice age. Every one of John’s pictures on Nyx ends by saying how old its light is. The further away, the further back. The most distant light of all set out when the universe was young, and Further Apart Every Day found that the universe’s growth has stretched it redder on the way, by just as much as the universe has grown.

Nearly everything in the journeys ahead comes from those five readings. Lately there are other messengers too, which aren’t light at all: ghostly particles called neutrinos, which come straight out of the Sun’s core and through the Earth, and the ripples in spacetime the relativity series heard from colliding black holes. Each comes in its own journey. But most of what anyone knows about the universe, it knows from light, spread into its colours and read.

“Comte said we’d never know,” said John.

“Comte said a great many things,” said Albert. “He was right about most of them, and it is the wrong ones everybody remembers. That is the trouble with never.”

Back to Teide

They drove down in the dark, slowly, with the probe back in its cupboard and the grating back in Albert’s napkin. Below them the plateau was black, and the café, when they came round the last bend, was a single lit window in a great deal of nothing.

Albert unlocked, and put the lights on, and the kettle. John sat at their table by the window and looked out at the stars, which looked exactly as they always had and now said a great deal more.

“So we know what they’re made of,” he said. “Hydrogen and helium, mostly. And the light tells us how hot they are, and how they’re moving, and how far back we’re seeing.” He turned his cup round. “Which brings us back to where we started. Where did it all come from?”

Albert set the teapot on the counter: the old one, from the back of the cupboard behind the fryer, that came out only for visitors.

“An old friend of mine is coming for tea tomorrow,” he said. “You can ask him.”

“Does he know?”

“He will tell you that it did not come from anywhere.” Albert began looking, without much hope, for the tea. “He does not believe the universe had a beginning at all. He thinks it has always been here, more or less as it is now, and always will be.”

“Is he right?” said Cal.

“He is a very great astronomer,” said Albert, “and he likes his tea strong. You can decide for yourselves.”

Out in the car park, The Van’s screen glowed faintly through its windscreen. It had been asked nothing, but it had evidently been listening.
