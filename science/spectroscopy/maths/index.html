Why a line’s strength depends on the temperature

A dark line needs atoms in the right state to make it: in the right stage of ionisation (holding the right number of electrons), and with an electron on the right rung of the ladder, the level the line climbs from. Two rules of heat, Boltzmann’s and Saha’s, say how many atoms are in each state.

Boltzmann’s rule. In a gas at temperature TTT, the numbers of atoms with an electron on two levels aaa and bbb, of energies Ea<EbE_a < E_bEa​<Eb​, are in the ratio

NbNa=gbga e−(Eb−Ea)/kT,\frac{N_b}{N_a} = \frac{g_b}{g_a}\, e^{-(E_b - E_a)/kT},Na​Nb​​=ga​gb​​e−(Eb​−Ea​)/kT,

where ggg counts the ways each level can be filled (its statistical weight) and k=8.617×10−5k = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}k=8.617×10−5 electronvolts a kelvin. For hydrogen, level nnn has gn=2n2g_n = 2n^2gn​=2n2 and energy −13.6 eV/n2-13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2−13.6 eV/n2, so the second rung, from which all four of its visible lines climb, is 10.210.210.2 eV above the bottom, and

N2N1=4 e−10.2 eV/kT.\frac{N_2}{N_1} = 4\, e^{-10.2\ \text{eV}/kT}.N1​N2​​=4e−10.2 eV/kT.

In the Sun’s outer layers, T=5,772T = 5{,}772T=5,772 K, kT=0.497kT = 0.497kT=0.497 eV, and N2/N1=4 e−20.5=5.0×10−9N_2/N_1 = 4\,e^{-20.5} = 5.0 \times 10^{-9}N2​/N1​=4e−20.5=5.0×10−9: about one hydrogen atom in 200 million.

Calcium. Calcium’s two violet lines (Fraunhofer’s H and K) climb from the bottom rung of calcium that has lost one electron, Ca II. Calcium loses its first electron for 6.116.116.11 eV and its second for 11.8711.8711.87 eV (with U=1,2,1U = 1, 2, 1U=1,2,1 for Ca I, II and III). At 5,772 K, Saha’s rule puts 98.7 per cent of the Sun’s calcium in Ca II, almost all of it on the bottom rung. The Sun has 2.2×10−62.2 \times 10^{-6}2.2×10−6 calcium atoms for each hydrogen atom (Asplund et al., 2009), so for every hydrogen atom there are 2.2×10−62.2 \times 10^{-6}2.2×10−6 calcium atoms ready to make H and K, against 5.0×10−95.0 \times 10^{-9}5.0×10−9 hydrogen atoms ready to make hydrogen’s lines: about 430 times as many. (The lines are about equally strong for each atom ready: their oscillator strengths, 0.68 for K and 0.64 for hydrogen’s red, are much the same.) At Vega’s 9,602 K, 90 per cent of the calcium has lost two electrons, and hydrogen’s ready atoms outnumber calcium’s 38 to one.

Payne’s method. Measure how strong each element’s lines are, work out from Saha’s and Boltzmann’s rules what share of the element’s atoms is ready to make them at the star’s temperature, and divide: what’s left is how much of the element there is. Done star by star, the hot ones and the cool ones come out with nearly the same mixture, and it’s mostly hydrogen and helium. (A real analysis follows the light out through the star’s layers, each at its own temperature and pressure, and allows for the background’s own opacity; the rules above are its heart.)

A hot body’s glow at each wavelength λ\lambdaλ follows Planck’s law, the quantum series’ second journey’s,

A star’s peak is often outside the visible band, so in practice astronomers compare its brightness through two filters, a blue one and a green-yellow one, and read the temperature from the ratio. Planck’s law gives the ratio of the glow at 440 nm to that at 550 nm as 0.98 at 5,772 K and 1.49 at 9,602 K: Vega is half as bright again in the blue, for the same green. Stars aren’t perfect glows, since their own lines and layers bend the curve, so the temperature quoted for a star is the “effective temperature”: that of a perfect glow of the star’s size giving out the same light in all.

A line’s Doppler shift

A source moving away from you at speed vvv, much less than light’s speed ccc, has its waves stretched by a share v/cv/cv/c:

Δλλ=vc,\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{v}{c},λΔλ​=cv​,

and one coming closer has them squeezed by the same share. (The exact rule, relativity’s, is λseen/λsent=(1+β)/(1−β)\lambda_\text{seen}/\lambda_\text{sent} = \sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}λseen​/λsent​=(1+β)/(1−β)​ with β=v/c\beta = v/cβ=v/c, positive going away; the two agree to better than a part in ten thousand at a star’s speeds.)

Vega comes towards us at 13.9 kilometres a second, β=−4.64×10−5\beta = -4.64 \times 10^{-5}β=−4.64×10−5. Hydrogen’s red line, 656.28 nm in the laboratory, arrives at

656.28 nm×(1−4.64×10−5)=656.25 nm,656.28\ \text{nm} \times \left(1 - 4.64 \times 10^{-5}\right) = 656.25\ \text{nm},656.28 nm×(1−4.64×10−5)=656.25 nm,

shifted by 0.030 nm towards the blue; its blue-green line, 486.13 nm, by 0.023 nm. The figure draws the shift 200 times larger. A colour alone couldn’t show a shift so small, but a pattern of lines whose places are known from the laboratory can: every line moves by the same share. Measured against thousands of lines at once, a star’s speed towards or away from us can be found to within metres a second. (The Earth’s own motion round the Sun, about 30 kilometres a second, changes the shift through the year, and is taken out.)

Brightness, distance and power

A star giving out power LLL in every direction spreads it over a sphere, so at distance ddd each square metre receives

F=L4πd2:F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}:F=4πd2L​:

twice as far, a quarter as much. Turn it round, and a measured brightness and distance give the power, L=4πd2FL = 4\pi d^2 FL=4πd2F.

In visible light Vega’s is 0.03 and the Sun’s −26.74-26.74−26.74, so Vega is 100.4×26.77=5.1×101010^{0.4 \times 26.77} = 5.1 \times 10^{10}100.4×26.77=5.1×1010 times fainter to us. (Vega spins so fast that it bulges, and we see it almost from its pole, which is hotter and brighter than its equator, so this reckoning, which assumes a star shines equally in every direction, makes it a little brighter than it is. Careful models make its total output about forty times the Sun’s.)
