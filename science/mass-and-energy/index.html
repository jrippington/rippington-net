An invoice from the universe

Albert’s remark about the cost of their engine had stayed with Cal all night.

“What exactly powers The Van?” he asked, as soon as the coffee arrived.

John put down his cup. “The engine.”

“I’d noticed that. What powers the engine?”

“Something highly advanced.”

“Meaning?”

“Meaning I can’t find the manual.”

Through the café window they could see The Van’s dashboard. It was displaying, by turns, at a rate that suggested an argument between two departments of the same computer:

FUEL LOW

FUEL FULL

Albert brought over two lemon doughnuts, their sugar catching the morning sun. Cal accepted his politely and then, after a moment’s thought, put it behind a napkin.

“I have a simpler question,” said John. “What happens if we just keep accelerating?”

“We go faster,” said Cal.

“But never as fast as light. We know that much.” John counted it off on his fingers. “The headlights: however fast we went, our own light left us at the full speed of light, so we can never catch it up. The light clock: the faster The Van goes, the flatter the light’s zigzag, and at the speed of light it would lie down flat. The light would never reach the other mirror, and our clock would stop. And the Lorentz factor, which says how slowly our clocks run and how short the way to Andromeda gets, grows without limit as we get closer.” He spread his hands. “So the speed of light is a limit, and I believe it. What I don’t understand is what does the stopping.”

“Must something?” said Albert.

John had been hoping someone would ask. He turned over a paper napkin and took out a pen.

“Suppose The Van pushes us along at one g,” he said. “That’s how quickly a doughnut speeds up when it falls off this table, and it’s not too much to ask of the engine. Every second, the engine adds 9.8 metres a second to our speed. That’s nearly 850 kilometres a second every day. Light travels at about 300,000 kilometres a second. So,” he finished, underlining something twice, “by this, after 354 days we’re going at the speed of light. Less than a year. Except that we can’t be. So something has to give before then. What?”

“The engine gives up?” said Cal. “Or the fuel runs out?”

Albert smiled, in the way he did when someone had asked exactly the right question.

“Your speedometer might support that reasoning,” he said. “Nature does not. But the engine doesn’t give up, and the fuel doesn’t run out.”

“Then what stops us?”

“Nothing stops you,” said Albert. “That is what makes it so interesting.” He wrapped a doughnut in a napkin and put it into John’s hand. “For the journey. And do keep an eye on the bill.”

One g, for as long as it takes

The Van left the café car park with a sound like a filing cabinet being lowered, slowly, down a staircase. It climbed away from Tenerife, then from the Earth, and eventually from anything it might bump into.

They had taken three instruments with them, and for once all three were working.

The first was the battered probe, which had once watched them throw a doughnut across the cabin. Cal fed it out through the airlock and left it hanging in the dark beside them. Since The Van and the probe weren’t moving relative to each other before the burn, the probe made a perfect marker: whatever speed The Van had relative to it afterwards would be the speed the engine had given it.

The second was the accelerometer from the cupboard, the little weight between two springs whose needle moves only when The Van speeds up, slows down or turns. John fixed it to the dashboard where they could both see it.

The third was a dial that neither of them could remember noticing before, labelled ENERGY. Cal tapped it cautiously, and it didn’t fall off. It turned out to show the energy of The Van’s motion: how much energy the engine had put into making The Van move, relative to the probe. To work that out it needed The Van’s mass. The handbook was no help, so they told the dial two and a half tonnes, which seemed about right.

John pointed The Van away from the probe and set the engine to one g.

They were pressed back into their seats. The accelerometer’s needle swung over and settled at 1.00 g. It felt, Cal thought, exactly like sitting at the café table, which struck him as odd. He made a note to think about it later.

For ten minutes everything went precisely to plan. The probe shrank to a blinking point, and then to nothing. The Van’s speed relative to it climbed steadily: 2.9 kilometres a second after five minutes, 5.9 after ten. The energy dial climbed too, and faster. When the speed had doubled, the energy had gone up four times.

“Right on schedule,” said John. “Only 353 days and twenty-three hours until the napkin says we reach the speed of light. I’d like to see what stops us.”

“We are not,” said Cal, “spending a year in here with the kettle.”

So John switched off the engine, and asked the simulator to work out the rest. It agreed, after a long pause, to imagine The Van carrying on at one g for as long as they liked, and to show them what the probe, the accelerometer and the energy dial would say.

Day 354 arrived on the screen, and The Van was travelling at about three quarters of the speed of light: 0.76c, the simulator said, using the physicist’s shorthand of c for the speed of light.

“It’s broken,” said John. “We should be nearly there by now.”

“It’s the simulator,” said Cal. “Not the speedometer.”

They let it run on. After a year on board, The Van was doing 0.77c. After two years, 0.968c. After three, 0.996c. The accelerometer read 1.00 g the whole way, so the engine’s push had never weakened. Yet the speed crept closer and closer to c without ever arriving, and the simulator had to write it with more and more nines. The energy dial, meanwhile, had stopped creeping and started to run. After three years on board it showed more energy than the whole of humanity uses in three and a half years.

John stared at it. Then, because he was John, he asked the simulator the question he had really come to ask.

“Can it do the galaxy?”

It could. The Milky Way is about 100,000 light-years across. If The Van pushed at one g for the first half of the way, then turned round and braked at one g for the second half, the simulator said, the crossing would take about 22 years by The Van’s own clocks. On the galaxy’s clocks a little over 100,000 years would go by, for the reasons they had found on their third and fifth journeys, but John chose not to dwell on that.

“Twenty-two years,” he said. “Within a lifetime.”

“And how fast are we going at the halfway point?” said Cal.

The simulator showed a speed with nine nines after the decimal point, and then some more. The Van would be travelling slower than light by less than six centimetres a second.

“Very nearly the speed of light,” said John happily.

“And the energy?”

The simulator itemised the bill. The Van’s energy of motion at the halfway point came to about as much as the whole world uses in nearly 19,000 years. Underneath, in a separate line all of its own, it had listed the cargo: one lemon doughnut, 60 g (estimated). The doughnut’s share alone was more than the world uses in five months.

Cal read the line twice. Then he looked at the napkin in John’s hand.

“That,” he said, “is the most expensive doughnut in the universe.”

John considered this, and then ate it. Cal pointed out that this made no difference to the bill whatsoever, since the doughnut was still on board, only in a different place.

They turned The Van round, and spent a good deal longer finding the probe than they had spent leaving it.

Why the speed never reaches c

John’s sum on the napkin is exactly what Isaac Newton’s laws of motion predict, and those laws had just worked perfectly for ten minutes. Where they go wrong, and why the simulator’s speeds kept growing nines instead, comes down to energy.

What an engine gives a spaceship

An engine doesn’t produce speed directly. It burns fuel (or, in The Van’s case, does whatever The Van’s engine does), and turns the fuel’s energy into motion. The word energy is so familiar that it’s worth pinning down.

Energy

The capacity to make things happen: to move something, lift it, heat it or light it up. It comes in many forms, among them the energy of motion, heat, light, the chemical energy stored in food and fuel, and electrical energy, and it can change from one form into another. In all those changes the total amount stays exactly the same: energy is never created or destroyed, only passed on or converted. It is measured in joules. Lifting an apple one metre takes about one joule.

Because energy is conserved, we can keep accounts. Whatever energy The Van’s motion has, the engine must have supplied it, and the fuel must have paid for it. The energy dial was keeping that account.

The energy of motion

You already know, from experience, how the energy of motion behaves at everyday speeds, even if you have never written it down. The UK’s Highway Code says that a car travelling at 20 miles an hour needs about 6 metres to brake to a stop, and one travelling at 40 needs about 24. Double the speed, and the braking distance goes up four times. The brakes take the car’s energy of motion away at a roughly steady rate per metre, so four times the distance means four times the energy.

Kinetic energy

The energy an object has because it is moving. At everyday speeds it is half the mass times the speed squared. Double the speed and the kinetic energy goes up four times; double the mass and it doubles. Like speed itself, it depends on the frame of reference: relative to the probe, The Van had a great deal of kinetic energy after its burn, while relative to itself it had none.

That is what the energy dial showed during the real ten-minute burn. Each second the engine added the same 9.8 metres a second to The Van’s speed; when the speed had doubled, from 2.9 kilometres a second to 5.9, the kinetic energy had gone up four times.

Newton’s bill

If Newton’s rule, half the mass times the speed squared, held at every speed, John’s napkin would be right, and nothing would stop The Van. Speed would rise steadily, by 9.8 metres a second every second, and pass the speed of light at day 354. The bill at that moment would be half The Van’s mass times the speed of light squared, which is an enormous number for a camper van, but a perfectly finite one. Keep paying, and The Van would go faster still. Nothing in Newton’s laws objects. The simulator, though, was using Einstein’s.

The real bill

In the third journey, John and Cal met the Lorentz factor, the factor by which a moving clock runs slow. It grows without limit as the speed approaches c: it is 1.67 at 80 per cent of the speed of light, about 7.09 at 99 per cent, and about 70.7 at 99.99 per cent.

The same factor sets the energy bill. Einstein’s rule for kinetic energy, which is exact at every speed, goes like this: take the Lorentz factor, subtract one, and multiply what’s left by the object’s mass and by the speed of light squared. Notice that, at any given speed, the bill is in proportion to the mass, just as Newton’s is: twice the mass, twice the bill.

At everyday speeds Einstein’s rule comes out the same as Newton’s, to a quite absurd precision. For the ten-minute burn, at 5.9 kilometres a second, the two differ by about three parts in ten billion, which is why the energy dial agreed with Newton. But the two rules part company as the speed rises. At 80 per cent of the speed of light, Einstein’s bill is about twice Newton’s. At 99 per cent it is more than twelve times as large. And at the speed of light itself, where Newton’s bill stops at half the mass times the speed of light squared, Einstein’s has no end, because the Lorentz factor has none.

So John’s plan fails for a reason no engineer can fix: no finite amount of energy brings anything with mass up to the speed of light. Each step closer costs more than the last. Going from 0.9c to 0.99c, the step the simulator took in a little over a year, costs nearly four times as much as everything spent getting to 0.9c. Every further nine after that, from 0.99c to 0.999c, then to 0.9999c, costs about three times as much as the nine before it (closer and closer to the square root of ten, about 3.16, as the nines go on).

The same push, less and less speed

That explains the bill. But it leaves something odd. The accelerometer read 1.00 g the whole way: John and Cal felt the same steady push on their backs throughout. How can the same push give less and less speed?

Think about the burn from The Van’s own point of view. At any instant there is an inertial reference frame moving along with The Van at its speed at that moment, and by the principle of relativity that frame is as good as any other: nothing about it marks it out as fast. Measured in that frame, The Van starts each second at rest, and the engine adds 9.8 metres a second to its speed, just as it did in the very first second. From the inside, the burn never changes.

The catch is the one John and Cal found when they chased their own headlights: speeds don’t simply add. Add 9.8 metres a second to a speed far below light’s, and the result, measured from the probe, is 9.8 metres a second faster, near enough. But add the same 9.8 metres a second to 0.99c, by Einstein’s rule for combining speeds, and the probe sees The Van go only about 0.2 metres a second faster. At 0.9999c the gain is about 2 millimetres a second. The push is always the same, and each second’s 9.8 metres a second is real, but measured from the probe each second’s gain is smaller than the last, because it is being added to a speed already close to c. However many of them you add, they never sum to c.

Where the energy goes

If the engine keeps pushing and The Van hardly speeds up, where does the engine’s effort go? Into kinetic energy, as the dial showed, and into momentum, a quantity as basic to physics as energy itself.

Momentum

The “quantity of motion” an object carries in a particular direction: at everyday speeds, its mass times its velocity. A loaded lorry at 50 kilometres an hour has far more momentum than a bicycle at the same speed, which is why it is so much harder to stop. A push changes an object’s momentum, and in any collision or explosion the total momentum of everything involved stays the same. At high speeds the mass times the velocity has to be multiplied by the Lorentz factor, so momentum, like kinetic energy, grows without limit as the speed approaches c. Light carries momentum too, even though it has no mass.

A steady push adds momentum at a steady rate, for as long as it lasts, with no limit. What does level off is the speed. As The Van’s speed approaches c, its momentum and kinetic energy keep climbing, but the speed they correspond to hardly changes.

Doesn’t the mass go up?

You may have read that an object’s mass increases as it speeds up, so that it gets harder and harder to push. Older textbooks often said so, and called the result “relativistic mass”. It is an understandable way of describing the bill, and the arithmetic can be made to work. But most physicists now avoid it, because it mixes up mass with energy.

Think back to the principle of relativity. Inside The Van, coasting at 0.99c relative to the probe, nothing is different from before the burn: John feels no heavier, and no experiment in the cabin can even tell that The Van is moving. So in its own frame, The Van’s mass is what it always was.

Rest mass

The mass of an object measured in a frame in which it is at rest: what you would find by weighing it, or by pushing it and seeing how readily it accelerates, if you travelled along with it. Every observer, however they are moving, agrees about it, which is why physicists today mean rest mass when they say “mass”. It doesn’t grow with speed. What grows, as the speed approaches c, is the object’s energy and momentum.

During the burn, then, The Van’s mass stayed the same, while its kinetic energy and its momentum grew without limit and its speed crept towards c.

Has anyone actually paid it?

Particle accelerators pay it every day. In 1964, William Bertozzi, at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, used a linear accelerator to give electrons kinetic energies from half a million to fifteen million electronvolts (an electronvolt is a tiny unit of energy, convenient for particles). He timed the electrons over a measured 8.4 metres to find their speeds, and checked their energies independently by letting them stop in an aluminium disc and measuring how much it warmed up. By Newton’s rule, an electron with fifteen million electronvolts would have been travelling at more than seven times the speed of light. Bertozzi’s electrons, at every energy, came in just below c, as Einstein’s rule says they should.

In its third run, from 2022, the Large Hadron Collider at CERN, near Geneva, accelerated protons to 6.8 trillion electronvolts each. That gave them a Lorentz factor of about 7,250, and a speed about ten kilometres an hour short of light’s: the pace of a gentle jog, against light’s 1.08 billion kilometres an hour. Pushing them any closer would take ever more energy for ever less speed, as the simulator showed.

A box with a lamp at each end

They were back at the café by the middle of the afternoon. Albert brought coffee and, inevitably, two more lemon doughnuts, and listened to the account of the burn, the simulator and the bill. He asked to see the napkin with the sum on it, and studied it for some time.

“Well,” he said at last, “you have discovered that energy has a price, and that the price goes up near the speed of light. Now I should like to show you that the price was there all along, even when nothing was moving at all.”

He went into the back of the café, and came out with a small white box of the sort he used for doughnuts to take away. It had a little lamp fixed to each end.

“Suppose this box is floating in empty space,” he said. “It flashes both lamps at once: two identical pulses of light, one out of each end.”

“Two lamps,” said Cal. “One front, one back.”

“Precisely. Now, light carries momentum. When a lamp sends out a pulse, the pulse pushes back on the lamp, a very little, like the kick of a gun. But the two pulses are the same, and they go in opposite directions. The two kicks cancel.”

“So the box stays put,” said John.

“It stays put. Afterwards it has less energy inside it, by exactly the energy of the two pulses, and it is just where it was.” Albert put the box down between their cups. “Now watch the same flash from your probe, with the box drifting past it.”

Light, in packets

Light behaves as a wave, as John and Cal found when they chased a beam of it, but it is also delivered in small, separate amounts. A beam that seems smooth and continuous to us is, looked at closely enough, a stream of these packets.

Photon

The smallest amount of light (or of any electromagnetic radiation, such as radio waves or X-rays) that can be emitted or absorbed. Each photon carries a definite energy, more for bluer light and less for redder, and a definite momentum, in the direction it’s travelling. It always travels at c. A photon has no rest mass at all: there is no frame of reference in which it is at rest, and its energy is entirely the energy of its motion.

Light’s momentum is very small, which is why you have never been knocked over by a torch. But it is real: in 2010, on its way to Venus, the Japanese spacecraft IKAROS was pushed along by sunlight bouncing off a sail about fourteen metres square, with a force about equal to the weight of a tenth of a gram.

The same flash, seen twice

In the box’s own frame, the story is just as Albert told it. The two pulses are identical: the same colour, the same energy, each carrying half the energy the box gives out. Call that total, measured in the box’s own frame, the flash’s energy: each pulse carries half of it. Their momenta are equal and opposite, and cancel. The box doesn’t move before the flash, or after it.

Now look from the probe, with the box drifting past it at, say, six tenths of the speed of light. Nothing about the flash itself has changed: it’s the same two pulses. But the probe is moving relative to the box, and that changes what it measures.

This is the Doppler effect again, the effect that makes an ambulance’s siren higher as it comes towards you and lower as it goes away. Think of it this way. Throw a ball forwards from a moving van, and it goes faster than if you’d thrown it standing still. Shine a light forwards from a moving van, and it can’t go any faster: the second journey settled that. So the extra shows up in the only place it can, in the light’s colour. Seen from the probe, the light sent forwards is squeezed, its waves packed closer together, and it’s bluer. The light sent backwards is stretched, and redder.

And bluer light carries more energy. You already know this, in your skin: ultraviolet light, bluer than blue, gives you sunburn, and red light never does. Light comes in tiny packets, called photons, and the box sends out the same number of them whoever is watching; you can count them. What changes, from one point of view to another, is how much energy each one carries, and a squeezed, bluer one carries more.

Now the surprise. At six tenths of the speed of light, the probe measures the forward light with twice the energy it had in the box’s frame, and the backward light with half. Doubling gains more than halving loses, as anyone with a pound to double or halve knows: double it and you’re a pound up, halve it and you’re only fifty pence down. So the light, seen from the probe, carries more energy in all than it did in the box’s own frame: a quarter more, 1.25 times the flash’s energy. And 1.25 is exactly the Lorentz factor at six tenths of the speed of light. Whatever the speed, the gain always beats the loss, and the light always carries exactly the Lorentz factor times the flash’s energy.

Who pays for the extra?

Now do the accounts, in both frames, as an accountant would, keeping the box at six tenths of the speed of light.

In the box’s frame, the box gave out light carrying the flash’s energy. Energy is conserved, so the box itself has exactly that much less energy than before. That’s no surprise: the lamps used something up.

In the probe’s frame, the box gave out light carrying one and a quarter times the flash’s energy, which is more. So in this frame the box must have lost one and a quarter times the flash’s energy: the flash’s energy itself, plus an extra quarter of it. Where did the extra come from?

The box’s energy, as the probe measures it, is made of two parts: whatever energy it has inside it, the part it would have even if it were standing still, and its kinetic energy, the energy of its motion past the probe. The energy inside it went down by the flash’s energy, the same as in the box’s own frame. So the extra quarter has to have come out of its kinetic energy.

But the box’s speed didn’t change. The kicks cancelled in the box’s frame, so it stayed still there, and so it carries on drifting past the probe at the same speed as before. Its kinetic energy fell, and its speed didn’t.

Stop there, because this is the whole of it. How much energy of motion a thing has depends on two things, and only two: how fast it’s going, and how much mass it has. A lorry and a bicycle going at the same speed carry very different energies of motion, and the only difference between them is their mass. The box’s energy of motion went down. Its speed didn’t change. So the only thing that can have changed is its mass. The box gave out energy, and it got lighter.

How much lighter? Look again at Einstein’s rule for kinetic energy: the Lorentz factor less one, times the mass, times the speed of light squared. At six tenths of the speed of light the Lorentz factor is 1.25, so the Lorentz factor less one is a quarter, and the box’s kinetic energy, relative to the probe, is a quarter of its mass times the speed of light squared. The speed stayed the same, so that quarter stayed the same too. A quarter of the mass times the speed of light squared went down by a quarter of the flash’s energy; so the mass times the speed of light squared went down by the whole of the flash’s energy, and the mass itself went down by the flash’s energy divided by the speed of light squared.

At any other speed the fraction is different, but it is the same fraction on both sides of the accounts. The extra the light carries, seen from the probe, is the Lorentz factor less one, times the flash’s energy; the box’s kinetic energy is the Lorentz factor less one, times its mass times the speed of light squared. The fraction cancels, and the answer never changes: the box’s mass went down by the flash’s energy divided by the speed of light squared.

“Even though it didn’t move?” John asked.

“Even though it didn’t recoil in its own frame,” said Albert. “It sent out light with some energy, and it is lighter by that energy divided by the speed of light squared. Every way you look at it.”

“And the light has energy and momentum?”

“Yes.”

“Does it have mass?”

“Each photon has no rest mass at all,” said Albert. “One must be careful with words.”

He was right to be careful. The mass the box lost didn’t vanish: it went with the light. In the box’s frame, the two pulses together carry the flash’s energy and, because their momenta cancel, no momentum at all. That is precisely what the mass the box lost would have if it were simply standing still: the same energy, and no momentum. So the pair of pulses, taken together, has the mass the box lost, while neither pulse on its own has any. Mass, it turns out, belongs to a whole system, and a system can have more mass than its parts added together.

Run the flash

Albert’s box is drawn below as an engineering drawing, set up at six tenths of the speed of light. At the bottom, the box’s energy is drawn as a block. It is c deep and its mass wide. The lower layer, c high, is its energy at rest: its mass times c times c. On top sits its energy of motion, a layer whose height is set by its speed: a quarter as tall as the one below at six tenths of the speed of light, and taller and taller, without limit, as the speed nears c, which is the energy bill from earlier in this journey. (The c’s aren’t lengths, of course: the block is a picture of multiplying, like the grids you may have used for long multiplication.)

Press Fire, and watch it all happen in order. The box fires both lamps: seen from the box, two equal flashes; seen from the probe, squeezed ahead and stretched behind. The light’s energy is added up. Then the light’s energy comes off the block. Its depth is c, its layers’ heights are c and what its speed makes them, and the speed doesn’t change: so the only way to take energy off is a slice cut through both layers, off the width, the mass. The slice through the lower layer is the energy from inside the box; the slice through the upper layer is the energy from its motion. The sum underneath shows how wide the slice is. Then set a different speed and fire again: the numbers change, and the answer doesn’t.

The probe’s picture also shows the box a little shorter: length contraction, which doesn’t change the accounts.

At everyday speeds, the number that cancels is the half in Newton’s ½mv², the energy of motion you may remember from school, and it cancels just the same.

Where E = mc² comes from

When a body gives out energy, in light or in any other form, its mass goes down by that energy divided by the speed of light squared. Turn that round, and it says that every mass carries energy, in proportion to it, simply by existing: its mass times the speed of light squared.

This is E = mc², the most famous equation in physics (E stands for the energy, m for the mass and c, as always, for the speed of light), and Albert’s box is very nearly how it was found. In September 1905, a few months after his first paper on relativity, Einstein sent the Annalen der Physik a three-page paper with a question for a title: does the inertia of a body depend upon its energy content? His argument was the one above, with a body sending out two equal pulses of light in opposite directions, watched from two frames. His answer was yes. If a body gives off energy in the form of radiation, he wrote, “its mass diminishes”, by that energy divided by the square of the speed of light. And he suggested a test: radium, which gives off energy steadily, ought to be losing mass as it does.

The way the argument is pictured in this article was inspired by Mahesh Shenoy’s explanations of it on his channel, Float Head Physics.

Rest energy (E = mc²)

The energy an object has simply because it has mass, measured in a frame in which it is at rest: its mass times the speed of light squared. Because the speed of light squared is so large (about 90,000 trillion, in metres and seconds), a little mass is a great deal of energy: one gram is about 90 trillion joules. It isn’t energy waiting to be released at will. Most of it is locked in, and only special processes can set much of it free. But every change in an object’s energy, whether it warms up, cools down or gives out light, changes its mass by the energy divided by the speed of light squared.

Does a hot kettle weigh more?

The Van’s kettle, boiling a litre of water up from 20 °C, puts about 335,000 joules of heat into it. Divide by the speed of light squared, and the hot water is heavier than the cold by about four billionths of a gram. A wound-up clock spring is heavier than an unwound one; a charged battery is heavier than a flat one. None of these differences could ever be weighed on a kitchen scale. But they are real, and where much larger energies are involved, they have been measured.

In 2005, physicists at MIT and at the US National Institute of Standards and Technology tested the equation more precisely than ever. At MIT they weighed atoms of silicon and sulphur with and without one extra neutron, by watching single charged atoms circle in a magnetic trap. At NIST they measured the energy of the gamma rays such an atom gives out when it captures a neutron. The mass lost and the energy given out agreed, through E = mc², to within about four parts in ten million.

The Sun shines by the same bookkeeping. Every second it sends out about 380 trillion trillion joules of light, and so every second it becomes about four million tonnes lighter.

The cost of a single pastry

John picked up his doughnut and held it up to the light.

“So this,” he said, “has rest energy. E = mc².”

“Indeed,” said Albert.

“Sixty grams of it,” said Cal, “according to the simulator.”

John did the sum on the back of the napkin. At sixty grams, the doughnut’s rest energy came to about 5,000 trillion joules. A large power station, producing a billion joules every second, would take about two months to make as much.

“Could we use it to power The Van?”

“That depends how you propose to get the energy out,” said Albert. “Eating it, for example, releases a few hundred kilocalories.”

Cal looked it up on his phone. A sugared doughnut gives about 400 kilocalories for every hundred grams, so about a million joules for this one. Set against its rest energy, that was about two parts in ten billion.

“So eating it uses almost none of it.”

“Almost none,” said Albert. “Chemistry, whether it’s digesting a doughnut or burning petrol, only rearranges the outer parts of atoms, and releases a minute fraction of the rest energy. Splitting heavy atoms, in a nuclear power station, releases about a tenth of one per cent. Fusing hydrogen into helium, as the Sun does, releases about seven tenths of one per cent. Only when matter meets antimatter does the whole of the rest energy come out.”

John looked thoughtfully at the doughnut, and then at The Van.

“I was about to suggest a new engine.”

“You are not,” said Cal firmly, “installing that thing in the reactor.”

The joke points at a real difficulty. Even with an engine that turned rest energy entirely into motion, the simulator’s figure, the kinetic energy alone, would be only the floor of the bill. A real starship has to carry its fuel, and push the fuel it hasn’t burned yet along with everything else; it has to stop at the other end, which costs as much again; and it has to get rid of waste heat, and shield its crew. The real bill would be far larger.

How can light travel at c?

Light travels at c without any bill at all, because a photon has no rest mass and the rules for things with mass don’t apply to it: it is emitted travelling at c, and in a vacuum it never travels at any other speed. Things with mass each have a frame in which they are at rest, and from there, however much energy is spent, their speed can only creep towards c. Both follow from the same rules of space, time, energy and momentum, which these seven journeys have been uncovering.

The Van’s most extravagant journeys, then, are fantastical engineering. The paths it might take are allowed by Einstein’s equations. The bills are not ones anyone could pay.

And there was another small problem.

“We keep talking about moving steadily,” said Cal, “relative to probes, and stations, and beacons, all floating about in empty space. But space isn’t empty. There’s gravity everywhere.”

John pointed at the volcanic rock under their feet. “Good point. But gravity’s simple enough. It’s a force that pulls us down.”

Albert looked up so sharply that his hair, for a moment, seemed to stop moving.

Back to Teide

The afternoon light was turning gold. John set his doughnut down, untouched for once, and looked out at the mountain’s long shadow stretching across the plateau.

“So,” he said. “There’s no absolute rest. Light has one speed, for everyone. Clocks and distances depend on how you’re moving. A round trip can change how much you age. And you can’t reach the speed of light however much energy you spend.”

“And energy has mass,” said Cal. “Even in a doughnut box.”

Albert nodded. “A good beginning.”

“Beginning?” said John.

“Of course. So far you have described the world as though gravity were not there.”

He pushed back his chair.

“Tell me,” he said. “What do you actually feel when you stand on the ground?”
