Two perfectly accurate clocks disagree about whether two flashes happened together. John blames the wiring. Cal blames John. Albert blames the word “now”.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 4 of 14

The same moment

Albert’s “They don’t always” had bothered John all night, and by lunchtime he thought he had found a way round it.

“I’ve discovered a comforting thought,” he announced.

Cal glanced at him over the café table. “That has never ended well.”

It was mid-afternoon on the plateau. The coffee was hot, the lemon doughnuts were on their way, and the mountain was doing what it always did, which was to look as though it had been there first and intended to be there last.

“Yesterday,” said John, “we learned that a clock moving past us runs slow. Fine. I accept it. Different clocks, different seconds. But surely there’s still one now. One moment, shared by the whole universe, that everybody is in at the same time.”

“Why?”

“Because something must be happening in Andromeda at this very instant.” John pointed vaguely upwards, in roughly the right direction for the afternoon. “A star exploding. A comet going round something. Whatever it is, it’s happening now, while I’m sitting here.”

“Yes,” said Cal, “but what do you mean by ‘this very instant’, when Andromeda is two and a half million light-years away?”

John held up his wrist. “This.”

“Your watch is in Tenerife.”

“It tells the time everywhere.”

“It tells the time here.”

Albert placed two lemon doughnuts on the table. Neither had been arranged with any regard for appearances. Beside them he set down a shoebox, and the lid had a label on it in his tidy capitals:

SET BEFORE USE

“Gentlemen,” he said, “may I introduce you to a problem with synchronising distant clocks?”

John narrowed his eyes. “Why do you always have a relevant prop?”

“Good catering,” said Albert, “requires preparation.”

Cal opened the box. It was full of small clocks, each about the size of a matchbox, each with a little lamp, a little light sensor and a little thruster, and there seemed to be rather more of them than a shoebox ought to hold. Every clock showed a different time.

Albert took two out. He handed one to John and carried the other to the far end of the terrace, where he set it down on the wall, perhaps twenty metres away, and came back.

“Make that one,” he said, pointing at the wall, “agree with yours. Without walking over there.”

John looked at the distant clock, then at the one in his hand. “I could phone someone.”

“There is nobody over there.”

“I could shout.”

“Sound takes time to arrive. So does a phone call. So does everything.” Albert smiled. “How long, exactly? And how would you know?”

John opened his mouth, and found that the answer had more steps in it than he’d expected. Albert went back inside to fetch the milk. Behind him, the clock on the wall carried on telling its own time, and nobody on the terrace could quite say what it ought to read.

The two flashes

The Van took them back to The Station.

It was where they had left it at the end of the third journey, a little beyond the Moon: the girder, just under twelve kilometres long, with a clock every hundred metres and a gate at each end. Light takes 20 microseconds, 20 millionths of a second, to go from the middle of the girder to either end.

“Welcome back,” said The Station’s computer, in its calm voice. “Please don’t touch the clocks.”

The plan was simple. John had written it on the back of a napkin, which was becoming a tradition. The Station’s computer fitted a lamp, a beacon, to each of the two gates, which John labelled A at the back and B at the front. Both beacons would flash at noon, as read by the clocks on their own gates. Halfway along the girder, beside the middle clock, The Station had a light detector, which would record the moment each flash arrived.

The Station’s clocks had been set going together by a single flash from the middle of the girder, so, as far as The Station was concerned, they were all in step, and the two beacons would flash together.

Then there was The Van’s part. The Van would come racing along the girder at six-tenths of the speed of light, engines off, coasting, and pass the middle at noon. John would sit in the middle of The Van, beside a window, with a light detector of his own, and a clock.

And there was Albert’s box.

Cal fed the little clocks out of the airlock one at a time, while The Van was still a long way off and coasting towards The Station. Each clock fired its thruster just enough to take its place in a line, and then switched the thruster off and coasted alongside The Van, at The Van’s own speed. When Cal had finished there was a line of clocks one and a half kilometres apart, stretching twelve kilometres ahead of The Van and twelve behind, all moving together. Relative to The Van, they weren’t moving at all. It was a great deal of line for a shoebox.

“Now we set them going together,” said John, “exactly the way The Station set its clocks.”

First John set his own clock, the middle one, so that it would read noon as he passed the middle of the girder. Then he sent a single flash of light from his seat in the middle of The Van. Each of Albert’s clocks, when the flash reached it, set itself to the time on John’s clock when he sent it, plus the time light takes to travel to it from John’s seat: five microseconds for the clocks one and a half kilometres away, ten for those at three, and so on. It was The Station’s own method, done with the same care, from the middle of The Van instead of the middle of the girder. The Station’s computer, which had been watching with polite interest, agreed to note the readings on all its clocks.

That is a lot to keep in your head at once, so here is the whole arrangement at the moment that matters, noon by The Station’s clocks.

“Now we have two lines of clocks,” said Cal. “Theirs, and ours. Both set properly.”

“Both set properly,” said John.

The Van’s dashboard clock, which had never agreed with anything, showed a picture of a sandwich.

They went past at six-tenths of the speed of light. The whole girder took a little over sixty microseconds to go by, much less than a blink, and everything that mattered happened during that time, recorded by the instruments. As The Van shot past the middle of the girder, John’s clock and The Station’s middle clock were side by side for an instant, and both read noon.

At noon, by The Station’s clocks, the beacons on gates A and B flashed.

Twenty microseconds after noon, the detector in the middle of the girder saw both flashes arrive together, as it should. Light had travelled six kilometres from each gate, at the same speed, and arrived at the same moment. The Station’s computer concluded, reasonably enough, that the two flashes had happened at the same time.

“So they’re simultaneous,” said John.

“In The Station’s frame,” said Cal.

John’s detector, though, saw the flash from B, ahead, ten microseconds after noon by his clock. The flash from A, behind, didn’t reach him until forty microseconds after noon. By then The Van had gone right past gate B and out the far end of The Station, and A’s light had had to chase it.

John shrugged. “Of course,” he said. “We were rushing towards B’s light and running away from A’s. Anybody would see B first.”

“Quite right,” said Albert over the radio, as if he had been listening all along. “But seeing them at different times isn’t the interesting part. Look at my clocks.”

Each of Albert’s clocks had a light sensor, and each one had noted the reading on its own face at the moment a flash went off right beside it. One of them had been passing gate B at the instant B flashed. Another had been passing gate A at the instant A flashed. There was no light-travel delay to worry about: each clock was right there, at the flash, as close as the gate’s own clock was.

The clock beside gate B said B had flashed fifteen microseconds before noon.

The clock beside gate A said A had flashed fifteen microseconds after noon.

John checked the readout twice. The second time it was worse.

The dashboard flashed a new warning:

CLOCKS DISAGREE. PLEASE CHOOSE A FRAME.

“For once,” said Cal, “that’s quite good advice.”

When did the flashes happen?

John’s first explanation, that they were rushing towards one flash and away from the other, is perfectly sensible, but it only explains when he saw each flash. When each flash happened is a different question, and a harder one.

Thunder, and old starlight

You already know how to tell the two apart, from thunderstorms. You see the lightning, and some seconds later you hear the thunder, though both happened together, in the same flash: the thunder is simply slower getting to you. Sound travels at about 340 metres a second, so if you count the seconds between the flash and the thunder and divide by three, you have, roughly, the distance of the storm in kilometres. In doing that sum, you are correcting for the travel time, and working out when the thunder happened from when you heard it.

Light travels so fast, about 300,000 kilometres a second, that in everyday life we never need to make the same correction for it. We see things as they happen, near enough. But the further away something is, the more the difference matters. The light from the Andromeda Galaxy, which you can see from a dark site like the Teide plateau as a faint smudge on an autumn evening, set out about two and a half million years ago: Andromeda is two and a half million light-years away, a light-year being the distance light travels in a year (more on that in the next journey).

So John’s question at the café, “what’s happening in Andromeda now?”, asks about something nobody could see: when distant things happened. The only way to answer it is the way you date the thunder: take what arrives, and correct for how long it took to arrive.

The Station does its sums

Start with The Station’s account, which is simple. The detector in the middle of the girder saw both flashes arrive at twenty microseconds after noon. Each gate is six kilometres from the middle, and light covers six kilometres in twenty microseconds, so each flash happened twenty microseconds before it arrived: at noon. Both at noon. So the flashes were simultaneous.

It can explain John’s results too. In The Station’s frame, The Van was moving towards B, so The Van met B’s light partway along the girder, at twelve and a half microseconds after noon by The Station’s clocks; A’s light had to chase The Van, at a closing speed of only four-tenths of the speed of light, and caught it only at fifty microseconds after noon, by which time The Van was beyond the end of the girder. (John’s own clock read 10 and 40 microseconds at those two moments, rather than 12.5 and 50, because it was moving past The Station’s clocks and so, as the light clock showed, running slow by the Lorentz factor, which at six-tenths of the speed of light is 1.25.) Everything fits. The Station’s verdict: the flashes happened together, and John saw B’s first because he was moving towards it.

John does his

Now do the same sum from John’s point of view, and take the first journey seriously. The Van was coasting: an inertial frame, every bit as good as The Station’s. In The Van’s frame, John wasn’t moving at all. He was sitting still in the middle of his line of clocks. It was The Station, girder, gates and all, that was rushing past him, backwards, at six-tenths of the speed of light.

So “I was rushing towards B’s light” isn’t something John can say in his own frame. In his frame, the light from each flash came towards him at the speed of light, the same speed in every direction, whatever the gates were doing when they flashed. That is the constancy of the speed of light, from the second journey, and it is just as true in The Van’s frame as in The Station’s.

Where were the flashes, in The Van’s frame? Albert’s clocks know, because each flash happened right beside one of them, and they are at fixed, known places in The Van’s line: the flash from B happened beside the clock seven and a half kilometres ahead of John, and the flash from A beside the clock seven and a half kilometres behind. (Seven and a half, not six. In The Van’s frame the girder was moving, and the two flashes, as we’re about to find, happened at different times, so each happened wherever its gate had got to by then. There’s another surprise hiding in that sentence, about how long the girder is, and that is the next journey’s business. One surprise at a time.)

So, in his own frame, John sat exactly halfway between the two flashes. Light from each had seven and a half kilometres to travel, which takes it twenty-five microseconds, at the same speed from both sides. If the flashes had happened at the same time, their light would have reached him at the same time.

It didn’t. B’s light arrived at 10 microseconds after noon on his clock, so B must have flashed twenty-five microseconds earlier, at 15 microseconds before noon. A’s light arrived at 40 microseconds after noon, so A flashed at 15 microseconds after noon. Those are the very readings on Albert’s clocks beside the gates. John’s sums, with light’s travel time allowed for, and Albert’s clocks, which needed no allowing for, agree: in The Van’s frame, B flashed thirty microseconds before A.

The unequal arrival times are easy to mistake for the discovery, but in The Station’s frame John’s motion explains them, and the flashes were simultaneous. The discovery is what’s left after the travel time has been allowed for, in each frame, by that frame’s own measurements: in The Station’s frame, both flashes at noon; in The Van’s, thirty microseconds apart.

Who is right?

The obvious question is which of them is wrong. The obvious answer, that it must be the one who was “really” moving, is the one the first journey took away: there is no way to say which of two inertial frames is really moving. The Station and The Van each did the experiment carefully, with clocks set going together by the same method, and corrected for the light’s travel time properly. They disagree because they have different meanings for “at the same time”.

Simultaneity

Two events are simultaneous, in a given frame of reference, if that frame’s clocks, set going together and standing right beside each event, read the same time when it happens. Events that are simultaneous in one inertial frame are generally not simultaneous in another frame moving relative to it, if they happen in different places. This is called the relativity of simultaneity. Two events that happen at the same place and at the same moment, though, are simultaneous for everybody.

So a doughnut landing in a hand, and a clock beside it reading noon, happened together for every observer there is. Argument is only possible about events some distance apart, and the further apart they are, the bigger the disagreement can be. That is why nobody notices it on Earth: across a café terrace, even at the speed of a racing car, the disagreement is far smaller than a billionth of a second.

How do you set a clock far away?

Back in the café, Albert’s challenge was to make the clock on the wall agree with John’s, without walking over there. It is the same problem as The Station’s, because “simultaneous” is only as good as the clocks that decide it, and distant clocks have to be set somehow.

Carrying the clock

The obvious method is to set the two clocks side by side, where nobody can argue about whether they agree, and then carry one of them to the wall. For everyday purposes it works. But the third journey found that a moving clock runs slow, so a clock carried across the terrace falls behind while it’s being carried: by an absurdly small amount at walking pace, but by some amount. Carrying it more slowly makes the error smaller, never zero, and at The Station, with clocks twelve kilometres apart, it would take an awfully long time.

Signals, and Einstein’s rule

The other method is to send a signal: a phone call, a radio time signal, or, best of all, a flash of light, since light’s speed is the same for everybody. The trouble is that the signal takes time to arrive, and you need to know how long, to subtract it.

One way to find out needs only one clock. Send a flash from your clock to a mirror beside the distant clock, and time how long the round trip takes, out and back, on your own clock. Suppose it takes two microseconds. Then it’s natural to say that the light took one microsecond to get there and one to get back, so the moment the flash reached the mirror was exactly half-way between when it left and when it returned. Set the distant clock to read that half-way time at that moment, and the two clocks agree.

Notice the word “natural”. The rule assumes that light takes as long to get there as to get back: that its speed is the same in both directions. Checking that would mean timing the light’s one-way journey, and to time a one-way journey you need two clocks, one at each end, already set going together, which is what we were trying to do in the first place. The French mathematician Henri Poincaré saw in 1898 that the time of a distant event can only be settled by some such agreed rule, and in 1905 Einstein made this rule the starting point of relativity, in the first section of his paper “On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies”. In each inertial frame, light’s speed is the same in every direction (the second postulate, from the second journey), so in each inertial frame the half-way rule gives a consistent set of clocks: check any two of them against each other, by light, and they agree.

Synchronising clocks

Setting clocks in different places going together, so that they agree. In relativity it is done with light, by Einstein’s rule: send a flash from one clock to the other and back, and the moment it arrives at the far clock counts as half-way between the moments it left and returned. Equivalently, send a flash from a point exactly midway between two clocks: since light travels equally fast both ways, it reaches them at the same time. The Station set its clocks this way, and so did The Van. Clocks synchronised in one inertial frame are in step in that frame, but not in another moving relative to it.

Two sets of clocks, both set properly

So The Station set its clocks by a flash from the middle of the girder, and John set Albert’s by a flash from the middle of The Van. Both did it correctly. Now watch John’s synchronisation from The Station’s point of view.

In The Station’s frame, The Van and its line of clocks are moving forwards at six-tenths of the speed of light. John’s flash spreads out from his seat at the speed of light both ways. The clocks behind him are moving towards the spreading flash, so it reaches them sooner; the clocks ahead are moving away from it, so it reaches them later. Each clock, though, adds the same amount, its distance from John divided by the speed of light, because in The Van’s frame that’s the time the light took. So, in The Station’s view, the clocks at the back set themselves too far ahead and the clocks at the front too far behind: the further forward a clock is, the further behind it reads. (Physicists remember this as “leading clocks lag”: in a moving line of synchronised clocks, the ones at the front read behind the ones at the back, as seen from a frame they’re moving through.) That is what Albert’s clocks showed, at noon by The Station’s clocks: fifteen microseconds before noon at gate B, at the front, and fifteen after it at gate A. As far as The Station is concerned, nothing strange happened to the flashes. John’s clocks were simply set wrong.

The Van can say the same about The Station. In The Van’s frame, The Station is the thing moving, backwards, so The Station’s synchronising flash, sent from the middle of a moving girder, reached gate A, which was coming towards it, sooner, and gate B later. In The Van’s view, The Station’s clocks are the ones out of step: when both gates’ clocks said noon, B’s had in fact said it thirty microseconds earlier. Each frame’s clocks are properly set by its own lights, and each frame thinks the other’s are set wrong. There is no third, neutral frame to appeal to, any more than the first journey could find a neutral speedometer. That is the relativity of simultaneity: both sets of clocks are working, and “in step” means something different in each frame, because light’s speed is the same in each.

Drawing time

To see how both accounts can be true at once, it helps to draw them, on a kind of picture this series will use again and again. It was introduced in 1908 by the mathematician Hermann Minkowski, who had once taught Einstein, in a lecture announcing that space by itself and time by itself would fade into shadows, leaving only a union of the two. That union is spacetime.

Spacetime

Space and time taken together as a single whole: every place, at every moment. Each event has its own position in spacetime, given by where it happened and when. Every inertial frame divides spacetime into a space and a time, and frames moving relative to each other divide it differently, which is why they can disagree about which events happen at the same time.

Albert’s napkin

Back at the café, John was trying to explain the experiment to Cal with the salt, the pepper and two teaspoons, and it wasn’t going well.

Albert put down a clean paper napkin and a pen. “Draw it,” he said. “But not the way you’re thinking. Draw time as well.”

He drew an arrow along the bottom of the napkin. “This way is where: along the girder, along the car park, along anything you like.” He drew another arrow, going up the side. “And this way is when. Later is further up.” He put a dot in the middle. “The doughnut landing on your plate: there, and then. One place, one moment. A dot.”

“That’s an event,” said John, who remembered the word from The Station.

“It is.” Albert drew a line straight up the napkin. “Your cup, sitting on the table. It stays in the same place while time passes, so its line goes straight up.” Then he drew a line that leaned over. “Cal, walking to The Van. His place changes as time passes, so his line leans. If he walked faster, it would lean further.”

A line like that, a thing’s whole history in a single stroke, every place it is at every moment, is called its worldline.

“Now the clever part,” said Albert. “Choose the size of your steps so that one step up the napkin is the time light takes to go one step across.” At The Station, for instance, a step across might be three kilometres, and then a step up is ten microseconds, the time light takes to cover three kilometres. “Then a flash of light goes one step across for every step up, and its line leans over at exactly forty-five degrees.” From a single dot, he drew two diagonal lines, one leaning left and one leaning right, like a V. “One for the light going each way.”

“And The Van?” said John.

“Leans over, if it’s going fast. Never as far as light.” He drew a steep line between the two arms of the V. “Nothing does.”

Then he tapped the point of the V. “Call this here and now. Everything inside the V, above it, is everything that light, or anything slower than light, could reach from here and now. The same V turned upside down, below, holds everything that could have reached you. In a world with more room than a napkin, light spreads out in every direction and the V becomes a cone, and that is what it’s called: a light cone.” Last of all he drew a dashed line straight across the napkin, through the point of the V. “And this is now: everything, everywhere, that happens at the same moment as here and now.”

“As far as The Station’s concerned,” said Cal.

“For whichever frame the napkin is drawn in,” said Albert. “That is going to be the whole problem.”

That is all a spacetime diagram is. Places across, times up; an event is a point, and a thing’s history is a line; light’s lines lean at 45 degrees, and nothing material can lean further, however fast it goes; and a level line joins events that happen at the same time, in the frame the diagram is drawn for.

Spacetime diagram

A drawing of events with one direction for position and another for time, conventionally position across the page and time up it, with the units chosen so that light travels along lines at 45 degrees. (The time up the page is often marked as a distance, the distance light covers in that time, as The Station’s diagram marks ten microseconds as three kilometres.) Each event, a single happening at one place and one moment (as the third journey used the word), is a point on the diagram. A spacetime diagram is drawn in one particular frame of reference: its horizontal lines join events that frame calls simultaneous.

Worldline

The line an object traces through a spacetime diagram: the whole of its history, every place it is at every moment. Something at rest in the diagram’s frame has a vertical worldline; something moving steadily has a straight, sloping one; something that speeds up, slows down or turns has a curved one. Nothing material, from a doughnut to The Van, can have a worldline sloping as far as light’s, at 45 degrees.

The flashes, drawn

Now draw The Station’s experiment, in The Station’s frame. The two gates and the middle of the girder stay put, so their worldlines are three vertical lines, six kilometres apart. The Van’s worldline slopes, passing the middle at noon. The two flashes are two points at the same height, noon, one on each gate’s worldline, and The Station’s “now” at noon is the horizontal line through both. Light from each flash runs off at 45 degrees both ways. The two inward light lines meet on the middle’s worldline, twenty microseconds above noon; the one from B meets The Van’s worldline early, and the one from A has to chase it.

The next figure runs the whole experiment, in two panels. The first shows The Station, its gates and its middle, with its clocks underneath; The Van, with John at its middle; Albert’s line of clocks above, riding with The Van; and the light from the flashes, spreading out. The second shows the same thing as a spacetime diagram, with a line marked “now” climbing up it as time goes by. Each clock shows how many microseconds it reads before or after noon.

Play it through in The Station’s frame first, with Light arriving chosen. Watch the two flashes happen together, the light meet in the middle of the girder, and John see B’s light first and A’s long afterwards. Notice Albert’s clocks along the top: in The Station’s frame, they don’t agree with each other, and they read further behind the further forward they are.

Now choose The Van’s frame. Everything is drawn again, relative to The Van. John and Albert’s clocks sit still, and the girder slides backwards past them. Now Albert’s clocks all agree, and it is The Station’s clocks underneath that read differently from each other. Play it through, and watch B flash first, then A, and their light reach John from equal distances on both sides, B’s first. Then choose Times assigned: the diagram draws each frame’s lines of “now” through the two flashes, level for the frame it’s drawn in and tilted for the other, and marks where the tilted ones cross John’s worldline (or, drawn in The Van’s frame, the middle’s) with the reading of that clock there. Change The Van’s speed, and watch the disagreement grow with it; set the speed to zero, and both frames agree about everything.

Two frames on one diagram

The spacetime diagram, drawn in The Station’s frame, shows The Station’s simultaneity as horizontal lines. What do The Van’s lines of “now” look like on the same diagram?

Why The Van’s now is tilted

Use Einstein’s rule, and do it on the diagram. Take any event, say the flash at gate A. To give it a time on The Van’s clocks, John sends a flash of light towards gate A, timed so that it arrives just as A flashes, and A’s light comes straight back to him. On the diagram, John’s flash goes out along a 45-degree line from his sloping worldline to the event, and the echo comes back along the other 45-degree direction to his worldline again. The time The Van gives the event is half-way between the moment John sent his flash and the moment the echo arrived, measured on John’s own clock.

On The Station’s worldline, which is vertical, out-and-back light makes a symmetrical shape, and the half-way point is level with the event: The Station’s “now” through A is horizontal. But John’s worldline slopes towards the right. Light going out to the left from it and coming back has further to come back, because John has moved on. The out-and-back shape is lopsided, and the half-way point on John’s worldline is higher than the event. So the line joining A’s flash to the moment John’s clocks give it, John’s line of “now” through A, tilts upwards towards the right.

Work it through for every event, and every one of The Van’s lines of “now” tilts by the same amount, in the same direction. On The Station’s diagram, The Van’s “now” is a set of parallel lines, tilted up towards the direction The Van is moving, by the same angle that The Van’s worldline is tilted away from the vertical. The faster The Van goes, the further both tilt, like the two blades of a pair of scissors closing on the 45-degree line of light from opposite sides. A line of “now” can never tilt as far as 45 degrees, any more than a worldline can.

That one picture explains the experiment. B’s flash lies to the right of A’s, so a line tilted up towards the right through A’s flash passes above B’s flash: by the time The Van’s “now” reaches gate B, B has already flashed. In The Van’s frame, B came first.

A warning about these drawings

On paper, The Van’s tilted axes look like The Station’s turned through an angle, the way you might turn a map, but a turned map keeps its distances, and a change of frame keeps something else: a combination of time and distance in which, where Pythagoras would add two squares, spacetime subtracts them. That minus sign is what makes the two geometries different, and it is why the tilted axes squeeze towards the light line instead of turning round.

Can effect happen before cause?

At this point, relativity sounds alarmingly like permission for events to happen in any order whatsoever. If The Van’s frame says B flashed first, and a frame moving the other way says A did, what stops a frame from saying that a firework exploded before anyone lit the fuse?

Light does.

The light cone

Take any event, say A’s flash. Light from it spreads out at the speed of light in every direction. On a spacetime diagram, with one direction of space, that’s two lines leaving the event at 45 degrees, upwards to the left and upwards to the right. With all three directions of space it would be a cone, an expanding sphere of light drawn at every moment, so that is what it’s called. Every event has one, opening upwards into its future, and another opening downwards into its past: the light that could have reached it.

Light cone

The set of all the places light from an event could reach, at every later moment: on a spacetime diagram, the lines at 45 degrees leaving the event upwards (its future light cone). Nothing, signal or object, travels faster than light, so everything the event can possibly affect lies on or inside its future light cone. Its past light cone, opening downwards, holds every event that could have affected it. Light’s speed is the same in every inertial frame, so every frame draws the same light cones.

Timelike and spacelike

Changing frame tilts the lines of “now”, but it never moves a light cone, and that divides every pair of events into two kinds.

Timelike and spacelike

Two events are timelike separated if one lies inside the other’s light cone: something travelling slower than light could get from one to the other, so the first could cause the second. Every inertial frame agrees which came first. They are spacelike separated if each lies outside the other’s light cone: they are too far apart, for the time between them, for even light to connect them. Then neither can have caused the other, and frames moving differently can disagree about their order, or say they happened together. Events joined exactly by light, on each other’s light cones, are called lightlike, and every frame agrees about their order too.

The two flashes at The Station were twelve kilometres apart, and simultaneous in The Station’s frame: light would have needed forty microseconds to get from one to the other, and they had none at all. So they are spacelike separated. Neither could have caused the other, and so nothing goes wrong if their order depends on the frame: no story, in any frame, has one flash making the other happen. The Van, going one way, says B came first; something going the other way says A came first; The Station says they were simultaneous. All are right.

Now suppose the middle of The Station, on seeing A’s flash, waits ten microseconds and sends a reply. The reply happens thirty microseconds after A’s flash and six kilometres away: well inside A’s future light cone, because something going at two-thirds of the speed of light could have carried the news. Those two events are timelike separated, and no frame’s “now” can tilt enough to put the reply before the flash. Cause comes before effect, in every frame.

Why nothing can go faster than light

Light cones also explain the cosmic speed limit. Suppose something could carry a message faster than light, from A’s flash to some event outside its light cone. Some frame, The Van or another, would give that second event an earlier time than the flash. In that frame, the message arrives before it is sent. Given a second such message, sent back, you could arrange for a reply to arrive before the question was asked, and the universe would fill with paradoxes. Relativity keeps the order of cause and effect safe, without any universal clock, precisely because nothing can outrun light. The seventh journey will find the other side of the same coin: why nothing can be pushed past it, either.

John, reading over Cal’s shoulder in The Van’s cabin, summed it up.

“So two fireworks on opposite sides of the sky can have no definite order,” he said, “but whoever lights a fuse still does it before the firework goes off.”

“Yes,” said Cal.

“Good. I’d hate to lose that.”

One more figure draws The Station’s events on a spacetime diagram twice: first in The Station’s frame, then in The Van’s. In each, the frame drawn has its time and place square to the page, the other frame’s are tilted, and the dashed lines are each frame’s “now” through A’s flash. A’s light cone is the shaded hourglass. Under each diagram is that frame’s verdict: which came first.

Play it, and let The Van’s speed sweep one way and back. Watch The Van’s lines tilt in The Station’s diagram, and The Station’s tilt the other way in The Van’s, while the light cone stays exactly where it is in both. With Two flashes, The Station always says they happened together, while The Van says B came first when it is moving one way, A first when it is moving the other, and together only when it is standing still. Then try A flash and its light, and A flash and a reply, where the second event is on or inside A’s light cone. However fast The Van goes, in either direction, A comes first.

Andromeda’s now

Which brings us back to John’s comforting thought, and to Andromeda.

In any one inertial frame, it makes perfectly good sense to ask which distant events are simultaneous with a given moment at the café: they are the events on that frame’s line of “now” through that moment. The trouble is that the line tilts with the frame, and the tilt is multiplied by distance. At The Station, six-tenths of the speed of light over twelve kilometres made thirty microseconds’ difference. At ordinary speeds over ordinary distances the difference is unmeasurably small. But Andromeda is about two and a half million light-years away, and over that distance even a stroll matters.

Suppose Cal stands on the plateau, and John strolls past him, towards Andromeda’s part of the sky, at five kilometres an hour. Their lines of “now” are tilted relative to each other by the smallest of angles. But stretched out across two and a half million light-years, that tiny tilt adds up: the events at Andromeda that John’s frame calls “now” are about four days later, by Andromeda’s reckoning, than the ones Cal’s frame calls “now”. The physicist Roger Penrose made this vivid in his book The Emperor’s New Mind (1989), with two people walking past each other in the street, one of whom would say that an invasion fleet in Andromeda had already set off, and the other that its commanders had not yet decided to send it. It is sometimes called the Andromeda paradox.

It sounds alarming, and it is easy to read too much into it. John’s stroll changes nothing in Andromeda, and it doesn’t let him see Andromeda’s future: the light from those events, in either frame’s “now”, won’t arrive for two and a half million years. What changes with John’s stroll is only which distant events his frame’s clocks would call simultaneous with this moment, which is a matter of bookkeeping.

What relativity takes away is a single, universal “now”, spread across the whole universe like a sheet, the same for everybody. What it leaves is something firmer: the light cones, the same for everybody, and with them the order of every pair of events that could ever affect each other.

“That’s either profoundly beautiful,” said John, “or the most elaborate clock problem ever invented.”

“Possibly both,” said Cal.

Back at the café

The Van landed in the fading light, with a sigh from its suspension and a short, apologetic beep from the kettle. The red dust on its steps was from a previous expedition, but John had started calling it scientific evidence, because it sounded better than poor housekeeping.

Albert’s clocks went back into their shoebox, all showing different times again, which Albert said was how he liked them.

They sat outside while the light went. The mountain turned from gold to rust to a dark shape against the sky, and then the stars came out, a few at a time and then all at once, the way they do on the plateau.

Cal looked towards the north-east, where the autumn constellations were climbing, and found the faint grey smudge above the ridge.

“What’s happening there right now?” he asked.

John had an answer this time, though it was a little longer than before.

“First,” he said, “tell me whose now you mean.”

Cal thought about it. “Mine. Sitting here.”

“Then something happened there, a long time after the light you’re looking at left it, that your frame counts as now. If I get up and walk to The Van, my frame counts something else. Neither of us will ever see either of them. And it doesn’t matter, because nothing that happens there now can touch us here for two and a half million years.”

“That’s less comforting than your comforting thought.”

“It’s more honest.”

Albert came out with the coffee pot. He nodded, which was the most he usually gave away. Then he looked towards the lights of the car park, where The Van’s dashboard clock was still showing a sandwich.

“You have learned,” he said, “that observers disagree about which distant events share a moment. Next you must ask whether they agree about the distance between them.”

John looked at The Van, and thought about The Station’s girder, just under twelve kilometres long, as far as The Station was concerned.

“Oh,” he said. “I don’t think I’m going to like that.”
