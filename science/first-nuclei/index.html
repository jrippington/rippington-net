Albert's old oven gets ferociously hot and cools the moment its door is opened, so only the quickest things bake. The universe had an oven like it. In The Van's simulator, John and Cal watch the first minutes: a race between neutrons falling apart and a first step the light keeps smashing, a quarter of the matter turned to helium, and a pinch of heavy hydrogen left over, which weighs the universe.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 3 of 18

Too hot, then too cold

Albert’s oven was older than the café. It stood behind the counter at the far end, a squat black box of iron with a door like a safe’s, and it had two settings: off, and far too hot. Its thermostat had given up long before anyone could remember, and Albert had never seen the point of mending it. He lit it in the dark, before the first coaches, let it roar until the iron ticked, and turned off the gas. Then he opened the door, and from that moment the oven cooled, quickly, and whatever he wanted baked had to be baked on the way down.

John found him there the morning after Fred’s visit, in an apron dusted with flour, watching a thermometer the size of a saucer on the oven door. On the counter were two trays. On one were rounds of biscuit dough, rolled no thicker than a coin and dusted with sugar. On the other was a tall tin of fruit loaf, heavy with currants.

“You said you were baking,” said John.

“I said it,” said Albert, “because you asked what was made first. Watch.”

The needle on the thermometer stood past the last number on the dial. Albert opened the door a crack, and John stepped back from the breath of heat. The needle began to fall.

“If I put them in now,” said Albert, “they burn. Whatever starts to bake scorches before it can set.”

He waited while the needle swung down through the numbers. Then, at a mark on the dial he seemed to know without looking, he slid both trays in and shut the door. Shutting it made little difference: the oven was losing its heat through every seam.

Three minutes later he took out the biscuits, golden at the edges and crisp right through. Twenty minutes after that, with the needle near the bottom of the dial and the oven hardly warmer than the kitchen, he took out the loaf. It had a pale crust on top. When he cut into it, the middle was raw.

Cal had come in from the car park in time to see it cut, and looked at it with sympathy.

“Shame about the loaf,” he said.

“Not a shame,” said Albert. “A result. In an oven too hot to bake anything for the first few minutes, and too cold to bake anything soon after, only the quickest and simplest things get done. The biscuits are thin. The loaf needs an hour.” He tipped the biscuits on to a rack to cool. “The universe had an oven like this, once. So ask what was simple enough to bake in a few minutes.”

Cal took the grain of sugar out of his shirt pocket, still folded into its napkin, with the words on the outside where he could see them.

DO NOT EAT

“Hydrogen, carbon and oxygen,” he said. “Which of those is simple enough?”

“Hydrogen,” said John at once. “Its nucleus is a single proton. You can’t get simpler than that.”

“Then hold on to it,” said Albert. “It is not quite what you think.”

Ten billion degrees

They took the biscuits out to The Van. Yesterday the simulator had run the universe backwards, from the galaxies of today to a glowing fog and on into heat too fierce for any eye. Today John turned its HISTORY dial the other way, and set it going forwards from one second after the beginning.

HISTORY: FORWARD FROM 1 SECOND

The dashboard screen went white from edge to edge, too bright to look at, and the simulator dimmed it to a glare.

TEMPERATURE: 10 BILLION DEGREES. RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

The second journey found where the simulator’s confidence comes from. Nothing has been seen from that time: no light from it reaches us, because for hundreds of thousands of years afterwards the universe was a fog, too thick for light to cross, and the fog’s edge, the surface of last scattering, is as far back as any light can show. But nothing at ten billion degrees is exotic either. It’s the physics of nuclei, measured in laboratories for a century, in reactors and in accelerators, reaction by reaction.

So the simulator could draw the soup with some confidence. Everything we can see today was packed into a ball about eighteen light-years across, and everything in it was swarming: protons, and neutrons, the particles about as heavy as protons, with no electric charge, which live with protons in every nucleus but hydrogen’s; electrons, and their antimatter twins, positrons; lumps of light, far more of them than anything else; and neutrinos, ghostly particles able to pass through nearly everything, which the ninth journey meets properly. There were no atoms and no nuclei. At ten billion degrees nothing could hold together but a single proton or neutron.

Two kinds of particle, one of them dearer

In the soup, protons and neutrons were turning into each other all the time, in collisions with the electrons, positrons and neutrinos round them. A neutron hit hard enough in the right way became a proton; a proton hit in the right way became a neutron.

The two aren’t quite equal. A neutron weighs a little more than a proton, by about a seventh of one per cent, the weight of two and a half electrons. And The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe found that weight is energy, by E = mc². So turning a proton into a neutron costs energy, that little extra weight’s worth, and turning a neutron back into a proton gives it back.

Albert looked pleased, as he generally did when E = mc² came into anything.

While the soup was much hotter than that cost, it hardly mattered: there was energy everywhere for the dearer particle, and the swapping made neutrons as fast as protons. But as it cooled, the cost began to tell. A theatre with two kinds of seat sells both while its customers are rich; when money is short, the cheaper seats fill and the dearer ones empty. The cooling soup made fewer and fewer neutrons, and more and more protons.

Then the swapping stopped. It needed the neutrinos, and the neutrinos had almost stopped hitting anything. As the soup thinned and cooled, a neutrino went further and further between collisions, until, a second or two after the beginning, it could expect to travel for longer than the universe had existed without touching a thing, as it passes through the whole Earth today. Whatever share of neutrons there was then, there it stayed.

NEUTRONS: 1 FOR EVERY 5 PROTONS. SWAPPING: STOPPED

Something else had happened while they watched. The positrons were going. As the soup cooled through a few billion degrees, there was no longer energy enough to keep making electrons and positrons in pairs, as What the Sugar Is Made Of found energy can, and each positron soon met an electron, and the two vanished together into light. Within the first minute most of the positrons had gone, and within a few hours the last of them, leaving one electron for every proton. Why there was anything left at all is a question for the end of this journey.

A clock that’s running

“So now they just stick together,” said John. “Protons and neutrons. It’s hot enough for fusion. Hotter than the Sun.”

It was: at ten seconds the soup was still about three billion degrees, two hundred times hotter than the Sun’s core. But there was a clock running, and Cal had spotted it on the screen first.

“The neutrons are disappearing,” he said.

They were. A neutron on its own doesn’t last. A free neutron turns itself into a proton, throwing out an electron and an antineutrino as it goes. It’s the weak force at work, the force whose rare changes, as Through the Wall found, make the Sun burn so slowly; here it runs the other way. It’s radioactive, and like any radioactive thing it goes by chance, with a half-life: about ten minutes. Every minute and a half, about one in ten of the free neutrons left becomes a proton. Inside a stable nucleus, held by the strong pull of the particles round it, a neutron is safe for ever; most of the neutrons in the grain of sugar’s carbon and oxygen have been there for billions of years. But out on its own, it’s on a clock.

So the oven had a race on. Every neutron not locked into a nucleus within the next few minutes would become a proton, and a lone proton is hydrogen. Whatever could be made of neutrons had to be made soon, before the neutrons were gone and before the soup was too cool to make anything.

The easiest step in nuclear physics

The first step ought to be easy, and it is. In the Sun, as Through the Wall found, two protons have to get close enough to touch, against the electric push between two positive charges, and they manage it only by leaking through the electric hill between them. A neutron has no charge. There’s no hill between a neutron and a proton at all: they come together, touch, and the strong pull takes hold. Two particles, one of each, stuck together, and a lump of light flies off with the energy they gave up.

The result is a nucleus with one proton, and since the protons decide which element an atom belongs to, it’s still hydrogen. But it’s hydrogen with an extra neutron, twice as heavy as the ordinary kind. It’s called deuterium, from the Greek for “second”, and it’s the first of the things the oven made.

Isotope

One of the different forms of a chemical element, whose nuclei have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. The protons decide which element it is, and so its chemistry, since they set how many electrons its atoms hold; the neutrons add weight, and decide whether the nucleus lasts. Hydrogen has three isotopes: ordinary hydrogen, a single proton; deuterium, a proton and a neutron; and tritium, a proton and two neutrons, which is radioactive. Isotopes are named by their element and their count of protons and neutrons together: helium-4 has two of each, and helium-3 one neutron fewer.

Deuterium isn’t rare on Earth. About one hydrogen atom in every six and a half thousand in the water in Albert’s kettle is deuterium, and a molecule of water with deuterium in place of its hydrogen is the “heavy water” of nuclear reactors. Every deuterium nucleus in it began in the first minutes of the universe.

But in the soup, at three billion degrees, the deuterium wasn’t lasting. On the screen, pairs of particles stuck together in flashes all over the soup, and broke apart again at once.

DEUTERIUM: FORMING. DEUTERIUM: BROKEN

The bottleneck

Deuterium is the most fragile nucleus there is. Pulling its proton and neutron apart takes about 2.2 million electronvolts (the tiny unit of energy for single particles of Why the Atom Survives): about 160,000 times the price of freeing hydrogen’s electron, but, shared between its two particles, less than any other nucleus needs. A lump of light carrying 2.2 million electronvolts or more, hitting a deuterium nucleus, breaks it. And at one second, at ten billion degrees, the typical lump of light in the soup carried just about that much.

“But it’s cooling,” said Cal. “By ten seconds the typical lump has a third of that. By a minute, a sixth. It should be safe by now.”

It wasn’t. A minute went by on the simulator’s clock, and the deuterium was still breaking as fast as it formed. Two minutes, and nothing had changed except that there were fewer free neutrons.

Albert didn’t say anything. He went back into the café and came out again with the little card from his till, which John and Cal had last seen in Red Hot, White Hot, and propped it against the windscreen.

WHOLE DOUGHNUTS ONLY. NO SLICES. NO CLUBBING TOGETHER

Albert’s till, as Red Hot, White Hot found, works as light does. Each customer pays alone, from what’s in their own pocket, and a crowd of customers each with a few coins can’t club together to buy a doughnut, however many of them there are. Here the customers are the lumps of light, and what they’re buying is the breaking of a deuterium nucleus. It costs one big note, 2.2 million electronvolts, paid by a single lump.

In a glow, most lumps of light carry about the typical amount, but not all: a few carry much more, and fewer the bigger the amount. That’s the long tail of a hot body’s glow, trailing away towards short, energetic waves. So as the soup cooled, the big notes didn’t vanish all at once. They became rare. At ten seconds, about one lump in seventy could still pay for a deuterium nucleus. At a minute, about one lump in a hundred and forty thousand.

What Cal had left out was how many lumps of light there were. In the soup there were more than a billion and a half lumps of light for every single proton and neutron. (How anyone knows that is a story for later in this journey: the oven’s own leftovers count them.) One lump in a hundred and forty thousand, out of a billion and a half, is still about twelve thousand big notes for every particle in the soup. Every deuterium nucleus to form was broken up almost at once, and nothing heavier could be built, because everything heavier has to be built through deuterium.

Step by step:

  • To make anything heavier than a single proton or neutron, the oven had to make deuterium first, because nuclei are built two particles at a time; three meeting at once almost never happens.
  • Deuterium is held together so weakly that a single lump of light with a little over two million electronvolts breaks it.
  • The lumps of light outnumbered the particles by more than a billion and a half to one, so even when only a tiny share of them had that much energy, there were more of those than there were nuclei to break.
  • So deuterium could only last once the big notes were fewer than the nuclei: once fewer than about one lump of light in a billion and a half could pay.

That took the glow until it was a little under a billion degrees. On the simulator’s clock it was about three and a half minutes after the beginning. Steven Weinberg, who wrote a famous book about all this, called it a bottleneck, and the name has stuck: the deuterium bottleneck.

Three and a half minutes

Then it broke.

On the screen, the flashes of deuterium stopped breaking apart, and in seconds there was deuterium everywhere. It didn’t stay deuterium for long. A deuterium nucleus meeting a proton became helium-3, two protons and a neutron; two deuterium nuclei meeting became helium-3 or tritium; and helium-3 or tritium meeting one more particle became helium-4, two protons and two neutrons.

Helium-4 is where everything ends up, because it’s held together far more firmly than anything lighter. Pulling a helium-4 nucleus right apart costs about seven million electronvolts for each of its four particles, against a little over one for each of deuterium’s two and about two and a half for each of helium-3’s three. Once nuclei can form at all, they run down into helium-4 as water runs down into the deepest hollow, and stay there. Within about two minutes of the bottleneck breaking, nearly every neutron still free had ended up in a helium-4 nucleus, two to each.

HELIUM-4: A QUARTER BY WEIGHT. RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

“A quarter,” said John. “Why a quarter?”

“Count,” said Albert.

By the time the bottleneck broke, the clock had been running for three and a half minutes, and the neutrons, one for every five protons when the swapping stopped, were down to about one for every seven. Take sixteen particles at that moment: two neutrons and fourteen protons. The two neutrons go into one helium-4 nucleus, and take two of the protons with them. That’s four of the sixteen particles in helium, and twelve protons left on their own, as hydrogen. Protons and neutrons weigh almost the same, so four of sixteen is a quarter of the weight. The oven made a universe about a quarter helium and three quarters hydrogen, by weight; counting nuclei instead, about one helium for every twelve hydrogen.

Every step of that is something measured on Earth. The neutron’s extra weight sets how many neutrons there were while the swapping went on. How rarely neutrinos hit anything, measured in laboratories too, sets when the swapping stopped. The neutron’s half-life sets how many were left at three and a half minutes, and deuterium’s fragility, with the count of the lumps of light, sets when the bottleneck broke. The only way out would be for some of the neutrons to end up somewhere other than helium-4, and there’s nowhere else for them to go: helium-4 is the deepest hollow there is among the light nuclei. The answer comes out a quarter, give or take a little, and even the amount of matter in the universe hardly changes it.

The helium weighs a little less than the protons and neutrons it was made from, as Through the Wall found of the Sun’s helium, and the difference went out as energy, into the glow. Albert pointed that out, with satisfaction. In the oven it hardly mattered: by E = mc², the glow itself outweighed all the matter in the soup, hundreds of thousands of times over.

No stepping stones

“And then it carries on,” said John. “Helium plus a proton, helium plus a helium. Carbon. Oxygen. It’s still hot.”

It’s what the first people to think about the oven expected too. In the 1940s George Gamow, who had worked out at twenty-four how an alpha particle tunnels out of a nucleus, and his student Ralph Alpher, in Washington, set out to make every element in the first minutes of the universe. Their idea was that the young universe began as a hot, dense gas of neutrons, and that nuclei were built up by catching neutrons one at a time, each catch making a nucleus one particle heavier, up through the whole table of the elements. Their paper appeared on 1 April 1948, and Gamow, who couldn’t resist it, added the name of his friend Hans Bethe, who hadn’t worked on it, so that it would be signed Alpher, Bethe, Gamow, like the first three letters of the Greek alphabet. Alpher, whose thesis it was, was not amused: he thought, rightly, that the famous names would get the credit.

The trouble was found within a couple of years, by Enrico Fermi and Anthony Turkevich in Chicago, who worked through the reactions one by one. Catching particles one at a time is like crossing a stream on stepping stones, one stone for each weight of nucleus: a stone at one particle, at two, at three, at four. And at five there is no stone. No nucleus with five particles lasts: helium-5 and lithium-5 both fall apart in about a billionth of a trillionth of a second. Nor does anything with eight: two helium-4s stuck together make beryllium-8, which falls back into two heliums in about a ten-thousandth of a trillionth of a second. In 1950 the Japanese physicist Chushiro Hayashi added the other correction, that the young universe can’t have been all neutrons, because the swapping would have made them into protons too; the neutron’s share comes from the swapping, as this journey found.

So the road beyond helium had gaps in it, at five and at eight. The oven could get round the first, a little: a helium-3 and a helium-4 meeting make seven particles, jumping straight over five, and that made a trace of beryllium-7, which later became lithium-7. That’s the oven’s heaviest product, about one lithium nucleus for every two billion of hydrogen. Beyond it, at eight, the road stopped.

But the gaps aren’t the whole of the story, and on their own they aren’t even the bigger part. The other reason was the oven itself. Every step beyond helium needs nuclei with more protons in them to come together, and the more charge two nuclei carry, the harder they push each other apart: two heliums push four times as hard as two protons, a helium and a lithium six times as hard. To get through those hills, nuclei need heat and crowding and time, and the oven was losing all three. By the time helium was made it was under a billion degrees and falling, and by twenty minutes it was down to a few hundred million. Its nuclei were spread about as thinly as the molecules of the air on the plateau, and ten million times more thinly than the particles in the Sun’s core. Physicists who have tried putting a stable nucleus of five or eight particles into their calculations, just to see, find that the oven still makes almost nothing heavier. It was too cool, too thin and too brief. The loaf needed an hour.

OVEN: TOO COLD. RESULT: HYDROGEN AND HELIUM

The drawing below is the stepping stones: the lightest nuclei, from one particle to twelve, each stone standing as high as its particles are firmly held. The stones the oven reached are drawn in amber, and the gaps at five and eight are empty.

The stars do get across. In a star’s core, for millions of years at a time, helium is squeezed far more densely than anything in the oven, at a hundred million degrees, and now and then three helium nuclei come together before the first two can fall apart, and make carbon, by a step which only just works. That is the eleventh journey’s story, and it’s where the grain of sugar’s carbon came from. But the oven didn’t make any.

The oldest gas

Everything on the screen so far had come with the same grade, and Cal had noticed.

“Reconstructed,” he said. “All of it. Has anyone checked?”

They had, and this is where the simulator’s confidence earns its keep. If the oven did what the simulator said, the oldest gas in the universe, the gas least touched by stars since, should be about a quarter helium, with a little deuterium and helium-3 and a trace of lithium in it, and nothing else. There are three ways of checking, and each is a different kind of detective work.

Helium

The Sun, the first journey found, is about three quarters hydrogen and a quarter helium by weight. That’s the oven’s quarter, with a little more helium added by earlier stars, which lived and died before the Sun was born. To see the oven’s share alone, astronomers look at gas that has been through as few stars as possible: the glowing clouds in small, faint galaxies, some with as little as a thirtieth of the Sun’s share of oxygen. Such a cloud shines in the bright lines of its elements, as the first journey found the nebulae in John’s pictures do, and the lines say how much helium and how much oxygen it holds. The less oxygen a cloud has, the fewer stars have lived and died in it, and the less helium it has too. Follow that trend back to no oxygen at all, to gas no star has ever touched, and the helium comes out at about 24.5 per cent of the weight. The oven’s prediction is 24.7.

Could the stars have made it all, without any oven? Stars do make helium; it’s what they run on. But a quarter of all the matter is far more helium than all the stars there have ever been could have made in the time, and in 1964 one of the first people to make that argument was Fred himself. He and Roger Tayler, in Cambridge, worked out that the helium must have been made somewhere far hotter and denser than any ordinary star, and they named a hot beginning as one possibility. Fred, characteristically, preferred stars far bigger than any seen today.

“He will tell you so himself,” said Albert, “if you ask him. And then he will tell you something else.”

Deuterium

Deuterium is the oven’s fingerprint, because nothing since has made it in any quantity, and stars only destroy it. It’s so fragile that a star burns all its deuterium at about a million degrees, before it’s even hot enough to start on its hydrogen. So every deuterium nucleus in the universe is one the oven made and nothing has yet destroyed, and to see the oven’s share, astronomers need gas that has hardly been inside a star at all.

They find it between us and the quasars. A quasar is the blazing core of a galaxy so far away that its light set out billions of years ago, and on its way here the light passes through clouds of thin gas, some of it nearly untouched since the oven. Each cloud takes its own colours out of the quasar’s light, the dark lines of the first journey. Hydrogen’s dark lines are there, and just beside each of them, a far fainter line, shifted by about three hundredths of one per cent towards the blue: deuterium’s, its lines very slightly moved because its nucleus is twice as heavy. The first good measurement was made in 1996, by David Tytler, Xiao-Ming Fan and Scott Burles, with the Keck telescope in Hawaii. In 2018 Ryan Cooke, Max Pettini and Charles Steidel put together the best seven clouds and found about two and a half deuterium nuclei for every 100,000 of hydrogen, to within about one per cent.

The water in Albert’s kettle has more deuterium than that, about six times as much, because chemistry in the cold of the young Solar System gathered it into ice. That’s why the oven’s share has to be measured in gas nothing has happened to.

Lithium

The third check doesn’t quite work. The oldest stars in our galaxy, made of gas with almost none of the heavier elements, show lithium’s lines in their light, and in 1982 Monique and François Spite found that they all had about the same amount, as if it were the oven’s. But it’s only about a third of what the oven should have made. Either these old stars have quietly destroyed some of their lithium over twelve billion years, which is possible and hard to prove, or something in the physics of the oven isn’t yet understood. Nobody knows which, and astronomers call it the lithium problem.

“I like that it admits it,” said Cal.

“It is a very honest machine,” said Albert. “I sometimes wish it were a little less honest about my doughnuts.”

Weighing the universe with deuterium

So far, the second slider in the race against the clock has been left alone. It sets how much ordinary matter there is: how many protons and neutrons there were in the soup, compared with the lumps of light.

That changes how much deuterium the oven leaves. When the bottleneck breaks, deuterium appears everywhere and is swept on into helium as fast as it can find partners. With more particles crowded together, it finds them sooner, so more of it is burnt into helium and less is left over at the end. With fewer, more is left. And the amount left is very sensitive to the crowding: double the matter, and the leftover deuterium falls to about a third. The helium hardly changes either way, since nearly every neutron ends up in it regardless. But the deuterium is a set of scales.

Go back to the race against the clock and move the ordinary matter slider. The deuterium left at the end moves with it, against the band showing the amount measured in the oldest gas. Only one amount of matter puts it there; the button finds it. With too little matter, too little deuterium is burnt and too much is left; with too much matter, the opposite. The calculation is only as good as the reactions it’s built from, and the least certain of them, deuterium catching a proton, was measured in 2020 at the energies of the first minutes, by the team called LUNA, in a laboratory under a mountain in Italy, where nearly a kilometre and a half of rock keeps out the cosmic rays.

The amount it picks is very small. Spread through today’s universe, it comes to about one proton’s or neutron’s worth of ordinary matter in every four cubic metres of space. The lumps of light have been stretched and cooled since the oven, and have spread out with everything else, but their number hasn’t changed, and they still fill space, now as a glow less than three degrees above absolute zero, as Further Apart Every Day found. Counted against the matter, there are about one and a half billion of them for every proton and neutron, which is the count the bottleneck needed.

And there’s a stranger result. Further Apart Every Day found, from the way the expansion of the universe has slowed and then sped up, that the universe’s contents are about 30 per cent matter and 70 per cent dark energy. Call that everything. The deuterium says that matter of the ordinary kind, the protons and neutrons atoms are made of, is only about 5 per cent of everything.

ORDINARY MATTER: 5 PER CENT OF EVERYTHING. RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

“Then what’s the rest of the thirty?” said John.

Whatever it is, it isn’t made of protons and neutrons, or the oven would have counted it: it would have crowded the soup, burnt more deuterium, and left less. Five sixths of all the matter in the universe isn’t made of atoms. The deuterium alone can only hint at it, but it was the first hint. Where the rest is, and what it might be, is the fifth journey’s question, and the next one weighs the ordinary matter again, by physics with nothing to do with deuterium.

THE OTHER 95 PER CENT: NOT IN THE OVEN

A billion and one

Cal had been looking at the billion and a half.

“That’s an awful lot of light,” he said, “for not very much stuff. Where did it all come from?”

It’s the wreckage of antimatter. What the Sugar Is Made Of found that energy makes matter and antimatter in exact pairs, a particle with every antiparticle, and that when they meet again, they vanish together into light. Before the first second, in a soup far hotter than the oven, quarks and antiquarks were being made in pairs and unmade into light continually, as the electrons and positrons still were at one second. As the universe cooled, the making stopped, and the matter and antimatter met and vanished, nearly all of it, into light. The positrons John and Cal had watched vanishing in the first minute were the last of it.

But not quite all of it vanished, because there wasn’t quite as much antimatter as matter. For every billion particles of antimatter there were about a billion and one of matter. The billion and the billion met and became light, which is why there’s so much of it. The one was left over, with nothing to meet, and it’s everything: the protons and neutrons the oven cooked, the stars, the café, the grain of sugar, and John and Cal.

Why the one? Nobody knows. Physicists have found, in their accelerators, that matter and antimatter don’t behave quite alike, which is the kind of difference needed, but the differences found so far are far too small to have left that much over. Something else did it, in the first instants, and the simulator, asked, was blunt.

WHY THERE IS ANY MATTER AT ALL: SPECULATION. CONFIDENCE: NONE

“So we’re the leftovers,” said Cal.

“Everything is,” said Albert. “It is only a question of what from.”

Back to Teide

By evening the biscuits were long gone, mostly to the coach parties, and the loaf, raw in the middle, had been cut up for a bread pudding. The light went orange across the plateau, and the café’s windows threw long shapes on the car park.

Cal unfolded the napkin on the table by the window and looked at the grain.

“So its hydrogen,” he said, “isn’t something the oven made at all.”

“No,” said Albert. “Its hydrogen is what the oven did not bake.”

That was what he’d meant in the morning. A hydrogen nucleus is a single proton, and every proton in the grain had come through the oven without being stuck to anything: one of the twelve protons left over in every sixteen particles, and before that one of the one-in-a-billion left over by the antimatter. Twenty-two of the grain’s forty-five atoms are hydrogen, and every one of their nuclei has been a proton on its own since the oven, 13.8 billion years ago.

ORIGIN OF THIS HYDROGEN: RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

Cal folded the napkin again, words outwards, and put it back in his pocket.

On the shelf behind the counter, between the cookery books, was a slim paperback John hadn’t noticed before. He took it down. It was Steven Weinberg’s The First Three Minutes, from 1977, its spine cracked white from reading.

“The best cookery book I own,” said Albert.

John turned it over in his hands. He was thinking of the light: a billion and a half lumps of it for every particle, glowing at a billion degrees, filling the whole of space, with nowhere outside it to go.

“Fred asked where the heat had gone,” he said.

“So he did,” said Albert, and went to wash the trays.

Out in The Van, on a shelf above the kettle, there was a little portable television. It had come with The Van, and in all their journeys nobody had ever switched it on. John switched it on, to see what was on, and pulled out its aerial. Nothing was on. The screen filled with grey snow, swarming and shifting, and the little speaker hissed.

He left it on. It hissed to itself in the dark car park, between the channels, all night.
