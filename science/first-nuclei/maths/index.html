When deuterium can last

Deuterium’s binding energy, the energy it takes to pull its proton and neutron apart, is

B=(mp+mn−md) c2=2.2246 MeV,B = (m_p + m_n - m_d)\,c^2 = 2.2246\ \text{MeV},B=(mp​+mn​−md​)c2=2.2246 MeV,

The neutrons’ share and the helium it makes

The swapping. A neutron is heavier than a proton by

Q=(mn−mp) c2=1.2933 MeV,Q = (m_n - m_p)\,c^2 = 1.2933\ \text{MeV},Q=(mn​−mp​)c2=1.2933 MeV,

0.14 per cent of a proton’s mass and 2.5 times an electron’s. While the weak reactions n+νe↔p+e−n + \nu_e \leftrightarrow p + e^-n+νe​↔p+e− and n+e+↔p+νˉen + e^+ \leftrightarrow p + \bar\nu_en+e+↔p+νˉe​ are fast, the two are in balance, with

np=e−Q/kT:\frac{n}{p} = e^{-Q/kT}:pn​=e−Q/kT:

0.22 at 101010^{10}1010 K (kT=0.86kT = 0.86kT=0.86 MeV). The reactions’ rate falls as about T5T^5T5, the expansion rate as T2T^2T2, and below about 0.8 MeV the swapping can’t keep up. It stops gradually: in the figure’s network, n/pn/pn/p is 1 in 3.2 at 1 second and about 1 in 5 from 10 to 30 seconds.

Why the oven stopped

No five, no eight. An unstable nucleus’s lifetime is τ=ℏ/Γ\tau = \hbar/\Gammaτ=ℏ/Γ, from the energy width Γ\GammaΓ of its state, with ℏ=6.58×10−22\hbar = 6.58 \times 10^{-22}ℏ=6.58×10−22 MeV s. Helium-5’s width is between about 0.5 and 1.4 MeV, depending on how it’s defined and measured, so it lasts about 10−2110^{-21}10−21 seconds; lithium-5’s, about 1.2 to 1.5 MeV, a few times 10−2210^{-22}10−22 seconds. Beryllium-8, two helium-4s, is above them by 92 keV and has a width of 5.6 eV: it lasts about 1.2×10−161.2 \times 10^{-16}1.2×10−16 seconds, a half-life of 8.2×10−178.2 \times 10^{-17}8.2×10−17 seconds.

The calculations. Michael Turner’s history of the subject (2022) puts it plainly: add a stable nucleus of five or eight particles to the calculation, and almost nothing changes. Long before more could be made, the hills, the thinning and the cooling had shut the oven down. The gaps were the end of Gamow and Alpher’s road of neutron catches, as Fermi and Turkevich found; in the real oven, the cold did most of the work.

The light-to-matter ratio

The oldest light is a glow at T0=2.7255T_0 = 2.7255T0​=2.7255 K, and the number of photons in a glow is

nB=6.0×10−10×4.107×108 m−3=0.25 m−3,n_B = 6.0 \times 10^{-10} \times 4.107 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}^{-3} = 0.25\ \text{m}^{-3},nB​=6.0×10−10×4.107×108 m−3=0.25 m−3,

Matter and antimatter. Before about a millionth of a second, quarks and antiquarks were about as numerous as photons. Each proton or neutron left now is three quarks of their excess, and there are 1/η=1.6×1091/\eta = 1.6 \times 10^91/η=1.6×109 photons for each, most of them made when the rest met and vanished. So the excess of quarks over antiquarks was a few parts in a billion: “a billion and one for every billion” is the right size, not an exact count.
