It's closing time, and Albert and his old friend are still arguing. The rules of quantum mechanics have never once been caught out, and they still don't say what happens when anyone looks. John and Cal hear four answers, each at its strongest, and The Van's simulator shows them that the dots on the screen can't tell the four apart. Not yet.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Field Guide to the Very Small, part 15 of 16

Closing time

By ten o’clock the café was empty but for the four of them. The chairs were upside down on the tables, the fryer had been cleaned and switched off, and Albert was wiping the counter for the third time, which he did when he was thinking. The doughnut box from the morning sat flattened by the till. Beside it, under its upturned glass, the grain of sugar glittered in the last of the light.

Niels had been there since the afternoon. He had drunk three cups of tea, made with water from The Van’s kettle, which he had pronounced, very quietly, perhaps the best tea he had ever had on the plateau, and possibly the only tea. He was sitting at the end of the counter with his hands round the last cup, and he and Albert had been arguing for six hours.

They argued the way old friends do, without heat and without ever quite agreeing. Albert made a point, briskly. Niels considered it for so long that everyone leaned in, and then answered so softly, and with so many careful qualifications, that it was hard to be sure he had disagreed at all until Albert threw down his cloth.

“You cannot ask the electron what it was doing between the slits,” Niels was saying. “There is, perhaps, nobody to ask. There is only what the experiment shows, and the experiment shows a dot.”

“Then I shall ask about something larger.” Albert leaned on the counter. “Do you really believe the Moon is only there when somebody looks at it?”

Niels thought about it for a long time.

“I believe,” he said at last, “that I could not show you otherwise without looking.”

“I’ve been to the Moon,” said John. “It was definitely there.”

“You were looking,” said Cal.

Albert laughed, and even Niels smiled into his tea. Then Cal turned on his stool and looked at the flattened box.

“This morning I said I’d know,” he said. “If that box had really been doing both things at once, eaten and uneaten, and me with it, I’d remember which. I’d find one. And Albert said it was a better question than I thought.”

“It is the question,” said Albert. “It is the one he and I have been arguing about since before you were born.” He pointed his cloth at John. “But you should not take it from us. Tell me what the rules actually say.”

Two rules

John turned over a napkin. Over fourteen journeys the rules had come in pieces, and he wanted them in one place.

“Rule one,” he said, writing. “Between looks, every possibility has its arrow, and the arrows turn steadily, all of them together.” That was the fifth journey’s discovery, and the eighth journey’s equation, Schrödinger’s, says exactly how the arrows change from moment to moment. Nothing is ever chosen under rule one. A superposition stays a superposition, and anything that touches it joins in, as the counter, the box, the air and Cal had joined in that morning.

“Rule two,” said Cal, taking the pen. “When you look, you find one result. Just one. And the chance of each result is set by how long its arrow is.”

They looked at the napkin. Both rules have been tested for a hundred years, in every laboratory on Earth, to more decimal places than anything else in science, and neither has ever been caught out. Rule one gives the stripes, the atom’s colours, the Sun’s tunnelling and the results of every Bell test. Rule two is how every one of those experiments is read: a click, a dot, a number.

“So what’s wrong with them?” said John.

“Nothing,” said Albert. “Until you ask when to use which.”

That is the whole of the trouble, and it takes a moment to see.

Rule two talks about looking. But what is looking? The detector at the slits is made of atoms, and atoms obey rule one. So, by rule one, when an electron in a superposition of A and B reaches the detector, the detector ends up in a superposition of “saw A” and “saw B”, exactly as the counter in the box did. Then the photograph of the detector’s dial joins in, and the light from the photograph, and John’s eye, and John’s brain. Follow rule one as far as you like, and it never produces one result. It produces a bigger and bigger superposition, with everything in it. Yet rule two says that when you look, you find one.

Where does rule one stop and rule two take over? At the detector? At the photograph? At the eye, or the brain? The theory doesn’t say. It has two rules, and it never says which one applies.

Decoherence, the last journey’s discovery, doesn’t settle it. It explains why the detector never looks doubled: once the air and the light have recorded “saw A” and “saw B”, the two can never interfere again. But decoherence is rule one, working as usual. It never picks one of them.

John had a go at an answer of his own.

“Then looking is when a person looks,” he said. “A mind. The superposition goes on until someone conscious sees the result.”

He was in distinguished company. In the 1930s John von Neumann, one of the great mathematicians of the century, showed that the theory lets you put the dividing line anywhere you like, all the way up to the observer’s mind, and in 1961 Eugene Wigner argued that the mind was where it belonged.

“So before there were people,” said Cal, “nothing ever actually happened? The Sun shone every possible way at once?”

“Until somebody looked,” said John, less confidently.

“The first fish.”

“Possibly a very clever fish.”

Even Wigner gave it up in the end, and few physicists today take it seriously, though nobody has proved it wrong. It moves the question rather than answering it: what is a mind, that atoms obey rule one everywhere but there?

The measurement problem

The question of how quantum mechanics’ two rules fit together. By the first, the arrows of every possibility change smoothly, all together, so that anything which interacts with a superposition, a detector or a person included, ends up part of it. By the second, a measurement finds one result, with a chance set by its arrow. The theory never says what counts as a measurement, or when the second rule takes over from the first. Decoherence explains why the different results never interfere, but not why only one of them is found.

Three things that can’t all be true

The philosopher Tim Maudlin, in 1995, put the problem as sharply as anyone. Here are three statements, each of which looks, at first, impossible to doubt:

  1. The arrows are the whole story. A thing is completely described by its arrows, and there is nothing more to it.
  2. The arrows always change by rule one, smoothly, with no exceptions, for everything.
  3. When you look, you find one result.

They can’t all be true. If the first two hold, then the detector, the photograph and John all end up, by rule one, in a superposition of results, and that superposition is the whole story, so there is no single result anywhere: the third fails. Take any two, and the third must go.

So something has to give, and every answer anyone has ever proposed gives up one of the three. That is the cleanest way to see them. Physicists call them interpretations.

Interpretation

In quantum mechanics, an account of what is really going on behind the rules: what the arrows are, what happens when nobody is looking, and what a measurement is. All the main interpretations predict the same results for every experiment done so far; they differ in the story they tell about the world between and behind those results. Some of them, collapse theories, change the rules slightly for big things, and so could one day be told apart by experiment.

“Four stories,” said Albert, “and he will tell you that only one of them is necessary.” He nodded at Niels. “Ask The Van. It is quicker than asking him.”

The simulator asks you to choose

The Van’s dashboard was showing a kettle when they got there, and Cal hit it in the usual place. John asked the simulator to run the two-slit experiment, with electrons one at a time, and to show them what actually happened between the slits and the screen.

The simulator thought about it.

“Of course it does,” said Cal.

There were four to choose from. They tried them all.

Niels’s answer: don’t ask

The first was the one Niels had been defending all evening, and it is the oldest. Near enough, it’s the view usually called the Copenhagen interpretation, after the institute in Copenhagen where Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg and their colleagues worked much of quantum mechanics out in the 1920s.

At its strongest it says this. Physics is our way of saying what we will find when we look, and it makes no claim about the world when nobody is looking. We can only describe an experiment in everyday terms, a source, a pair of slits, a screen, a dot, because those are the terms in which any result is recorded and shared. The arrows are a tool for working out the chances of what the screen will show. Asking what the electron was “really” doing between the slits is asking for something no experiment could ever give us. On this view the question has no answer, and the theory has no gap: the sixth journey found that the path and the stripes are complementary, so that you can have either, but the experiment that shows one rules out the other.

On the screen, when John chose it, the space between the slits and the screen was simply blank, and a dot appeared.

INTERPRETATION: DON’T ASK. NO FURTHER INFORMATION AVAILABLE

Niels, who had followed them out to the car park with his tea, read it over Cal’s shoulder.

“It is, perhaps, a little short,” he said. “But not wrong.” He was quiet for a moment. “It is wrong to think that the task of physics is to find out how nature is. Physics is about what we can say about nature.”

It is hard to argue with a view that never claims more than experiments show, and it has served physics superbly. Most working physicists use something like it every day, whatever they believe on Sundays: the physicist David Mermin once summed up the everyday version as “shut up and calculate”. Its weakness is the one on John’s napkin. It needs a line between the quantum part, the electron with its arrows, and the everyday part, the screen and the dot. But the screen is made of atoms too, and the view never says where the line is, or why atoms on one side of it obey different rules from atoms on the other. Of the three statements, it gives up the first: the arrows aren’t the whole story, because they aren’t a story about the world at all.

Many worlds: everything happens

The second answer gives up the third statement instead, and it is the boldest.

In 1957 Hugh Everett, a graduate student at Princeton, asked what would happen if you simply took rule one seriously, all the way, and threw rule two away. No collapse, no special moment of looking, nothing but the arrows turning smoothly for ever, for everything.

Then this is what happens at the two slits. The electron’s arrows reach the screen, and the screen, the air, the detector and John all join the superposition, as rule one says they must. Decoherence, the last journey’s discovery, splits the result into branches: one in which the dot landed here and John saw it here, another in which it landed there and John saw it there, and so on for every place a dot could land. The branches can never interfere again, because the air and the light keep their records. Each contains a John who sees one dot, and who has no way to see or feel the others. Every result happens. Each John, quite correctly, finds one.

INTERPRETATION: MANY WORLDS. BRANCHES CREATED: NUMBER TOO LARGE FOR DISPLAY FONT

The name came later, from Bryce DeWitt, who championed the idea in the 1970s: the many-worlds interpretation. At its strongest it is the simplest of all the answers. It adds nothing to the equations and takes nothing away. It needs no line between the quantum world and the everyday one, and no observer outside the universe to do the looking, which is why many cosmologists, whose subject is the whole universe, like it. Decoherence, which was worked out years after Everett, turned out to be exactly what it needed.

Its difficulty is with chance. If every result happens, what does it mean to say that one result is more likely than another, as rule two does, and as every experiment confirms? If the dot lands everywhere, in one branch or another, why do bright stripes collect more dots in our experience? Its defenders have worked hard on this, and some, David Deutsch and David Wallace among them, argue that a rational person in a branching world must bet by the lengths of the arrows, which would bring rule two back as a rule for betting. Others are not persuaded. And it asks a great deal of anyone’s sense of proportion: a copy of the café, and the plateau, and John and Cal, for every outcome of every quantum event since the beginning.

John had been wondering about other universes ever since the end of One Way In, where The Van’s simulator, at the end of space and time, had asked who knows what lies beyond. This is one of the places physics finds them, although they aren’t what lies beyond a black hole’s end. They aren’t on the far side of anything. They are copies of this universe, side by side with it, forever out of reach.

Cal had been thinking about something else.

“So there’s a world,” he said, “where I didn’t eat that doughnut this afternoon?”

“There is no such world,” said Albert.

The pilot wave: real paths, steered

The third answer gives up the first statement, as Niels’s does, but in the opposite direction: the arrows aren’t the whole story because there is more to the world than the arrows, not less.

Louis de Broglie, whose matter waves the fourth journey met, suggested it in 1927, at the fifth Solvay conference in Brussels, where the founders of quantum theory met to argue over what it meant. The electron, he said, is a particle, a real one, always in exactly one place, and it rides on a wave, made of its arrows, which goes through both slits, interferes with itself, and steers the particle. The particle goes through one slit. The wave goes through both. Where the wave cancels, the particle is steered away, so no dots fall there, and the stripes appear, though every particle has followed a perfectly definite path. De Broglie was criticised at the conference and gave the idea up. In 1952 David Bohm, in the United States, found it again for himself and worked it out properly.

On the screen, the simulator drew the paths, the particles curving through the slits and bunching into the bright stripes, and none of them ever crossing the line down the middle.

INTERPRETATION: PILOT WAVE. PARTICLE POSITION: KNOWN. TO NOBODY

At its strongest, the pilot wave is what Albert always wanted. Particles have real positions, all the time. Nothing collapses, and there is no special moment of looking: a measurement is just particles going where the wave steers them, and the theory gives exactly rule two’s chances, so long as you don’t know where the particles started, which you never can. The positions are, in a sense, the instructions Albert spent the thirteenth journey betting on.

But the instructions come with a price. When there are two particles, the steering wave is a single wave of both together, and what happens to one can change, at once, how the other is steered, however far apart they are. That is exactly the kind of link the thirteenth journey found that Bell’s theorem allows: not instructions carried along by each particle, but a connection between them. John Bell admired the pilot wave greatly. Reading Bohm’s papers, he wrote, “In 1952 I saw the impossible done.” Its instant links need a single “now” stretching across the universe, which When Exactly Is Now? found that no experiment can ever pick out.

“I like the particles,” said Albert, when they reported back. “I do not like the links.”

“You never have,” said Niels.

The paths have even been measured, in a way. In 2011 Sacha Kocsis, Aephraim Steinberg and their colleagues in Toronto sent single photons through two slits and gave each one a measurement so gentle that it barely disturbed it, a tiny nudge recording a little of which way it was heading, at different distances from the slits. Each nudge told them almost nothing on its own, but averaged over many thousands of photons, the nudges traced out the average paths of the photons from the slits to the screen. They curved and bunched just as the pilot wave’s paths do. But ordinary quantum mechanics predicts exactly the same averages, so the experiment shows what the averages are, not that each photon really follows one.

Collapse theories: the rules change

The last answer gives up the second statement. Rule one isn’t exact, it says. It is very nearly right, but every so often nature breaks it.

In 1986 Giancarlo Ghirardi, Alberto Rimini and Tullio Weber, in Italy, wrote down how. Every particle, they proposed, suffers at random, very rarely, a sudden squeeze: its wave is narrowed, in an instant, to a region about a ten-thousandth of a millimetre across. For a single particle it is so rare, about once in three hundred million years, that no experiment on electrons or atoms would ever notice. But a body is made of a great many particles, held together. A pointer weighing a gram or two is made of about a million billion billion of them, so one of its particles is squeezed about a hundred million times a second, and when one is squeezed, the whole pointer, being one solid thing, goes with it. A superposition of the pointer in two places lasts about a hundred-millionth of a second. So small things keep their superpositions for ages, and big things never have them for long. Rule two, the collapse, is just rule one’s rare exception made frequent by sheer numbers.

INTERPRETATION: COLLAPSE. WAVE SHRINKING AT RANDOM. PLEASE STAND BY

Roger Penrose, from 1996, and Lajos Diósi in Budapest before him, proposed a reason for the collapse. Mass bends spacetime, as John and Cal found throughout the relativity series. A lump of matter in two places at once would be two different bendings of spacetime at once, and Penrose suspects that nature can’t keep that up for long. The bigger the lump, and the further apart its two places, the sooner it must choose. His rule gives a time. For the sodium clusters of 2026, of more than seven thousand atoms each, it comes to about two thousand years. For a droplet of water a thousandth of a millimetre across, a second or two.

Penrose is the same Roger Penrose who proved, in 1965, that a black hole must hide a place where spacetime ends, which earned him a share of the Nobel Prize in 2020.

At their strongest, collapse theories are the only answers that actually say when rule two happens, and why big things are never seen in two states: nature itself won’t allow it, whatever the air does. And that makes them different from the other three in one important way. They change the rules, so they predict something different, and they can be tested.

Their weakness is that the rules they add are made up, for now, and their settings, how often the squeeze comes and how narrow it is, can be adjusted to stay out of trouble. And the squeeze has side effects. Every squeeze gives a particle a tiny kick, so collapse theories predict that everything should be very slightly warmer than it ought to be, and charged particles should glow very faintly. In 2020 Sandro Donadi and his colleagues looked for that glow, deep under the mountains of the Gran Sasso in Italy, where rock shields out almost all other radiation, with a detector made of germanium. They saw almost nothing beyond the background, and that ruled out the simplest version of the Diósi and Penrose idea, the one with no settings to adjust at all. Other versions survive, and the search goes on.

One experiment, four stories

Back in the café, John set the four readouts side by side on the napkin, and saw what they had in common.

In every story, the screen had shown exactly the same thing: dots, one at a time, building exactly the same stripes, with exactly the same chances of landing at every place. Niels’s view said there was nothing to say about what happened in between. Many worlds said every dot landed everywhere, in one branch or another. The pilot wave said each electron took one definite, curving path. Collapse said the wave spread through both slits and then shrank, at random, to a point. Four stories about the same experiment, each telling a quite different tale of the world, and the screen couldn’t tell them apart.

Follow it through. An experiment ends with a record: a dot, a click, a number. Every one of the four stories gives the same records, with the same chances, for every experiment anyone has yet done. So no experiment done so far can choose between them. The only way out would be an experiment whose records the stories disagree about, and for three of them there may be no such experiment at all: they were built to give exactly rule one’s and rule two’s results. Only collapse theories change the results, and only for things big enough to feel the squeeze.

The figure below is the simulator’s run, told four ways. At the top, electrons pass the two slits one at a time, and land as dots on the screen on the right, with the stripes’ brightness beside it. Choose a story, and the space between the slits and the screen fills with that story’s account of what happened: blank for Niels’s view; the branches for many worlds, the dot that landed in our branch bright and the others faint; the steered paths for the pilot wave, the latest electron’s bright and the others faint, computed from the wave itself; and for collapse, the wave spreading and then shrinking to the dot. Switch between them as the dots arrive. The dots don’t change, and neither do the numbers in the table. Only the story does.

Where the question becomes an experiment

If collapse theories are right, there is a size beyond which the stripes must fail. Find it, and you would have caught the world deciding. Never find it, and the collapse theories are cornered, setting by setting. So for a hundred years physicists have been putting larger and larger things through slits.

The record began with electrons, in 1927. Helium atoms followed in 1930. In 1999 the Vienna group of Markus Arndt and Anton Zeilinger sent through the footballs of sixty carbon atoms the fourth journey met; in 2011 they managed molecules of 430 atoms, and in 2019 molecules of nearly two thousand. In January 2026 the same group, with Sebastian Pedalino and their colleagues, sent clusters of more than seven thousand sodium atoms through gratings made of light, each cluster weighing more than 170,000 hydrogen atoms, and the stripes were exactly where quantum mechanics said they would be.

That sounds large, and it is, for a superposition. But by Penrose’s rule, those clusters could stay in two places for about two thousand years, and the experiment needed only a few thousandths of a second. To catch his collapse in the act, an experiment would need something whose two places last only a second or so by his rule: a droplet about a thousandth of a millimetre across, a few million times heavier than today’s record. Other experiments have made heavier things into superpositions of a sort: in 2023 a team in Zurich put a sapphire crystal of sixteen millionths of a gram into a superposition of two vibrations at once, but its two places differed by about a billionth of a billionth of a metre, far less than the width of a proton, and collapse theories care how far apart the places are. Groups around the world are now trapping glass beads about a ten-thousandth of a millimetre across in beams of laser light, in a vacuum, with their jiggling cooled almost to its lowest possible level, and trying to coax them into two places at once. Nobody knows yet whether they will see stripes.

The other answers are harder to test, because they were built to give rule two’s results exactly. If the world ever does something they can’t, it will be an experiment that finds out, as it was an experiment that settled Albert’s bet in the thirteenth journey. Some physicists hope that a theory joining gravity to the quantum, the theory nobody yet has, will settle the question in passing. Others think it will turn out to be a question about words, not about the world. Nobody knows.

What is certain is that it is a real question, precisely stated, and open. The theory works perfectly. What it means is still argued over by the people who know it best, a century after it was written down.

Back to Teide

It was nearly midnight when Niels put on his coat. The plateau outside was black, and the sky above it was so full of stars that the mountain was only a shape where they stopped.

He shook John’s hand, and Cal’s, and then stood for a moment with Albert at the door.

“Next time,” said Niels, “perhaps we will agree.”

“I hope not,” said Albert. “What would we talk about?”

Niels laughed, very quietly, and went out into the dark. They watched his torch go down the terrace steps and along the edge of the car park, past The Van, until the night took it.

Cal turned back to the counter.

“So after all this,” he said, “we still don’t know what quantum mechanics is telling us about reality?”

“We know an extraordinary amount about what nature does,” said John. “Every experiment, every time, to more decimal places than anything else we know.”

“But not what it means.”

Albert had found three doughnuts left over from the day, a little stale, and was arranging them on a plate with great care, as though their positions mattered.

“Perhaps,” he said, “that is the next question.”

He put the plate on the counter. Cal looked at it, and then past it, at the grain of sugar under its glass. It had sat there for fourteen journeys while they found out that it was made of atoms, that its atoms were nearly empty, that their electrons were waves held in by the nucleus, kept apart by the exclusion principle and joined into molecules by sharing. And still he didn’t know what an electron actually was.

“There’s one thing I still don’t know,” he said.

“Only one?” said Albert.

“What it’s made of. At the very bottom.”

Albert looked at the grain, and then at Cal, and something in his face suggested that he had been waiting for this for some time.

“Come back in the morning,” he said, “and you can have it back.”
