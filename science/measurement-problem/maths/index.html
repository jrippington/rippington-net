The two rules

Rule one is Schrödinger’s equation. A system’s state, its arrows for every possibility together, is a vector ∣ψ⟩|\psi\rangle∣ψ⟩, and between measurements it changes as

iℏddt∣ψ⟩=H^∣ψ⟩,i\hbar \frac{d}{dt} |\psi\rangle = \hat H |\psi\rangle,iℏdtd​∣ψ⟩=H^∣ψ⟩,

where H^\hat HH^, the Hamiltonian, says how the system’s energy depends on its state. The equation is linear: if ∣ψA⟩|\psi_A\rangle∣ψA​⟩ and ∣ψB⟩|\psi_B\rangle∣ψB​⟩ are two ways the system can change, so is any combination α∣ψA⟩+β∣ψB⟩\alpha|\psi_A\rangle + \beta|\psi_B\rangleα∣ψA​⟩+β∣ψB​⟩. Nothing in it ever chooses.

Rule two is Born’s rule. Write the state in terms of the results a measurement can give, ∣ψ⟩=∑kck∣k⟩|\psi\rangle = \sum_k c_k |k\rangle∣ψ⟩=∑k​ck​∣k⟩. The measurement finds the result kkk with probability

P(k)=∣ck∣2,P(k) = |c_k|^2,P(k)=∣ck​∣2,

the square of the length of its arrow, and afterwards the system is in the state ∣k⟩|k\rangle∣k⟩: the other arrows are gone.

Why they clash. Let a detector start in its ready state ∣D0⟩|D_0\rangle∣D0​⟩, and suppose it works: an electron through slit A leaves it reading A, and one through slit B, reading B,

∣A⟩∣D0⟩→∣A⟩∣DA⟩,∣B⟩∣D0⟩→∣B⟩∣DB⟩.|A\rangle |D_0\rangle \to |A\rangle |D_A\rangle, \qquad |B\rangle |D_0\rangle \to |B\rangle |D_B\rangle.∣A⟩∣D0​⟩→∣A⟩∣DA​⟩,∣B⟩∣D0​⟩→∣B⟩∣DB​⟩.

Now send in an electron in the superposition α∣A⟩+β∣B⟩\alpha|A\rangle + \beta|B\rangleα∣A⟩+β∣B⟩. Rule one is linear, so it must give

(α∣A⟩+β∣B⟩)∣D0⟩→α∣A⟩∣DA⟩+β∣B⟩∣DB⟩:\left(\alpha|A\rangle + \beta|B\rangle\right)|D_0\rangle \to \alpha |A\rangle |D_A\rangle + \beta |B\rangle |D_B\rangle :(α∣A⟩+β∣B⟩)∣D0​⟩→α∣A⟩∣DA​⟩+β∣B⟩∣DB​⟩:

the detector in a superposition of both readings. Add the air, the light, the photograph and John, each with its states ∣EA⟩|E_A\rangle∣EA​⟩ and ∣EB⟩|E_B\rangle∣EB​⟩, and the sum only grows,

α∣A⟩∣DA⟩∣EA⟩+β∣B⟩∣DB⟩∣EB⟩.\alpha |A\rangle |D_A\rangle |E_A\rangle + \beta |B\rangle |D_B\rangle |E_B\rangle .α∣A⟩∣DA​⟩∣EA​⟩+β∣B⟩∣DB​⟩∣EB​⟩.

Decoherence makes ⟨EA∣EB⟩≈0\langle E_A | E_B \rangle \approx 0⟨EA​∣EB​⟩≈0, so the two terms can never interfere again, but both are still there. Rule two says that one reading is found, A with probability ∣α∣2|\alpha|^2∣α∣2 or B with probability ∣β∣2|\beta|^2∣β∣2.

Maudlin’s three statements (1995), in these terms: (1) ∣ψ⟩|\psi\rangle∣ψ⟩ is a complete description of the system; (2) ∣ψ⟩|\psi\rangle∣ψ⟩ always changes by rule one; (3) measurements have single results. Statements (1) and (2) give the sum above as the whole truth, with no single reading in it, so (3) fails. Niels’s view and the pilot wave give up (1), in opposite directions; collapse theories give up (2); many worlds gives up (3), in the sense that each of the terms has its own reading.

Branches and their weights

Branches. In many worlds the sum is the whole truth. Each term, ck∣k⟩∣Dk⟩∣Ek⟩c_k |k\rangle |D_k\rangle |E_k\rangleck​∣k⟩∣Dk​⟩∣Ek​⟩, is a branch, and decoherence keeps them apart: what any observer within a branch can see is described by the reduced state, the full one with everything else averaged away,

ρ=∑k∣ck∣2 ∣k⟩⟨k∣+∑j≠kcjck∗ ⟨Ek∣Ej⟩ ∣j⟩⟨k∣  ≈  ∑k∣ck∣2 ∣k⟩⟨k∣,\rho = \sum_k |c_k|^2\, |k\rangle\langle k| + \sum_{j \ne k} c_j c_k^*\, \langle E_k | E_j \rangle\, |j\rangle\langle k| \;\approx\; \sum_k |c_k|^2\, |k\rangle\langle k| ,ρ=k∑​∣ck​∣2∣k⟩⟨k∣+j=k∑​cj​ck∗​⟨Ek​∣Ej​⟩∣j⟩⟨k∣≈k∑​∣ck​∣2∣k⟩⟨k∣,

which looks exactly like “result kkk with probability ∣ck∣2|c_k|^2∣ck​∣2, and we don’t know which”. Every term is still in the full state.

Weights. Call wk=∣ck∣2w_k = |c_k|^2wk​=∣ck​∣2 the branch’s weight. The difficulty is to say why the weights should be probabilities. Counting branches won’t do: a measurement with wA=0.9w_A = 0.9wA​=0.9 and wB=0.1w_B = 0.1wB​=0.1 makes two branches, but every experiment finds A nine times in ten. David Deutsch (1999) and David Wallace (2012) argue that anyone who must place bets in a branching world, and who reasons consistently about it, must bet in proportion to the weights, which recovers Born’s rule as a rule for rational betting; Wojciech Zurek has given another argument, from the symmetries of entangled states. Whether these arguments succeed is still disputed.

The pilot wave’s guidance

The rule. In de Broglie’s and Bohm’s theory, a particle has a definite position Q(t)Q(t)Q(t) at all times, and the wave ψ\psiψ, obeying rule one exactly, steers it: the particle moves with velocity

dQdt=ℏmIm⁡ ⁣(∇ψψ)∣x=Q.\frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{\hbar}{m} \operatorname{Im}\!\left(\frac{\nabla \psi}{\psi}\right)\Big|_{x = Q}.dtdQ​=mℏ​Im(ψ∇ψ​)​x=Q​.

Why it gives Born’s rule. If an ensemble of particles starts distributed as ∣ψ(x,0)∣2|\psi(x, 0)|^2∣ψ(x,0)∣2, the guidance keeps it distributed as ∣ψ(x,t)∣2|\psi(x, t)|^2∣ψ(x,t)∣2 for ever (the property is called equivariance): the velocity is the probability current divided by the density, so the density flows exactly as ∣ψ∣2|\psi|^2∣ψ∣2 does. Nobody can know a particle’s starting position better than ∣ψ∣2|\psi|^2∣ψ∣2 allows, so the theory gives exactly rule two’s chances.

Two particles. For two particles the wave is a single function ψ(x1,x2,t)\psi(x_1, x_2, t)ψ(x1​,x2​,t), and particle 1’s velocity depends on where particle 2 is, at the same moment, however far away: this is the theory’s nonlocality, which Bell’s theorem allows, and which needs a definite “same moment” across space.

Average paths. In 2011 Kocsis and colleagues measured the average momentum of single photons at different distances from two slits, each with a weak measurement (a slight rotation of its polarisation), keeping only the photons later found at a given place. Howard Wiseman had shown in 2007 that this “weak-valued” average velocity is exactly the pilot wave’s guidance velocity. Ordinary quantum mechanics predicts the same averages: the measurement shows that the pilot wave’s velocity field is a real, measurable average, not that any single photon follows its path.

GRW (Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber, 1986). Testing them. A collapse localises a particle, and so gives it a small random kick: collapse theories predict a faint, steady heating of everything, and a faint glow from charged particles shaken by it. Donadi and colleagues (Nature Physics, 2021) looked for that glow with a germanium detector under the Gran Sasso, and found no more than the expected background, which ruled out the Diósi and Penrose model in its natural form, with each nucleus’s mass smeared over its own size; versions with the mass smeared more widely survive. Interference with ever larger objects tests the collapse directly: in GRW’s and related theories the stripes of a body of NNN particles fade as e−Nλte^{-N \lambda t}e−Nλt (more steeply for bodies smaller than rCr_CrC​), and each larger record pushes the allowed λ\lambdaλ down.
