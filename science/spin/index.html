Three pairs of sunglasses on the café table do something no sieve could do, The Van's compass will only point two ways, and a postcard of 1922 congratulates the right man for the wrong reason. By evening John and Cal have met spin, which has only two answers, and which is not, whatever its name says, spinning.

Three pairs on the table

The morning was so bright that the plateau hurt to look at. The sun stood clear of the eastern ridge, the sky above the mountain was a hard, clean blue, and every pale stone on the old lava flashed. Cal came in wearing his sunglasses and carrying John’s, which John had left in The Van.

“You said bring another pair,” he said to Albert, and put both pairs on the table.

“I said another pair,” said Albert. He went behind the counter, rummaged in a box where the café kept the things its visitors left behind, and came back with a third: big, square and very dark, left by somebody off a coach a long time ago. He laid it beside the other two. “Now there are enough.”

The evening before, Cal had put his sunglasses on against the low sun’s glare off the table tops, and the glare had gone. Then he had tilted his head to one side, and the glare had come back. He did it again now, with the morning glare off the table by the window: head straight, no glare; head tilted, glare.

“Ordinary dark glasses wouldn’t do that,” said John. “They’d make everything darker, whichever way you held your head.”

“These aren’t ordinary,” said Albert. “They are polarising. Hold them up, one pair in front of the other, and look through both at the window.”

Cal held John’s pair up in front of his own and looked through both lenses at once. The window was dimmer, as you’d expect through two pairs of dark glasses. Then he turned John’s pair slowly round, like the hand of a clock, and the window grew darker, and darker, until at a quarter of a turn it was black. Two lenses, each perfectly see-through on its own, together let nothing through at all. He kept turning, and the window came back.

“Now,” said Albert, “hold them crossed, so it’s black, and slip the third pair in between them.”

Cal held his own pair and John’s crossed in front of his eyes, so that the window was black, and John slid the square lenses of the coach party’s pair into the gap between them, tilted halfway between the two.

The window came back. Not as bright as through one pair, but there, unmistakably: the mountain, the blue sky, The Van in the car park. John took the middle pair out, and the window went black. He put it back, and the window returned.

Cal lowered the glasses and looked at them as if they had done something rude.

“That can’t happen,” he said. “Two of them let nothing through. You put a third in the way, and more gets through. How can adding a filter let more light through?”

Light that shakes

To see what the sunglasses are doing, go back to what light is. In Chasing a Beam of Light, John and Cal found that light is a wave of electric and magnetic forces, each change in one making the other, travelling through space together. The electric force in a light wave doesn’t push along the direction the light is going. It shakes across it, to and fro, like a rope flicked up and down: if a beam is coming straight towards you, its electric force might be shaking up and down, or side to side, or at any slant in between.

Light from the Sun or a lamp is a jumble of all those directions at once, changing from moment to moment. But light can be sorted, and the sorted kind has a name.

Polarisation

The direction in which a light wave’s electric force shakes, always across the direction the light is travelling: up and down, side to side, or any slant between. Sunlight and lamplight are a jumble of every direction; light that shakes in one direction only is called polarised. A polarising filter lets through light shaking along its own direction and stops light shaking across it. Light shaking at a slant to it partly gets through, and comes out shaking along the filter’s direction.

A polarising lens is a sheet of plastic in which long, thin molecules have been stretched out side by side, like a field of logs all lying the same way. Electrons can run easily along each molecule, so light whose electric force shakes along the molecules sets the electrons running and is soaked up. Light shaking across them can’t do that, and passes through. Each lens has its own direction, and only light shaking that way gets through.

Why make sunglasses like that? Because of glare. Light bouncing off a flat surface, a road, a lake or a varnished table top, comes off mostly shaking side to side, along the surface. Polarising sunglasses are made to pass only light shaking up and down, so they stop most of the glare and let the rest of the view through. Tilt your head through a quarter of a turn, and the lenses now pass side-to-side light, and the glare comes back. That was Cal’s puzzle of the evening before.

So the first two pairs held crossed are no mystery. Say the first passes only light shaking up and down. Then everything reaching the second is shaking up and down, and the second, turned a quarter of a turn, passes only light shaking side to side. There’s none, so nothing gets through.

What about light shaking at a slant? Think of a slanting shake as partly up and down and partly side to side, as a path running north-east is partly north and partly east. A filter takes the part along its own direction and stops the rest: light shaking at a slant of 45 degrees to a filter loses half of its energy getting through, and what comes out is shaking exactly along the filter. The steeper the slant, the less gets through; at a right angle, nothing. (Sunlight, a jumble of every direction, loses half at the first filter.)

That’s how the third pair works, if light is a wave. Out of the first filter comes light shaking up and down. The middle one, at 45 degrees, passes half of it, and what comes out is now shaking at 45 degrees. The last one, side to side, is at 45 degrees to that, and passes half again. So a quarter of what passed the first gets through all three, where none got through two. The middle filter hasn’t added light. It has turned the light’s shaking, so that the last filter has something to take.

A sieve can only take away

“Fine for a wave,” said Cal. “But light isn’t only a wave. It comes in lumps.”

He was right, and this is where the sunglasses stop being an ordinary piece of optics. In the third journey, John and Cal found that light arrives in photons, separate lumps that can’t be split: a photon is absorbed whole or not at all. Turn the light down until photons come through the lenses one at a time, as the probe’s camera counted them, and ask what happens to a single photon at the middle filter. It can’t lose half its energy and carry on at half strength, because there’s no such thing as half a photon of the same colour. It must either go through, whole, or be stopped, whole.

So the filter decides, photon by photon, and by chance. A photon shaking up and down, arriving at a filter set at 45 degrees, has an even chance of getting through: half of them do, and half are stopped, and there is no telling in advance which. Every one that does get through comes out shaking at 45 degrees. A photon at a gentler slant has a better chance, and at a steeper slant a worse one, and the chances match exactly the shares a wave would lose. Counted over many photons, the beam behaves like a wave; one at a time, it’s a run of yes or no.

Albert went behind the counter and came back with the sieve he used for dusting lemon sugar onto the doughnuts.

“Suppose,” he said, “that the lenses were sieves. A sieve sorts the sugar by something every grain already has: its size. The fine grains go through and the coarse ones stay in the sieve. Each grain carries its size with it, and the sieve only reads it.”

“And a second sieve, finer than the first, takes out more,” said John.

“And a third,” said Albert, “put between them?”

“Can only take out more still,” said Cal. “Every grain that gets through all three has to have got through the first and the last. If the first and last together let nothing through, three can’t let anything through either.”

That’s the whole argument, and it doesn’t depend on anything about sugar. Suppose each photon set out carrying a label of some kind, its direction of shaking or anything else you like, fixed when it left the lamp, and suppose a filter simply read the label and let the photon by or stopped it. Then a photon that gets through all three filters must have a label that passes the first and passes the last. With the first and last crossed, no label passes both: crossed, they let nothing through. So with any sieve between them, nothing could get through three.

Something does get through three. So a filter can’t be a sieve. It doesn’t read a direction the photon already had and leave it alone. It sets one. A photon that passes the middle filter comes out shaking at the middle filter’s direction, whatever it was doing before, and its old direction is simply gone: it’s no more up-and-down than any other photon at 45 degrees. That’s why the last filter can pass it.

“Perhaps,” said Albert, with the air of a man who has tried this argument before, “each photon carries instructions. Not one label, but a whole plan: what to do at every filter it might meet.”

“Then the filter’s still a sieve,” said Cal. “It reads the plan. And whatever the plan says, a photon that gets through all three got through the first and the last.”

Albert looked at the sieve for a while, and put it down.

“Unless the filter changes the plan,” he said. “That is allowed.”

It is. Plans or no plans, the only way three filters can pass more than two is if passing a filter changes the photon. The filter is not asking a question that already had an answer. It’s making the answer, and wiping out the last one.

Try it

You need two pairs of polarising sunglasses and a phone. A phone’s screen, of either common kind, gives out polarised light, so it can be the first filter. Turn the screen on, show something bright and white, and look at it through one lens of the first pair, turning the glasses slowly round. At one angle the screen goes dark, or nearly so: the lens is crossed with the screen. Hold it there. Now slide one lens of the second pair between the screen and the first, and turn it. At about halfway between, the screen comes back. (If a pair doesn’t darken the screen at any angle, its lenses aren’t polarising. Not all sunglasses are.)

The figure below is the same experiment on a bench, one photon at a time. A lamp sends photons through three filters: the first passes light shaking up and down, the last only light shaking side to side, and the middle one you can turn. Each filter’s direction is drawn in the circle above it, as you’d see it looking along the beam, and each photon carries a short dash showing which way it’s shaking. The band behind them is how bright the beam is at each stage. Play it, and watch the photons before the first filter shaking every which way, and after it all up and down. At the middle filter, each one either goes through or vanishes in a small flash, and the ones that go through come out turned. Count them: about one in eight of the lamp’s photons gets to the screen. Take the middle filter out, and none does. Turn it, and see that 45 degrees lets through the most. Then switch to sieves: each photon keeps its own direction from the lamp, and a filter only lets it by if that direction is close to its own. Now the middle filter can do nothing but stop photons, and the screen stays dark.

A compass with two answers

“That’s light,” said John. “Light’s strange anyway. It’s made of photons with no mass, going at the speed of light. Ordinary things don’t do this.”

Cal had gone out to The Van to switch the simulator out of quantum mode, as he’d promised the night before. He came back in without having done it, looking puzzled.

“The compass,” he said.

The Van had a compass on its dashboard, an old one in a glass dome, its needle floating in oil. In the ordinary way it pointed at magnetic north, give or take whatever The Van’s own wiring was doing to it. That morning, with the simulator still in quantum mode, the simulator had taken over the compass too. Cal had driven the simulator’s Van slowly round in a circle, out on the lava where it still was, and watched the compass. It hadn’t swung round smoothly, as a compass does. It had pointed north, exactly north, the whole way round, and then, when he asked it again, exactly south.

“So I set it to tell me east or west instead,” said Cal, “and it said west. Exactly west.” He showed them the screen.

“Then I asked it north or south again. And it said south. Last time it said north.”

Albert came out to look. He tapped the compass’s dome, as people do, and then the simulator’s screen.

“Your compass,” he said, “is behaving like a silver atom.”

He meant it quite exactly. A compass needle is a magnet, and in the simulator’s quantum mode, with Planck’s constant turned up absurdly high, the needle had become a single quantum magnet, which does something no ordinary magnet does. Ask it which way it points along any line you choose, north and south, east and west, up and down, and it gives only two answers: along the line, or against it. Never anything in between. And asking it one question wipes out its answer to the last.

The real compass on The Van’s dashboard points north and turns smoothly when The Van turns, and it always will. But it’s made of the same thing, many times over. A magnet’s magnetism, in iron and in a compass needle, comes almost entirely from its electrons, each of which is a tiny magnet of exactly this two-answer kind. A compass needle has billions of billions of them, most of them pointing the same way, and together they make an ordinary arrow that can point anywhere. Only one at a time do they show what they really are. The first people to see it were Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach, in Frankfurt, in February 1922.

Silver atoms through a magnet

Stern had the idea, by his own account, lying in bed early one cold morning in 1921, too cold to get up. Every atom with an odd number of electrons ought to be a tiny magnet, and silver is one of them: of its 47 electrons, 46 are in pairs whose magnetism cancels out, for a reason the next journey explains, and one is left over, making the whole atom a little magnet. Stern wanted to find out which way such tiny magnets point.

You can’t ask an atom directly. But you can push it with a magnet, and how a magnet pushes another magnet depends on which way it points. That needs a little thought, because an ordinary magnetic field, the same strength everywhere, doesn’t push a small magnet anywhere at all: it only turns it, the way the Earth’s field turns a compass needle without dragging it north. The north end is pulled one way and the south end the other, equally, and the pulls cancel. But make the field stronger at the top than at the bottom, and the end nearer the top is pulled harder than the other end. Now there’s a push left over, up or down, depending on which end is nearer the top. A small magnet pointing straight up is pushed up; pointing straight down, it’s pushed down; lying on its side, it’s not pushed at all; and anything in between is pushed by an amount in between.

So Stern and Gerlach built a magnet with one pole shaped into a sharp knife edge, above, and the other with a groove cut in it, below, so that between them the field was far stronger near the knife edge. They heated silver in a small electric oven until it gave off a thin vapour of single atoms, let the atoms stream out through two narrow slits into a flat beam, sent the beam between the poles of the magnet, all in a vacuum, and let it land on a small glass plate. Each run took hours, and the silver deposited on the plate was too thin to see.

What should the plate show? Atoms leaving a hot oven ought to have their tiny magnets pointing every which way, at random. Some point up and are pushed up, some point down and are pushed down, and most point somewhere in between and are pushed by somewhere-in-between amounts. So the thin line of the beam should be smeared out, up and down, into a broad band. That’s what any physicist of 1921 would have expected of ordinary magnets, however small.

That isn’t what they found. The beam had split in two. There were two lines on the plate, one pushed up and one pushed down, and nothing at all between them: every silver atom had been pushed all the way up or all the way down. The split was small, about a fifth of a millimetre at its widest, which is why it had taken months of trying to see it. But it was there.

Stern used to tell the story of the moment it was seen. Gerlach took the plate out of the apparatus, looked at it, saw nothing, and handed it to Stern, who peered at it closely with Gerlach looking over his shoulder. As they watched, the trace of the beam slowly appeared, dark on the glass. Stern’s salary, he said, was too low for good cigars, so he smoked bad ones, which were full of sulphur, and the sulphur on his breath had turned the invisible silver into black silver sulphide. Eighty years later, two physicists tried it: breath alone did nothing, but a little cigar smoke blackened a thin film of silver within seconds. They thought Stern’s plate had probably been in the smoke as well as the breath. Either way, the most important plate in the history of magnets was developed with a cheap cigar.

The figure below is Stern and Gerlach’s apparatus, from the side, with the oven on the left, the slits, the magnet’s two poles, and the plate; and at the right, the plate face on, as the atoms land on it. Choose what everyone expected, and the atoms come through with their tiny magnets pointing every which way, drawn as little arrows, and land everywhere between the top and the bottom: a smear. Choose what Stern and Gerlach saw, and every atom is pushed all the way up or all the way down, and the plate builds two traces with nothing between, wide apart in the middle, where the field changes fastest, and meeting at the ends. That’s the shape on Gerlach’s photograph: a pair of lips. Switch the magnet off, and there’s a single line.

Notice something about the atoms in the figure. Before the magnet, an atom that is going to land in one of the two traces has no arrow drawn on it at all. That’s deliberate, and it’s the lesson of the sunglasses again. You might think each atom’s tiny magnet already pointed up or down when it left the oven, and the magnet simply sorted them, like a sieve. But turn the magnet round, so that its knife edge is on the left instead of above, and the beam splits in two again, left and right, with nothing between. Turn it to any angle you like: two spots, always, along whatever line the magnet asks about. The atoms can’t all have been pointing exactly up or down, and exactly left or right, and exactly along every other line as well. The magnet doesn’t read a direction the atom already had. It asks a question, up or down along its own line, and the atom gives one of the two answers.

A postcard to Copenhagen

On 8 February 1922, as soon as the plate had shown its two lines, Gerlach sent a picture postcard to Copenhagen. The physicist there had developed the atom of 1913, the one that, as the eighth journey found, got hydrogen’s numbers right with a picture that turned out to be wrong, and that picture made a prediction for silver. Its outer electron, going round in an orbit, would make the atom a magnet, and the orbit, the theory said, could only be tilted in certain ways: for silver, two. Two spots, not a smear. On the front of the postcard was a photograph of the plate.

Albert reached behind the till, where a postcard was propped against the coffee machine, its picture faded to brown. On it, two pale lips of silver.

“Niels sent me a copy, years ago,” he said. “He sends it to everyone.” He turned it over and read the back aloud, translating from the German as he went.

“Attached, the experimental proof of directional quantisation. We congratulate you on the confirmation of your theory.”

“So the 1913 atom was right after all,” said John.

“About the two spots,” said Albert. “Not about why.”

The orbit the theory pictured doesn’t exist. Silver’s outer electron, in the atom’s lowest state, makes no magnetism at all by its motion round the nucleus: its cloud is a round one, with no orbit to tilt. So where did the two spots come from? From something nobody had thought of in 1922. The electron itself is a magnet, whatever it’s doing, and its magnet gives only two answers. That something was found three years later, and only in 1927 did anyone realise that it, and not the orbit, was what Stern and Gerlach had seen. By then everyone had congratulated everyone.

“Niels always says,” said Albert, putting the postcard back against the coffee machine, “that he was congratulated for the wrong reason.”

Spin

In the autumn of 1925, two young Dutch students in Leiden, George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit, were trying to make sense of the patterns of atoms’ colours, which had details nobody could explain. They found the details came out right if the electron had a magnetism of its own, as if it were a tiny ball of charge spinning on its axis, and if that magnetism could point only two ways. A spinning charge is a magnet: charge going round in a circle is an electric current, and a current going round, as Chasing a Beam of Light found, behaves exactly like a magnet. So they called it spin, wrote a short note, and gave it to their professor, Paul Ehrenfest.

Ehrenfest suggested they show it to the great Hendrik Lorentz, who worked through it and pointed out a problem: to have that much magnetism, a spinning electron’s surface would have to be moving far faster than light. The two students went back to Ehrenfest and asked him not to send the note. He had already sent it. “You are both young enough,” he told them, “to be able to afford a stupidity.”

It wasn’t a stupidity. The electron’s magnetism is real, and it does give only two answers, and that is what split Stern and Gerlach’s beam. Lorentz was right too, about the spinning. The name stuck anyway.

Spin

A property of the electron, and of the proton and the neutron: each is a tiny magnet, and carries an amount of turning (angular momentum), as if it were spinning, though nothing is. Measure the direction of its magnet along any line you choose and there are only two possible answers, along the line or against it, usually called “up” and “down”. Measuring it along one line sets the answer for that line, and wipes out whatever answer it would have given along any other.

Spin’s two answers, and the forgetting, are the sunglasses all over again. The cleanest way to see it is to chain magnets together, one after another, as the sunglasses were chained.

Send a beam of silver atoms through a magnet that asks up or down, and block the atoms that come out down. Every atom left is an up atom: send them through a second up-or-down magnet, and they all come out up again. The answer holds, as an answer should. Now put a magnet that asks left or right in between, and keep only the atoms that come out right. Every one of them was up a moment ago. Send them into a final up-or-down magnet. If each atom still carried its answer to the first question, they’d all come out up again.

They don’t. Half come out up and half come out down, exactly as if they’d never been asked. Asking left or right has wiped out the answer to up or down, as the middle filter wiped out the photons’ up-and-down shaking.

Here, too, there’s no way out through labels. Suppose each atom left the oven carrying a hidden answer to every question at once, up or down and left or right, and the magnets only read them. Then the atoms kept after the first magnet all had “up” written on them, and the left-or-right magnet, only reading, couldn’t rub it out, and every one of them would come out of the last magnet up. They don’t, so the magnets aren’t reading. Each one sets an answer and forgets the last.

There’s one difference from the sunglasses. For light, the opposite answer to “up and down” is “side to side”, a quarter of a turn away, so a filter at half of that, 45 degrees, splits the photons evenly. For an electron’s spin, the opposite answer to “up” is “down”, half a turn away: a magnet turned upside down asks the same question the other way round. So a magnet splits up atoms evenly when it’s turned half of that: a quarter of a turn, to ask left or right. Spin’s angles are halved compared with light’s, which is why physicists call it spin one half.

The figure below is three magnets in a chain. The first asks up or down and keeps the atoms that answer up. The middle one you can turn, and it keeps the atoms that answer along its own direction. The last asks up or down again, and counts. Start with the middle magnet set like the first: everything that reaches the last one comes out up. Turn it a quarter of a turn, to left or right, and the last magnet finds half up and half down. Turn it all the way over, upside down, and no up atom will answer along it at all. Then try “If atoms carried labels”, in which every atom carries a hidden arrow it never loses and the magnets only read it: the last magnet finds every atom up, whatever you do to the middle one. Real atoms don’t.

Spin isn’t spinning

So is the electron spinning? It behaves as if it were, in two ways. It’s a magnet, as a spinning ball of charge would be. And it carries an amount of turning, as a spinning top does. You can see the turning in an iron rod hung from a thread: magnetise it, lining up its electrons’ magnets, and you line up their turning too, and the rod twists a little the other way on its thread to make up for it. Einstein and the Dutch physicist Wander de Haas measured the twist in 1915. But Lorentz’s objection still stands, and it’s decisive.

Picture the electron as a tiny ball with its charge on the surface, spinning fast enough to be as strong a magnet as it is. How fast must its surface go? That depends on how big the ball is: a bigger ball can spin more slowly. So give it every advantage and make it as big as the electron could reasonably be, which a century ago meant about a thirty-thousandth of the width of an atom. Even then, its surface would have to be moving at about 137 times the speed of light. Nothing can do that: as Chasing a Beam of Light and the journeys after it found, nothing with mass can even reach the speed of light, let alone pass it. And since then, experiments have shown the electron to be far smaller even than that, if it has a size at all, which makes the spinning faster still.

So an electron isn’t a little ball spinning. It’s a particle that simply has a fixed amount of turning, and a magnet, built in, the way it simply has its charge and its mass. The name spin is a leftover from a picture that failed, kept because physicists had got used to it. Protons and neutrons have spin too, of the same two-answer kind, and so does every electron in the grain of sugar under Albert’s glass.

Hydrogen’s radio note

Spin’s two answers are written across the whole sky.

A hydrogen atom is one electron and one proton, and both are tiny magnets. In the atom’s lowest level, their two magnets can point the same way or opposite ways, and the two arrangements have very slightly different energies: the same way is a little higher. Left alone, an atom in the higher arrangement will sooner or later flip its electron to the lower one, and give out the difference as a single photon. Because the difference is so small, the photon’s energy is tiny, and its wavelength long: 21 centimetres, about the length of a pencil, in the radio part of the spectrum.

A single atom is in no hurry. On average, it waits about eleven million years before it flips. But space is full of hydrogen, thin clouds of it stretching between the stars across the whole Milky Way, and with so many atoms, some are flipping all the time. The galaxy hums, very faintly, at a single radio note. In 1944 the Dutch astronomer Hendrik van de Hulst, a student working in Leiden under wartime occupation, worked out that it should; in 1951 Harold Ewen and Edward Purcell, at Harvard, heard it first, with a horn-shaped aerial poking out of a laboratory window.

Radio waves 21 centimetres long pass straight through the dust hiding most of the galaxy from ordinary light, so with them astronomers could map the hydrogen right across the Milky Way and see its spiral arms for the first time. (A later series, about how galaxies are built, comes back to that map.) Every note of it is a hydrogen atom changing its mind, from one of spin’s two answers to the other.

A magnet in the hospital

There’s a much stronger magnet a little closer to home.

Your body is mostly water, and every molecule of water has two hydrogen atoms, each with a proton at its centre, and every proton is a tiny two-answer magnet. Put a person inside a magnet a few tens of thousands of times stronger than the Earth’s, the tunnel of a hospital’s MRI scanner, and the protons’ magnets line up with the field or against it. Pointing along the field takes very slightly less energy than pointing against it, so slightly more of them point along it: about five in every million more, which, with so many protons, is enough to work with. (The name is short for magnetic resonance imaging.)

The scanner then sends in pulses of radio waves at exactly the frequency that matches the difference in energy between the two answers, about 64 million waves a second in a common scanner, and flips some of the protons over. As they flip back, they broadcast at the same frequency, and how quickly they do it depends on what’s around them: fat, muscle, a tumour, the fluid round the brain. Small extra magnets, switched on and off, make the frequency different in different places, so the scanner can tell where each broadcast came from. Put together, the broadcasts make a picture of the inside of a body, without a knife or an X-ray, from protons answering up or down.

Back to Teide

By evening the plateau had cooled, and the three pairs of sunglasses lay folded on the table, the coach party’s on top. Cal went out to The Van, switched off the simulator’s quantum mode at last, and the simulator’s Van, which had been sitting out on the lava since the morning before, vanished from the screen. The compass in its glass dome swung, wobbled, and settled, pointing at magnetic north, more or less, and turning smoothly when he rocked The Van on its springs, as a compass should.

“It’s back to being billions of billions of electrons,” he said, when he came in.

“Every one of them still with only two answers,” said John. “Up or down. Along or against. Nothing in between.” He looked at the sunglasses. “It’s a strange thing for the world to be built on. Two answers.”

“You asked me something,” he said, “on the first morning, about that hand and this table.”

“Why it doesn’t go through,” said John. “When the atoms are almost all empty.”

“Those two answers,” said Albert, “are why it doesn’t.”
