Fred’s new atoms

In a steady universe the density ρ\rhoρ never changes, while every region’s volume grows with the cube of the scale, V∝a3V \propto a^3V∝a3. If the expansion rate H=a˙/aH = \dot a/aH=a˙/a is constant too, aaa grows as eHte^{Ht}eHt and any volume grows at the rate 3H3H3H times itself, so to keep the density fixed, new matter must appear at a rate

dmdt dV=3Hρ.\frac{dm}{dt\,dV} = 3H\rho.dtdVdm​=3Hρ.

In Hoyle’s version the density is the critical density, ρ=3H2/8πG\rho = 3H^2/8\pi Gρ=3H2/8πG. With today’s expansion rate, H0=67.4H_0 = 67.4H0​=67.4 kilometres a second per megaparsec =2.18×10−18 s−1= 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}=2.18×10−18 s−1,

ρ=3×(2.18×10−18)28π×6.674×10−11=8.5×10−27 kg m−3,\rho = \frac{3 \times (2.18 \times 10^{-18})^2}{8\pi \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11}} = 8.5 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg m}^{-3},ρ=8π×6.674×10−113×(2.18×10−18)2​=8.5×10−27 kg m−3,

about five hydrogen atoms’ worth in a cubic metre, and

3Hρ=5.6×10−44 kg m−3 s−1.3H\rho = 5.6 \times 10^{-44}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}.3Hρ=5.6×10−44 kg m−3 s−1.

A hydrogen atom weighs 1.67×10−271.67 \times 10^{-27}1.67×10−27 kg, so that is 3.3×10−173.3 \times 10^{-17}3.3×10−17 new atoms in each cubic metre each second: one atom a cubic metre in about a billion years, or about one atom in each cubic kilometre each year.

Squeezing heats

A gas. Squeezed quickly, with no time to lose heat to its surroundings, a gas obeys

TVγ−1=constant,T V^{\gamma - 1} = \text{constant},TVγ−1=constant,

with γ=1.4\gamma = 1.4γ=1.4 for air. Squeezing a pump’s air to a quarter of its volume would raise it from 20 °C (293 K) to 293×40.4=510293 \times 4^{0.4} = 510293×40.4=510 K, about 240 °C. A real pump squeezes more slowly and loses most of the heat to its barrel, which is why the barrel only gets warm. For a gas of single atoms, γ=5/3\gamma = 5/3γ=5/3, and in an expanding universe its volume goes as a3a^3a3, so on its own such a gas cools as T∝a−2T \propto a^{-2}T∝a−2.

Light. As the universe grows, light’s wavelength grows with it, λ∝a\lambda \propto aλ∝a (Further Apart Every Day). A hot body’s glow keeps its shape when every wavelength is stretched by the same factor, but its peak, by Wien’s law, sits at λmax∝1/T\lambda_\text{max} \propto 1/Tλmax​∝1/T, so the light’s temperature falls as

T=T0a,T0=2.7255 K.T = \frac{T_0}{a}, \qquad T_0 = 2.7255\ \text{K}.T=aT0​​,T0​=2.7255 K.

Run backwards, aaa shrinks and TTT grows without limit. When the oldest light set out, a≈1/1100a \approx 1/1100a≈1/1100, and T≈3,000T \approx 3{,}000T≈3,000 K. Early on, the light shares its heat with whatever particles it can make; as the universe cools, some kinds of particle disappear (the electrons and positrons, after a few seconds, turning into more light), so the strict rule is that TaT aTa times the cube root of g∗sg_{*s}g∗s​, a count of the kinds of particle sharing the heat, stays the same. That makes the very early universe cooler, at a given scale, than T0/aT_0/aT0​/a alone says: by about 30 per cent at one second, when the electrons and positrons were still there to be turned into light.

Density. The energy of light, per cubic metre, goes as T4T^4T4, so it falls as a−4a^{-4}a−4: the number of lumps falls as a−3a^{-3}a−3, and each lump’s energy as a−1a^{-1}a−1. Matter’s density falls as a−3a^{-3}a−3. So going back, light comes to outweigh matter, before about 50,000 years.

The distance ladder

Parallax. A star at distance ddd seen from either side of the Earth’s orbit, which has a radius of 1 astronomical unit (150 million km), shifts back and forth by an angle ppp, its parallax, with

d=1 AUp⇒d (parsecs)=1p (arcseconds),d = \frac{1\ \text{AU}}{p} \quad\Rightarrow\quad d\ (\text{parsecs}) = \frac{1}{p\ (\text{arcseconds})},d=p1 AU​⇒d (parsecs)=p (arcseconds)1​,

a parsec being 3.26 light-years. Bessel’s star, 61 Cygni, has a parallax of 0.286 arcseconds: 3.5 parsecs, 11.4 light-years. Gaia measured parallaxes to about a hundred-thousandth of an arcsecond for bright stars.

Cepheids. Leavitt’s law: a Cepheid’s true brightness rises with its period. In visible light its absolute magnitude (how bright it would look from 10 parsecs) is about

M≈−2.43 (log⁡10Pdays−1)−4.05,M \approx -2.43\,(\log_{10} P_\text{days} - 1) - 4.05,M≈−2.43(log10​Pdays​−1)−4.05,

and its distance follows from how bright it looks, mmm:

m−M=5log⁡10d10 pc.m - M = 5 \log_{10} \frac{d}{10\ \text{pc}}.m−M=5log10​10 pcd​.

Baade’s two kinds of Cepheid differ by about 1.5 magnitudes, a factor of four in brightness, so using the wrong one put every galaxy at half its true distance.

Masers. A cloud of gas orbiting a black hole at radius rrr with speed vvv is accelerated towards it at v2/rv^2/rv2/r. For the clouds passing in front of the black hole, that acceleration is along our line of sight, and shows as a slow drift in their lines’ shift, a few kilometres a second each year; their speed comes from the clouds at the disc’s edges. So r=v2/ar = v^2/ar=v2/a is known in kilometres, and radio telescopes across the Earth measure the angle θ\thetaθ it covers on the sky, giving

d=rθ.d = \frac{r}{\theta}.d=θr​.

For M106, d=7.58d = 7.58d=7.58 megaparsecs, to within about 2 per cent (Reid, Pesce and Riess, 2019): 24.7 million light-years.

Supernovae. Exploding white dwarfs of the kind the ladder uses peak at an absolute magnitude of about −19.3-19.3−19.3, once their brightness is corrected by how quickly they fade. Calibrated by Cepheids in the same galaxies, they reach billions of light-years, and their speeds then give the expansion rate, H0=v/dH_0 = v/dH0​=v/d: about 73.0±1.073.0 \pm 1.073.0±1.0 (the SH0ES team, 2022), against the oldest light’s 67.4±0.567.4 \pm 0.567.4±0.5.

The starting ratio comes from the theory of how the two were made together, in the same violent event (the eleventh journey). If the ratio measured now were a quarter of the starting one, the age would be 9.45×ln⁡4=13.19.45 \times \ln 4 = 13.19.45×ln4=13.1 billion years. Frebel and her colleagues combined the ratios of uranium and thorium to each other and to several other heavy elements in the light of HE 1523-0901 for an age of 13.2 billion years; the uncertainty, about two billion years, comes mostly from the starting ratios.
