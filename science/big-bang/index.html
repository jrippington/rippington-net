The Van's simulator runs the universe backwards until it glows, John and Cal find four clocks that know nothing of each other and tell the same time, and the simulator, asked what came before, says something nobody expected it to say.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 2 of 18

Tea, strong

Albert had found the tea by the time Fred arrived, in a tin marked SUGAR this time, and the old teapot was warming on the counter.

Fred came in out of the bright morning in a heavy coat he didn’t need, a big man with a broad, weathered face and a voice from the north of England that filled the café without trying. Under his arm were a rolled-up newspaper and a paperback novel, which he put face down on the table, as if it might give him away. He shook Albert’s hand for a long time, and then John’s and Cal’s, and looked at the teapot.

“Strong,” he said. “Leave the bag in. If it’s not strong, it’s just warm water with ideas.”

He and Albert were old friends, it was obvious within a minute, and they disagreed about almost everything, and enjoyed it. Albert poured the tea, which came out the colour of the plateau’s lava, and Fred tasted it and nodded.

“So,” he said, settling back. “Albert tells me you’ve been learning to read starlight. Good. Best thing there is. And he tells me you want to know where it all came from.” He looked from John to Cal. “And I suppose he’s been filling your heads with this big bang idea.”

“I have told them nothing,” said Albert. “They have worked out what the stars are made of. That is all.”

“Give it a day.” Fred drank some more tea. “The whole universe popping into being at one moment, for no reason, out of nothing, with nothing before it. It isn’t physics. It’s a creation story with sums attached.”

“Didn’t you give it its name?” said Cal, who had looked him up.

Fred put his cup down.

“On the wireless,” he said. “The Third Programme, March 1949. I was giving talks about the universe, and I needed a picture the listeners would remember. So I said that the other lot thought all the matter had been made in one big bang, at a particular time in the remote past. It wasn’t meant rudely.”

There was a pause.

“It sounds a bit rude,” said John.

“It sounds vivid,” said Fred. “Vivid isn’t rude.”

Albert said nothing, in a way that said a great deal, and refilled Fred’s cup.

A universe that never began

Fred’s own universe deserves a fair hearing, because it was a serious idea, put forward by serious scientists, and for nearly twenty years it was a real rival. In 1948 Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold, and separately Fred Hoyle, all in Cambridge, proposed what came to be called the steady-state theory.

They accepted that the universe expands: the galaxies’ light is stretched, as Further Apart Every Day found, and the farther the galaxy, the faster its distance grows. What they didn’t accept was that the universe had to change because of it. Their starting point was a principle: that the universe looks the same not only from every place, which most astronomers already assumed, but at every time. No special place, and no special moment either.

“So as the galaxies move apart,” said Fred, “new matter appears in the gaps. Not much. Atoms of hydrogen, one here and one there, out of nothing. New galaxies form from them, and the old ones move off and thin out, and the whole thing stays just as it is, for ever. No beginning, no end. The universe today looks like the universe a billion years ago, and like the universe a billion years from now.”

“Out of nothing,” said Cal. “Isn’t that the same thing you just said was cheating?”

“A little out of nothing, all the time, everywhere,” said Fred, “instead of the whole lot at once at the start. Which sounds more like physics to you?”

How little? To keep the universe steady while it expands at the rate it does, the theory needs about one new atom of hydrogen in each cubic kilometre of space, each year. Nobody could ever hope to catch that happening, so it couldn’t be tested directly. But the theory as a whole could be, and that was its great strength. It made a definite prediction: the universe shouldn’t change with time. Distant galaxies, seen as they were billions of years ago, should look just like nearby ones.

For the mathematically inclinedFred’s new atoms

And in 1948 Fred had a weapon. The rate at which the universe was expanding, as Edwin Hubble had measured it, said something about its age. Run the galaxies backwards at the speeds they have now, and they’d all have been together about two billion years ago. If the universe began, it began then. But by the 1940s, the radioactive atoms in the Earth’s own rocks and lead had shown that the Earth was more than three billion years old.

“A universe younger than its own rocks,” said Fred. “You can’t be younger than your own furniture. Their beginning didn’t add up.”

It didn’t, then. It was a fair argument, and a strong one.

Albert, who had been listening with his cup in both hands, set it down.

“When I was younger,” he said, a little stiffly, “I also preferred a universe that did not change. I believed it stood quite still. I even added something to my equations to keep it there.”

“There you are,” said Fred to John and Cal. “He’s on my side really.”

“I was on the side of a universe that does not change,” said Albert. “The universe was not.”

Running the film backwards

John had been thinking, while the tea went round, about the currant dough.

On the last day of the relativity series, Albert had pressed currants into a sheet of dough and let it rise, and every distance between the currants had grown, the farther ones faster, just as the distances between galaxies do. The dough had no centre and no edge that mattered: from every currant, all the others moved away in the same way.

“If the dough’s rising now,” said John, “then an hour ago it was smaller. Run the film backwards and the currants get closer together. Keep running it, and they get as close as you like.”

“If the film runs backwards in the same way,” said Fred. “That’s the whole question.”

They went out to The Van to find out. The simulator’s update had brought a second new switch with it, beside the one for its grades: a dial marked HISTORY, which turned both ways. John set it to run the universe backwards from today, starting with the galaxies round their own, and turned it.

HISTORY: REWINDING

On the screen, the galaxies drew together. It was slow at first: a billion years back, they were only a little closer; five billion, closer still, and the spirals among them smaller and bluer, crowded with young stars.

GALAXIES: CLOSER TOGETHER. OBSERVED

That was the light they had from those times, real light from real galaxies far away, which had set out billions of years ago. Further back, the galaxies grew fewer and smaller, and then, at about three hundred million years after the beginning, they were gone, and the screen was dark.

NOTHING SHINING YET. RECONSTRUCTED

John turned the dial further, and the dark began to glow: a dull red at first, everywhere at once, then orange, then yellow, then white, brighter and brighter, until the screen was a single blinding sheet of light and the simulator dimmed it to save their eyes.

TEMPERATURE: 3,000 DEGREES AND RISING. VIEW: A GLOWING FOG

Squeezing makes things hot

Cal had come out of The Van while it was glowing and was pumping up its back tyre, which was soft, as it usually was. When he’d finished he put his hand round the end of the pump, and then held it out to John.

“Feel that,” he said.

The end of the pump’s barrel was warm.

Squeezing a gas heats it. When the pump’s piston pushes in, it’s moving towards the air molecules in front of it, and every molecule that hits it bounces off faster than it arrived, like a tennis ball off a racket swung towards it. Faster molecules are hotter air. Let a gas expand, and the opposite happens: it pushes a retreating piston, the molecules bounce off it slower, and the gas cools, which is why a spray can goes cold in your hand.

The universe is the same, run backwards. Its gas, squeezed as every distance shrinks, gets hotter. And so does its light. Further Apart Every Day found that as the universe grows, light crossing it is stretched, its waves longer, its colour redder. Run the film backwards and the light is squeezed: its waves shorter, its colour bluer, its glow hotter. Whatever is in the universe, gas or light, gets hotter the further back you go.

So, step by step:

  • Every distance between distant galaxies is growing now. That’s measured, and Fred agrees.
  • So, unless something new keeps being added, every distance was smaller in the past, and everything in the universe was packed closer together.
  • Squeezing heats a gas, and squeezes light into bluer, hotter light.
  • So the universe was hotter and denser the further back you go, everywhere, without limit, as far as the rules can be followed: the dark, then a red glow, then a white-hot one, then hotter still.

The only way out is the one Fred took: that something keeps the universe from being denser in the past, by making new matter all the time. Whether that happens isn’t a matter of argument. It’s a matter of evidence, and the evidence comes through the rest of this journey and the next two.

The first person to follow the film all the way back was Georges Lemaître, the Belgian physicist and priest who, in 1927, had shown that general relativity allowed an expanding universe. In 1931 he proposed that the universe had begun as a single, unimaginably dense “primeval atom”, on what he called a day without yesterday. In the 1940s George Gamow and his student Ralph Alpher, in Washington, made the beginning hot, and worked out what a hot beginning would make, which is the next journey’s story.

The Big Bang

The hot, dense early state of the universe, out of which it has been expanding and cooling everywhere at once for about 13.8 billion years; and the theory describing it. It wasn’t an explosion at a point in empty space: it happened everywhere, and every distance between distant galaxies has been growing ever since. The name comes from a radio talk given in 1949 by the astronomer Fred Hoyle, who never accepted the theory. The theory describes what happened after the beginning, not the beginning itself.

“Everywhere at once” needs a little care, because When Exactly Is Now? found that “at once” depends on who’s asking. Here it means by the one clock all the galaxies share, which counts the time since the beginning (cosmic time, in Further Apart Every Day), and which reads the same for every galaxy riding along with the expansion.

How old?

If the film runs back to a hot, dense beginning, how long ago was it?

The expansion itself is a clock. Suppose the galaxies had always moved apart at the speeds they have now. Then a galaxy twice as far away, moving twice as fast, would have taken exactly the same time to get there as one half as far: every galaxy would have set out from the same place at the same moment. Divide any galaxy’s distance by its speed, and you get the same answer for all of them, about 14.5 billion years.

But the speeds haven’t been the same all along. For most of history, gravity slowed the expansion, so it was faster in the past and the galaxies got where they are sooner; and for the last six billion years or so, dark energy has been speeding it up, as Further Apart Every Day found. Put in what the universe contains, and how each part has pushed and pulled, and the clock reads about 13.8 billion years. The best measurement, from the oldest light, by the Planck satellite, gives 13.79 billion years, give or take twenty million.

For the mathematically inclinedThe age from the expansion

That calculation needs two things: how fast each galaxy is moving away, which its lines’ redshift gives directly, and how far away it is, which nothing gives directly at all. In 1948 the distances were wrong, and that was the whole of Fred’s age problem.

A ladder of distances

How far away is a star? Nothing in its light says, by itself. A bright light could be a feeble lamp close by or a lighthouse far off. Astronomers get the distances one step at a time, each step resting on the one below, which is why it’s called the distance ladder.

The bottom rung is geometry, and you can try it now. Hold up a thumb at arm’s length, and look at it with one eye shut, then the other. It jumps against the background, because your two eyes see it from slightly different places. The nearer the thumb, the bigger the jump; measure the jump and the distance between your eyes, and you could work out how far away the thumb is. Astronomers do the same with the Earth’s orbit: they look at a star in January and again in July, from two places 300 million kilometres apart, and measure how far it seems to move against much more distant stars. Friedrich Bessel managed it first, in 1838, for a star about eleven light-years away. The jumps are tiny, but the European Space Agency’s Gaia satellite measured them for more than a billion stars before it was switched off in 2025.

The second rung is stars whose true brightness is known. In 1912 Henrietta Leavitt, at Harvard, found that a kind of pulsing star, a Cepheid, varies at a rate set by how bright it really is: the slower it pulses, the brighter it is. Time a Cepheid’s pulse and you know its true brightness, and its faintness then says how far away it is, as the first journey’s lamp twice as far looked a quarter as bright. Cepheids can be seen in other galaxies. The third rung is a kind of exploding star, so bright it can be seen across most of the universe, whose peak brightness is always nearly the same: a lamp of known power, billions of light-years away.

Each rung is calibrated by the one below it, so the whole ladder depends on its bottom. In 1952 Walter Baade, at Mount Palomar, found that the bottom had been wrong: there are two kinds of Cepheid, of different brightness, and the astronomers measuring other galaxies had been using the wrong one. Every distance beyond our galaxy doubled overnight. More corrections followed, and by 1958 Allan Sandage’s measurements gave an expansion rate that made the universe more than ten billion years old. The universe was older than its rocks after all. Fred’s age problem had been a broken rung.

“I’ll grant you that,” said Fred. “Though I’d like it noted that it was a real problem.”

It was, and noting it is the honest thing to do. It was solved by measuring better, not by wishing it away.

A galaxy measured by geometry

One of John’s pictures holds a galaxy that lets astronomers skip the middle of the ladder altogether. Near the black hole at the centre of the spiral galaxy M106, clouds of gas orbit in a thin disc less than a light-year across, and they shine as natural microwave lasers, called masers, from water in the gas. Radio telescopes spread across the Earth can track those clouds: how fast they move along the line of sight, from their lines’ shift, and how fast they drift across the sky. A cloud whose true speed and apparent drift are both known gives its distance by pure geometry, as a car seen to cross a known angle of your view at a known speed is at a definite distance. M106 is about 24.7 million light-years away, measured to within a couple of per cent, and it’s one of the three anchors the whole distance scale is tied to.

The ladder still has a puzzle at the top. Measured with the ladder, the universe expands about nine per cent faster today than the rate the oldest light predicts. It’s the Hubble tension of Further Apart Every Day, and nobody yet knows whether a rung is still slightly wrong or something new is going on. It changes the universe’s age by about a billion years, at most.

Clocks that know nothing of each other

“So the expansion gives you an age,” said Fred, “if you trust your model of everything that’s in the universe, and your ladder, and your oldest light. That’s a lot of trusting.”

He had a point, and the answer to it is the best evidence of this journey. There are clocks that know nothing about the expansion at all.

The first is in John’s own pictures. M13, the great globular cluster in Hercules, is a ball of several hundred thousand stars, all born together, about 24,200 light-years away. A star’s life depends on its weight: the heaviest burn through their fuel fastest and go first, the lightest last almost for ever, for reasons the tenth journey sets out. So a cluster of stars all born together is a clock. Look at which of its stars are still burning steadily, and how heavy the heaviest of them is, and you can read off how long it’s been. M13’s stars are about 12 billion years old. M3, another of John’s clusters, about 33,200 light-years away, is about 11.75 billion, and the oldest clusters in our galaxy come out at about 13 billion.

Teleport to NyxThe Globular Cluster M3M3, another of John’s ancient clusters, about 11.75 billion years old

The second clock is the dead stars. A star like the Sun ends as a white dwarf, a burnt-out core the size of the Earth, which makes no new heat and simply cools, like an ember lifted out of a fire. The tenth journey meets them properly. The longer an ember has been out of the fire, the cooler and fainter it is, and the coolest, faintest white dwarfs in a cluster say how long the cluster has been there. In the globular cluster M4, the Hubble Space Telescope found white dwarfs that put it at about 12 billion years.

The third clock is radioactive atoms. Through the Wall found that a radioactive kind of atom decays at its own fixed rate, its half-life: uranium-238’s is about four and a half billion years, and thorium-232’s about fourteen billion. A star made with uranium and thorium in it has been losing the uranium faster than the thorium ever since. In 2007 astronomers measured both in the light of an old star called HE 1523-0901, and from how much uranium was left, compared with thorium, worked out its age: about 13.2 billion years, give or take two.

Three clocks made of stars, built on three different pieces of physics: how stars burn, how embers cool, how atoms decay. None of them uses the expansion, or the distance ladder beyond our own galaxy, or the oldest light. Each can only say that the universe is at least as old as the stars it measures, since nothing can be older than the universe it’s in. And all three say: a little under 13.8 billion years.

The figure below puts the four clocks side by side, each with its uncertainty, on one scale of billions of years. Choose an age for the universe, and each clock says whether it allows it. The clocks made of stars only say “at least this old”, so they rule out anything younger and accept anything older; the expansion says how old, give or take. Look for an age every clock accepts. Then go back to 1950, when the expansion, as it was then measured, made the universe younger than the Earth’s own rocks, and see why Fred’s argument looked so strong.

Is there a way out? Perhaps the clocks agree by accident. But each would have to be wrong in just the right way to land on the same answer as the others, and they’re built on physics that has nothing in common. Or perhaps the universe is far older, and these stars just happen to be the oldest around? That’s the steady state’s answer: in Fred’s universe new galaxies are always forming and old ones always moving off, so there should be stars of every age, some very much older than these. Astronomers have looked hard, in our galaxy and others, and nothing has been found clearly older than 13.8 billion years. (One old star near the Sun, nicknamed Methuselah, was put in 2013 at about 14.5 billion, give or take the best part of a billion, which overlaps.) The oldest things anywhere are just a little younger than the beginning, as if the beginning really happened.

“Clocks can be wrong,” said Fred.

“All of them?” said Cal. “By the same amount?”

Fred looked at his tea for some time.

“They can be wrong,” he said again, with less conviction, and asked for more tea.

What happened to Fred’s universe

Clocks weren’t what finished the steady state. Its own prediction did: that the universe shouldn’t change with time.

In the 1950s and 1960s, the radio astronomers at Cambridge, led by Martin Ryle, counted the sources of radio waves across the sky, faint and bright. The faint ones are mostly the far ones, seen as they were long ago, and there were far too many of them: the universe, long ago, had been more crowded with radio galaxies than it is now. Then, from 1963, came the quasars, the blazing cores of galaxies so far away that their light had been travelling for billions of years. John’s pictures are full of them, faint dots in the corners of the frames, and they’re almost all a very long way off: the nearest bright one is more than two billion light-years away. The universe long ago was a different place, full of things that don’t exist now, and a steady universe can’t do that.

The final blow came in 1965, and it’s the subject of the fourth journey: a faint glow from every direction in the sky, exactly what a hot beginning would have left behind. Fred never accepted it. He spent the rest of his life working on other versions of his universe, and arguing, brilliantly and stubbornly, with everyone who thought otherwise. He was wrong about the beginning. He was right about a great deal else, as a later journey finds.

“You keep saying I lost,” said Fred, cheerfully, to nobody in particular. “I’d like to see the referee’s report.”

Where knowing stops

After lunch, John wanted to run the film all the way back.

He took the simulator back to the glowing fog, and then further. The light grew too hot for any eye, its glow moving out of the visible colours into the ultraviolet, then X-rays, then gamma rays. At three minutes after the beginning it was about a billion degrees, and the simulator showed nuclei being made, which is the next journey’s story. At one second, ten billion degrees: too hot for any nucleus but a single proton to hold together. Further still, and the protons and neutrons themselves came apart into the quarks of What the Sugar Is Made Of, swimming in a soup of gluons and light. The simulator’s grade changed as they went.

RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

How far back can anyone trust it? It depends on how well the physics at each temperature has been tested on Earth.

Back to about 380,000 years after the beginning, the evidence is light: from galaxies seen back to about 300 million years, and from the glow of the fog itself, at 380,000, which reaches us still from the fog’s edge, the surface of last scattering, and is the fourth journey’s. Before that, nothing has been seen, because the fog was too thick for light to cross. But the physics of hot gas and light is among the best understood there is, and it can be followed back with great confidence to about a second, where the first nuclei were made. Those nuclei are still here, in the oldest gas, and the next journey checks them against what was made: RECONSTRUCTED, and very sure.

Before a second, the temperatures are those of the particles physicists collide in their accelerators. The Large Hadron Collider at CERN, near Geneva, reaches the energies of about a trillionth of a second after the beginning, one collision at a time, and the physics of those particles has been tested there in detail. Back to a trillionth of a second, the simulator is still reconstructing, a little less sure, but on solid ground.

Before that, it stops.

BEFORE A TRILLIONTH OF A SECOND: SPECULATION

Inflation

There is an idea about what came before, and it’s a good one, but it’s an idea.

In 1980 the American physicist Alan Guth proposed that in the first tiny fraction of a second, far less than a trillionth, the universe grew enormously fast, doubling in size again and again, by a factor of a hundred trillion trillion or more, in less time than light takes to cross a proton. It would explain some puzzles the Big Bang on its own can’t. Why the universe is so smooth, nearly the same in every direction, though its far sides have never had time to exchange so much as a ray of light: everything we see would once have been a tiny patch, smoothed out before it was stretched. Why space is so nearly flat, as a balloon blown up to the size of the Earth would look flat to an ant on it. And where the galaxies came from: the tiny, unavoidable jitter of the quantum series, stretched to the size of the sky, would leave a pattern of very slightly denser and thinner places, the seeds round which galaxies would later grow.

Cosmic inflation

A proposal, not a settled fact: that in a tiny fraction of the first trillionth of a second, the universe grew enormously fast, by a factor of a hundred trillion trillion or more, smoothing it, flattening it, and stretching tiny quantum jitters into the seeds of galaxies. The pattern of ripples in the oldest light, which the fourth journey looks at, matches what it predicts, which is evidence for it, not proof. Nobody has seen it directly, physicists don’t agree on what would have driven it, and some propose other ideas altogether.

The evidence is in the oldest light’s ripples, which the fourth journey looks at, and they match inflation’s predictions well. But matching isn’t proof. In 2014 a team announced that it had found the clearest signature of all, a twist in the oldest light made by ripples in spacetime from inflation itself, and within a year it turned out to be dust in our own galaxy. Inflation may well be right. Nobody knows yet.

Before the beginning?

And before that? John turned the dial to its stop and asked the simulator to go further.

TIME ZERO: NOT FOUND

It thought for a while longer.

BEFORE: THE QUESTION MAY HAVE NO ANSWER

“There,” said Fred, who had come out to watch. “Even your van agrees with me. There’s no beginning in it.”

“That isn’t quite what it said,” said Cal.

It isn’t. Run the rules of the Big Bang right back, and they say the universe was once infinitely hot and dense, at a single moment, which is where the rules stop making sense, as One Way In found that general relativity stops making sense at the centre of a black hole. That’s not a beginning anyone has seen; it’s the edge of the theory. To know what happened there would take a theory of gravity and the quantum together, which nobody has. It might be that time itself began there, and that asking what came before is like asking what’s north of the North Pole, as Stephen Hawking once put it. It might be that something came before. The honest grade is the simulator’s.

“So you don’t know,” said Fred.

“We know the universe was hot and dense,” said John, “13.8 billion years ago, because the film runs back that far and four clocks say so. We don’t know how it got that way.”

“That,” said Albert, from the café door, “is a great deal more than anyone knew when I was young.”

Back to Teide

By evening, Fred had drunk five cups of tea and won, by his own count, most of the arguments. He put on his heavy coat at the door and tucked his newspaper and his novel under his arm. It was, John had found out by turning it over while Fred was in The Van, a story about a cloud of gas that came to visit the Sun and turned out to be cleverer than anyone on Earth.

“I’ll tell you what bothers me about your hot beginning,” he said. “If it was all so hot, where’s the heat gone? You’ve got a universe that glowed white-hot everywhere, and now it’s cold and dark. Find me that heat, and I’ll listen.”

“Will you?” said Cal.

“I’ll listen,” said Fred. “I didn’t say I’d agree.” He shook Albert’s hand. “Good tea. Strong.”

They watched his car go down the road towards the coast. Albert collected the cups, and the teapot, and put the tea back in its tin.

“He is the best astronomer I know,” he said, “and the most stubborn. Those are not unrelated.”

John was sitting at the table by the window, turning his empty cup round on its saucer.

“If it all started hot,” he said, “and then cooled, something must have been made first. When it was too hot for anything, nothing could hold together. And then, as it cooled, something could. What was the first thing ever made?”

Albert looked across the café at his oven, cold now, with the evening light on its door, and smiled.

“Tomorrow,” he said, “I am baking.”
