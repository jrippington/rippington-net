Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 5 of 14

A rather ambitious weekend

There was an observable relationship between John’s consumption of lemon doughnuts and the scale of his proposals.

One doughnut produced questions. Two produced experiments. Three meant somebody should hide The Van’s keys.

John was on his third.

“I’ve been thinking about Andromeda,” he said.

Cal stopped reaching for the last doughnut. “Andromeda is two and a half million light-years away.”

“Approximately.”

“It’s not a day trip.”

“Not from Earth’s point of view,” said John. “But suppose we went very nearly as fast as light.”

“Then it would take a bit more than two and a half million years. That’s what a light-year means.”

Cal’s point is a good one, and it is built into the unit he used.

Light-year

The distance light travels through empty space in one year: about 9.46 million million kilometres. It is a distance, not a time. Light from something a million light-years away has been travelling for a million years when it reaches us. Smaller versions are useful too: a light-day (about 26 thousand million kilometres) and a light-hour (about a thousand million kilometres). The Andromeda Galaxy is about two and a half million light-years away.

The light in John’s own photograph of the Andromeda Galaxy’s core set out about two and a half million years before it reached his telescope, and nothing can go faster than light. So nothing can get to Andromeda in less than two and a half million years. That was Cal’s point, and it sounds unanswerable.

“Is it, though?” said John. “Two and a half million light-years away, I mean. For us?”

“It’s two and a half million light-years away for everybody. It’s on the map.”

“Whose map?”

Albert set down a cup so carefully that John suspected he had been waiting for this moment. Beside the sugar bowl he put a folding carpenter’s rule, the kind with brass hinges, which he unfolded to its full two metres with a series of small clicks.

“Before you measure the way to Andromeda,” he said, “measure something smaller.”

“Like what?”

Albert nodded towards the car park.

“How long,” he said, “is The Van?”

How long is a moving van?

Everybody knew how long The Van was. Cal had measured it more than once, mainly when trying to park. It was six metres from the front bumper to the rear door that required persuasion, give or take the dent.

But Albert’s question had a trap in it, and by now they could both see roughly where it was. Measuring something that sits still is easy. Measuring something that’s moving is not, and The Van, at the speeds it was capable of, would be moving very fast indeed.

So they set up an experiment, out in the empty space near The Station from the third and fourth journeys.

They put out the probe, the battered box from the first journey, with a camera on every face and a green light that blinked. Beside it, Cal laid out a line of Albert’s little clocks from the shoebox, thirty metres long, a clock every ten centimetres, all at rest relative to the probe. (The shoebox had once again turned out to hold rather more clocks than a shoebox should.) They set them going together by Einstein’s rule from the fourth journey, with a flash from the probe in the middle of the line, so the clocks were in step, as far as the probe was concerned.

Each clock had a tiny camera looking straight across The Van’s path, and one instruction. At exactly noon on its own face, it was to take one picture of whatever was directly in front of it, and report back. The probe would take a picture of its own at the same moment, with its widest camera, of everything in sight. The clocks sat right at the edge of The Van’s path, close enough to touch it, and the probe sat four metres back from the middle of the line.

Cal took two more clocks out to The Van and fixed one to the roof above the front bumper and one above the rear door, facing outwards, where the probe’s cameras would see them. He set them going together by the same rule, with a flash from the middle of the roof, so they were in step as far as The Van was concerned.

The Van backed off a long way, came up to eight-tenths of the speed of light, and coasted along the path, past the probe and its line of clocks, arranged so that its middle would pass the probe at noon.

It was all over in a fraction of a microsecond. Then the reports came in.

At noon, by the probe’s clocks, the clock 1.8 metres ahead of the probe had seen The Van’s front bumper directly in front of it. The clock 1.8 metres behind the probe had seen the rear door. Every clock between those two had seen a bit of The Van; every clock beyond them had seen empty space. And the two clocks at the ends had caught something else: The Van’s own clocks, on the roof above the bumper and the door. In the picture of the front bumper, The Van’s front clock read eight billionths of a second before noon. In the picture of the rear door, The Van’s back clock read eight billionths of a second after noon.

“3.6 metres,” said Cal.

“It’s six,” said John.

“Not according to the probe’s clocks.”

“According to ours,” said John, looking at the two pictures of The Van’s roof, “you didn’t take them at the same time.”

John looked round the cabin. It was exactly as cramped as usual. Cal, who had gone straight to the back with Albert’s rule while The Van was still coasting, measured The Van from the inside, very carefully, twice. Six metres, give or take the dent.

“So we were squashed,” said John.

“Not from in here.”

“And the probe’s photograph?” said Albert, over the radio. There was something in his voice that suggested he already knew.

The probe’s picture, taken at the very same noon, showed The Van stretched out, about ten metres long, and not yet level with the probe at all: in the photograph, the whole of The Van was still on its way, the front bumper a couple of metres short of the probe and the rear door nearly twelve metres behind it.

Cal looked at the two results side by side.

“So at the same moment,” he said slowly, “we were three and a half metres long, ten metres long, and six metres long.”

“Depending on how you look,” said John.

“Depending on what you mean,” said Albert.

Here is the moment the pictures were taken, as the probe sees it: its line of clocks fixed, and The Van driving past. Change The Van’s speed and watch two things. The Van gets shorter between the probe’s clocks, which all read noon. And The Van’s own two clocks, at its front and back, drift further apart from noon, one before and one after.

Distances need clocks

Everyday experience says that length belongs to the object. A ruler is thirty centimetres long; The Van is six metres long; a girder is twelve kilometres long. Measure it standing still or rushing past, and the answer ought to be the same, because the object hasn’t changed. It’s tempting to think that any other answer must mean the measurement went wrong, or that the object was really squeezed.

To see why that isn’t quite right, think about how you would actually measure the length of something moving.

Measuring a train

Suppose a train is going through a station without stopping, and you want to know how long it is, using the platform. You can’t lay a tape measure along it; it won’t keep still. So you station two friends on the platform with pots of paint and a long brush each. One stands where the front of the train will be, the other where the back will be, and at the same moment each puts a mark on the platform beside their end of the train. Afterwards, at your leisure, you measure the distance between the two marks.

Everything depends on the words “at the same moment”. If the friend at the back marks the platform a second later than the friend at the front, the train has moved on in that second, and the back mark lands further along the platform: the marks are closer together than the train is long, and you get the wrong answer. To measure a moving thing, you must mark both ends at the same time.

That was exactly what the line of Albert’s clocks did. Each clock took its picture at noon, on its own face; the clocks were in step, so the pictures were all taken at the same moment, in the probe’s frame. The pictures showed where The Van’s front and back were at that one moment, and they were 3.6 metres apart.

But “the same moment” depends on the frame

The fourth journey found that clocks set going together in one inertial frame are not in step in another frame moving relative to it. So “marking both ends at the same moment” means one thing to the probe and another to The Van. The pictures themselves showed it: by The Van’s own clocks on the roof, its front was photographed sixteen billionths of a second before its back. If the two frames disagree about which marks were made at the same moment, there is no reason why they should agree about the length. And they don’t.

In The Van’s frame, nothing happened to The Van. Its length is six metres, measured the comfortable way, with a rule laid alongside, everything at rest. The probe and its line of clocks were the things moving, backwards, at eight-tenths of the speed of light. And from The Van’s point of view, the probe’s clocks were not in step: it was the clock beside The Van’s front that reached noon first, and the clock at the back, further along the moving line, that reached noon later. In between, the whole line of clocks slid backwards past The Van, so by the time the back clock took its picture, the clock beside The Van’s rear door was one that had started out much further forward. The marks were made at different times on a moving ruler, and they ended up only 3.6 of the ruler’s metres apart. (The Van also measures the probe’s ruler itself as shorter than it should be, for the same reason the probe measures The Van as short; the two effects together give exactly 3.6.)

Both accounts describe the same pictures, taken by the same clocks, of the same bits of The Van. The probe says: we marked both ends at once, and The Van is 3.6 metres long. The Van says: you marked our front first and our back later, on a ruler that was sliding past, and that’s why you got 3.6. Neither has made a mistake. Length, like simultaneity, depends on the frame.

Proper length

Every frame can agree on one length, and physicists give it a name.

Proper length

The length of an object measured in the frame in which it is at rest: its length as measured with a ruler lying alongside it, neither moving relative to the other. The Van’s proper length is six metres; so is the measurement Cal made from inside it at eight-tenths of the speed of light. Every frame agrees about an object’s proper length (it is the length in that one particular frame), and every other frame measures the object along its direction of motion as shorter.

The word “proper” here means “its own”, as in the proper time a clock measures for itself, not “correct”. The probe’s 3.6 metres is just as correct a measurement of The Van’s length in the probe’s frame.

How much shorter

The factor turns out to be the same one that slows moving clocks: the Lorentz factor from the light clock. At eight-tenths of the speed of light it is 5/3, about 1.67, and six metres divided by 5/3 is 3.6 metres.

Length contraction

An object moving relative to an inertial frame is measured in that frame as shorter along its direction of motion than its proper length: its proper length divided by the Lorentz factor. Nothing squeezes the object, and it feels nothing; to anyone moving with it, it has its usual size. The shortening is in the measurement, which depends on which events count as simultaneous. At everyday speeds it is far too small to notice; at 87 per cent of the speed of light, lengths along the motion are halved.

For the mathematically inclinedWhy the distance shrinks

First, only lengths along the direction of motion contract. The Van’s height and width, measured across its path, are the same in every frame. (The light clock relied on this: its two mirrors, one above the other, were the same distance apart for The Van and for The Station, because The Van was moving sideways to the line between them.) One way to see why is to imagine The Van flying through a hoop that just fits it. If movement made the van’s height shrink, then in the hoop’s frame The Van would slip through easily, while in The Van’s frame the hoop, which is the one moving, would have shrunk and The Van would hit it. Both can’t be true, because whether The Van hits a hoop is a single event that everyone must agree about. So heights and widths don’t change.

Second, the effect is perfectly symmetrical. The probe measures The Van as short; The Van measures the probe’s line of clocks as short. And at The Station, The Van measured The Station’s twelve-kilometre girder, which was moving past it at six-tenths of the speed of light in the fourth journey’s experiment, as only 9.6 kilometres long. That is why the flashes in The Van’s frame were seven and a half kilometres from John, not six: the girder was shorter in The Van’s frame, and the two flashes, at different times, happened where its gates had got to.

“Does that mean The Van shrinks?” Cal had asked, back in the cabin.

“To somebody watching us fly past, yes,” said John. “Along the direction of travel.”

Cal had eyed the already cramped cabin. “It had better not shrink to us.”

It doesn’t. Relative to The Van, its ruler, its chairs, its dashboard and its passengers keep their usual sizes. It’s the rest of the universe, rushing past, that The Van measures as shortened along the direction of motion. And that is how the distance to a galaxy can shrink.

FitzGerald, Lorentz and Einstein

The idea of contraction came before Einstein. In 1889 the Irish physicist George FitzGerald wrote a letter of less than half a page to the journal Science, suggesting a way to explain why Michelson and Morley’s experiment of 1887, the search for the ether in the second journey, had found nothing. Perhaps, he suggested, things moving through the ether really are squashed along their direction of motion, by just enough to hide the effect they were looking for. The Dutch physicist Hendrik Lorentz came to the same idea independently in 1892, and worked it into his theory of electrons, picturing the forces between the particles of matter changing as it moved.

To FitzGerald and Lorentz, the contraction was a real squashing of matter, caused by motion through the ether. Einstein’s paper of 1905 got the same formula from his two principles alone, the principle of relativity and the constancy of the speed of light, with no ether and no forces. In his version nothing is squashed. The contraction is a consequence of how moving frames measure lengths, and it works both ways: each frame finds the other’s lengths contracted, which no physical squashing could explain.

Andromeda in a week

Back on the plateau, sitting in The Van in the café car park, John still had Andromeda in mind.

“If the universe rushing past The Van is shortened along the direction of motion,” he said, “then the distance to Andromeda is shortened too, as far as The Van is concerned. And if we went fast enough, it might be shortened a lot.”

“How fast is fast enough?”

“Let’s find out.”

The simulator

The Van had a simulator, which could fly any journey without the inconvenience of actually going anywhere. It lived on the dashboard screen, which at that moment was showing a picture of a kettle that nobody could explain. Cal hit the screen, firmly, in the place that usually worked, and the kettle was replaced by a star map.

John typed in the destination. Then, after some thought, he typed in how fast to go. The simulator wanted it as a Lorentz factor, the number that says how strongly the effects of relativity apply, because at speeds very close to light’s the speed itself becomes an awkward number to type. At 0.8 times the speed of light, the Lorentz factor is 1.67. At 0.99 times, it’s about 7.1. At 0.9999 times, about 71. Every extra nine after the decimal point multiplies it by about three.

John typed in 130 million.

The speed that goes with that is so close to the speed of light that the difference is about three parts in a hundred million billion: The Van would be going more slowly than light by about nine millionths of a millimetre a second. It is still slower than light. Remember that nothing can ever catch up with light, let alone overtake it.

The dangerously easy calculation

Simulations need a starting point, and this one made a simplifying assumption. It treated the Earth and Andromeda as standing still relative to each other, two and a half million light-years apart, in empty space, with gravity left out. Neither is quite true. Andromeda is in fact coming towards us, at about 110 kilometres a second, and over distances this large gravity complicates the picture (the series’ second half, from the eighth journey, is about gravity). But 110 kilometres a second is a snail’s pace next to The Van, and over a single crossing the approximation is a very good one.

In that common frame, the Earth’s and Andromeda’s, the calculation is simple. The Van covers two and a half million light-years at very nearly the speed of light, so the crossing takes very nearly two and a half million years by the clocks on Earth.

In The Van’s frame, the Earth and Andromeda are the things moving: the Earth falling behind at very nearly the speed of light, and Andromeda rushing towards The Van at the same speed. And the distance between them, measured in The Van’s frame, is contracted by the Lorentz factor: two and a half million light-years divided by 130 million is about 0.0192 light-years, which is about seven light-days. Andromeda, approaching at very nearly the speed of light, would cover seven light-days in about seven days, by The Van’s clock.

For comparison, the most distant spacecraft, Voyager 1, launched in 1977, becomes one light-day from Earth in November 2026. Seven light-days is a long way, but nothing like two and a half million light-years.

The simulator had come to the same answer. Its screen said:

YOUR JOURNEY TIME TO ANDROMEDA: SEVEN DAYS.

“One week!” said John.

Cal read the next line on the screen.

RETURN ARRIVAL: APPROX. FIVE MILLION YEARS AFTER DEPARTURE.

“One week,” said Cal, “provided you never have another appointment on Earth.”

For the mathematically inclinedThe crossing in numbers

Slow clock, or short distance?

At first sight the two frames sound like two different universes, so check that they really are describing the same journey.

In the Earth’s frame, The Van takes two and a half million years to cross two and a half million light-years. But The Van is a moving clock, and moving clocks, as the light clock showed, run slow by the Lorentz factor. Two and a half million years divided by 130 million is about seven days. So the Earth’s account says: the journey takes two and a half million years, but The Van’s clock, running 130 million times slow, records only seven days of it.

In The Van’s frame, its clock runs perfectly normally, and the journey takes seven days because the distance is only seven light-days.

Both accounts have the same departure, the same arrival and the same seven days on The Van’s clock. The Earth explains it with a slow clock and a long distance; The Van explains it with a normal clock and a short distance. Both measure The Van’s speed relative to the Earth as the same, just below the speed of light. The traveller and the people who stayed at home aren’t arguing about how long a light-year is. They are measuring distances and times in different frames, and each frame’s account is complete and consistent.

You have met this pairing before. In the third journey, muons made by cosmic rays high in the atmosphere reached the ground, though they live only about two microseconds. The Earth’s account was that their clocks run slow. The muons’ own account, it promised, would come later: here it is. In a muon’s frame, the atmosphere rushing up towards it at nearly the speed of light is contracted, fifteen kilometres of air squashed into less than one, and the ground arrives before the muon’s ordinary two microseconds are up.

The Earth’s frame and The Van’s also disagree about something else, and it links this article to the fourth journey. The Van measures the distance to Andromeda with its own “same moment”: where the Earth is and where Andromeda is at one instant of The Van’s clocks. As that journey found, The Van’s “same moment” isn’t the Earth’s, and across two and a half million light-years the difference is enormous. Measured that way, the way to Andromeda is shorter, though Andromeda and the space in between are just as they were.

You can try it yourself in the next figure. It shows the distance to Andromeda measured two ways, as the Earth measures it and as The Van does, on a ruler where each step is ten times the one before, with some familiar distances marked for scale. Start at the speed of Voyager 1, about 17 kilometres a second, the fastest thing we have sent out of the solar system. At that speed the two measurements agree, and the trip takes 44 billion years by either clock, three times the age of the universe. Then speed The Van up, with the slider or the buttons, and watch its measurement shrink: past the width of the Milky Way, past the nearest star, down to seven light-days. The two clocks underneath give the time for the trip; press play to run it.

You cannot ignore the speeding-up bit

The simulation, so far, has cheated, and it should confess. It started The Van already travelling at its cruising speed as it passed the Earth, and had it fly past Andromeda at the same speed, without stopping. A real journey would have to start from rest, speed up, and slow down again to arrive.

Speeding up to a Lorentz factor of 130 million can’t simply be switched on. There is a gentler plan, still completely beyond any engineering we have: accelerate at a steady 1 g for the first half of the journey, then turn The Van round and decelerate at 1 g for the second half. One g is a rate of speeding up, about ten metres a second faster every second (9.8, more exactly): the rate at which a dropped stone gains speed near the ground. Aboard The Van it would be a steady push towards the back, firm but comfortable, for years on end. For a journey of two and a half million light-years in empty space, that plan takes about 29 years by The Van’s clocks, while about two and a half million years pass on Earth. (Working it out takes a little more mathematics than this article uses: the motion of a ship that accelerates steadily, as its own accelerometer measures it.) An extraordinary result, and not a plausible launch plan.

For the mathematically inclinedSpeeding up steadily

“How much fuel?” asked Cal.

John asked the simulator.

The screen said:

NUMBER TOO LARGE FOR DISPLAY FONT.

“That’s probably not helpful,” he said.

Why the number is so large, and why no amount of fuel could ever take The Van all the way to the speed of light, is the seventh journey’s story. It isn’t the only problem, either. At those speeds every atom of the thin gas between the stars would strike The Van’s front bumper with the energy of a high-energy cosmic ray, and The Van would need shielding that nobody knows how to build.

The Van’s neglected speedometer chose that moment to flash 17 mph.

Does it look smaller through the window?

Which leaves the probe’s photograph. If The Van was 3.6 metres long in the probe’s frame at noon, why did the photograph taken at noon show it ten metres long, and in the wrong place?

Because a photograph records the light arriving at the camera at one moment, and that light left the different parts of The Van at different times.

Why the photograph is stretched

Go back to the experiment. The probe’s camera sat four metres from The Van’s path. At noon, the light arriving at the camera from The Van’s front bumper had set out about fifteen billionths of a second earlier, when the bumper was a few metres back down the path. The light arriving from the rear door, which was further away, had set out about forty billionths of a second earlier, when the rear door was further back still, by about ten metres. At eight-tenths of the speed of light, The Van moves a long way in those few billionths of a second. So the photograph shows the front where it was a little while ago and the back where it was a longer while ago, and The Van comes out stretched, and behind where it really is.

As The Van approaches, the light from its back always has further to come than the light from its front, and has to set out earlier, so its picture is stretched: longer even than its proper six metres. Once it has passed and is going away, the light from its front has further to come, and the picture is squashed, shorter even than the contracted 3.6 metres. As it goes by, the picture’s length runs down through all the values between.

Lampa, Terrell and Penrose

People took a surprisingly long time to think this through. In 1924 the Austrian physicist Anton Lampa pointed out that a moving rod, seen by eye, wouldn’t look contracted, but his paper was overlooked. In 1959 two people independently worked out what a fast-moving object would actually look like. The American physicist James Terrell, and the English mathematician Roger Penrose (the same Penrose as the Andromeda paradox in the fourth journey), found that a fast-moving sphere would still look perfectly round; light from its far side, setting out earlier, reaches the eye from round the back, and the sphere looks rotated rather than squashed. Contraction is real, in the sense that measurements agree with it; it just doesn’t show up in snapshots the way you might expect. The effect is now called Terrell rotation.

Nobody could photograph it for more than sixty years, because nothing big enough to photograph can be made to move fast enough. Then, in 2025, physicists at the Technical University of Vienna and the University of Vienna made a laboratory version. They lit a cube and a sphere with laser pulses lasting trillionths of a second and recorded them with an ultrafast camera, assembling each picture from slices of light taken at carefully chosen moments, so that in effect the speed of light was slowed to about two metres a second. When the objects were moved through the scene, the cube appeared twisted and the sphere stayed round: just as Terrell and Penrose had said.

So the short answer to “does it look smaller?” is “not in the simple way the drawings suggest”. The figure below keeps the two things apart. It drives The Van, six metres long at rest, past the probe at a speed you choose. The panel called Measured shows the measurement: where The Van’s front (amber) and back (ice) are at one instant of the probe’s clocks, with a faint outline of its six metres at rest for comparison. The panel called Photographed shows the probe’s photograph at the same instant: where the light now arriving at the camera left each end, drawn in amber, with the light’s paths to the camera and a faint outline of where The Van really is.

Play it, and watch The Van come and go. As it approaches, the photograph is stretched out far behind it; as it passes, the photograph catches up; as it leaves, the photograph is shorter than the measurement. Change the speed: at a tenth of the speed of light, all three lengths are nearly six metres, and the photograph is nearly where The Van is. Only the Measured panel shows the steady contraction. The figure is simplified, as it says: a thin van, seen side-on by one pinhole camera, showing only where things appear, not the changes in colour and brightness that would come with them.

That distinction also takes care of the most common misunderstanding of all: that a traveller would see their own ship shrink, or feel it. They wouldn’t. In The Van’s frame, The Van is six metres long, always.

Has anyone measured it?

Length contraction is harder to see directly than time dilation, because it is hard to make anything big move fast. But it is part of every calculation in particle physics, and in some collisions it sets the shape of everything that follows.

At the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider at Brookhaven National Laboratory in New York, gold nuclei are accelerated to Lorentz factors of about a hundred and smashed into each other. A gold nucleus is roughly round, but at that speed, in the laboratory’s frame, each one is contracted along its direction of motion to about a hundredth of its width: physicists describe the collisions as two thin pancakes meeting, and their calculations of what happens next, from the shape of the region where the pancakes overlap, depend on it.

The return date

John ran the simulation twice more, with different speeds, hoping for a different answer. The Van’s clock could be made to read almost anything: a few weeks there and back at enormous Lorentz factors, a few decades at gentler ones. The calendar at home could not. Andromeda is two and a half million light-years away in the Earth’s frame, and nothing goes faster than light in any frame. A round trip, however it was flown, would bring them back to an Earth at least five million years older than the one they had left.

Cal read the date at the bottom of the screen without saying anything.

“All our friends,” he said at last.

John stopped smiling. “Yes.”

“And Albert?”

“Definitely the café’s lease,” said John, although the joke barely helped.

They hadn’t gone, of course. Not really. The Van was parked beside the café, ticking as it cooled from the afternoon’s trip to the probe, which had taken about as long as a trip to the shops. On the simulator’s screen, Andromeda glowed, indifferent to their intentions.

Back to Teide

Evening settled over the plateau. The mountain darkened against a clear sky, and the stars came out.

Cal looked up at the faint smudge they had spent the afternoon not visiting, its light two and a half million years old.

“The amazing thing,” he said, “is that the distance wouldn’t stop us. It would stop the people we left.”

“Yes,” said John. “We could cross it in a lifetime. We just couldn’t come home to anyone.”

Albert brought out fresh coffee. Neither of them asked for another doughnut.

“You have discovered,” he said, “that the speed limit is not a limit on how much of the universe one traveller can cross in a lifetime.”

“Just a limit on staying in the same lifetime as everybody else,” said Cal.

John stared at the familiar mountain for a long time.

“Suppose we do a smaller trip,” he said finally. “Go out very fast, and come back. Who’s older?”

Albert put down the coffee pot.

“Ah,” he said. “Now we must talk about twins.”
