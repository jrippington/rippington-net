At sunrise, over a sea of clouds, Albert, Niels and Fred share a table for the first time, and John asks to see all forty-seven journeys at once. A stage that bends, actors that are ripples in fields, and a plot fourteen billion years long turn out to be one story, which none of its three parts could tell alone. Then the last two lemon doughnuts.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 18 of 18

A sea of clouds

John was up before the sun, which on the plateau is not difficult, since the sun has the eastern ridge to climb first.

He sat on the terrace wall with his coffee and watched the dark go grey. Below the plateau, where the island fell away to the sea, there was no island. There was cloud: a white plain of it, flat as a tablecloth, stretching to the horizon in every direction, pink along its eastern edge with the coming day, with the summit of La Palma standing out of it far to the west like a rock in a flood. Somewhere under it were towns, roads, beaches and the first coaches, their drivers peering through the fog. Up here the air was so clear that the last stars were still out.

Cal came out and sat beside him. He had his napkin in his hand, the one with DO NOT EAT on it in Albert’s handwriting, and he unfolded it on the wall between them. It was empty. A few glittering specks of lemon sugar clung to the creases, and that was all.

“I keep checking,” he said.

“You ate it,” said John.

“I know I ate it.” Cal folded the napkin up again, carefully, as though it still held something, and put it back in his shirt pocket with the words on the outside. “I just keep checking.”

Inside, the lights came on. Albert was opening up early, which he never did, and the smell of the first batch of doughnuts came out through the terrace door. Then two things happened that had never happened before. A large, quiet man came up the terrace steps, stopped at the top to look at the clouds, and went in asking, very politely, whether it might be possible to have some tea. And a minute later a second man came up the steps two at a time, didn’t look at the clouds at all, and went in asking whether the tea in this place was still fit to strip paint, because he hoped so.

John and Cal looked at each other, and went in.

Niels was at the window table with his cup, turning it a little on its saucer. Fred was at the counter, inspecting the teapot. They had never met. Albert came out from behind the counter, wiping his hands, and stood between them like a man introducing two thunderstorms.

“Niels,” he said. “Fred. You will disagree about everything.”

“Not everything,” said Fred.

Niels considered this for some time. “About most things,” he said at last, “in a sense.”

Fred looked at him with deep suspicion, and then, for no reason anyone could see, with great delight, and carried his strong tea over to Niels’s table and sat down opposite him.

Forty-seven journeys

When everyone had a cup and a doughnut, John said what he had been thinking since the stars went out.

“Forty-seven journeys,” he said. “We’ve been to the edge of the universe we can see and inside a grain of sugar. We’ve watched the first three minutes and the last black hole. And I’ve learnt it a piece at a time, one journey and then the next. I want to see it all at once.”

“All of it,” said Albert.

“All of it. How it fits.”

Albert took a napkin from the pile by the till, and the pen from behind his ear, which, as usual, had been hidden in his hair. He drew three boxes on it, side by side.

THE STAGE, THE ACTORS, THE PLOT

“Every story,” he said, “has a stage, actors, and a plot. Physics is no different. Relativity is the stage: space and time, and what they do. Quantum mechanics is the actors: what everything is made of, and how it behaves. And the history of the universe is the plot: what the actors did, on that stage, in fourteen billion years.” He tapped the three boxes in turn. “Tell me each one, and then we will see whether they are really three.”

The stage

John took the first box, because it was where they had started.

It began with a question that turned out to have no answer. On their very first journey, out in empty space, John and Cal had tried to find out how fast The Van was really moving, and found that nothing inside it could say. A doughnut thrown across the cabin, a pendulum, a ball, all behaved exactly the same whether The Van was drifting or racing along. There was no true speed and no true stillness, only speed compared with something else. No frame is the true one.

Then light. Everyone who measures it, however they are moving, finds it going at the same speed. Michelson’s careful experiment couldn’t catch the Earth’s motion through anything, and nothing since has caught light going faster or slower for anyone. That one fact, with the first, forced everything else. If two people moving differently both see the same light at the same speed, their clocks and rulers must disagree. A moving clock runs slow, which is why muons from the top of the atmosphere reach the ground at all. A moving traveller finds the distance ahead shorter. And two distant events that happen at the same moment for one observer happen in an order for another: there is no “now” stretched across the universe that everyone shares. Space and time aren’t separate things. They’re one thing, spacetime, and each of us slices it differently.

Mass turned out to be energy. The Van’s journeys at nearly the speed of light showed why nothing with mass can reach it, and the bill for the trip showed that a doughnut, sitting perfectly still, holds an enormous amount of energy simply by having mass: E = mc².

Then gravity. Falling freely, you feel no gravity at all, and standing on the café floor you feel the floor pushing you up. Clocks higher up run faster: Pound and Rebka measured it up a tower twenty-two and a half metres tall, and the GPS satellites overhead have to allow for it, or their positions would drift by about eleven kilometres a day. Put those together, and gravity isn’t a force at all. It’s the shape of spacetime, and a falling apple follows the straightest path it can through a spacetime that curves. The shape bends light, as the eclipse of 1919 showed; it can close off a region from which nothing returns, a black hole, now photographed; it can ripple, and in 2015 the ripples from two black holes merging 1.3 billion light-years away changed the length of a ruler four kilometres long by less than the width of a proton, and were heard. And space itself is expanding, carrying the galaxies apart.

“So the stage isn’t fixed,” said Cal. “It bends, it ripples, and it stretches.”

“And everyone in the audience sees it from a different seat,” said John, “and they’re all right.”

Albert wrote under the first box, in small letters: SPACETIME, WHICH BENDS. He looked, Cal thought, a little proud of it.

The actors

Cal took the second box.

“Everything is made of atoms,” he said. “That’s where we started, with the grain of sugar.” He touched his shirt pocket without seeming to notice. “And by the old rules, every one of them should have collapsed in a flash. That was the first clue.”

The rest came in order. Hot things glow in colours set by their temperature, and that only works if energy is handed to light in lumps, never in slivers. Light itself arrives in lumps, photons, one at a time. And everything that arrives in lumps also travels as a wave: single electrons, single molecules, sent one at a time through two slits, build up stripes that only waves can make. To find where something will be, you give every way it could get there an arrow, add the arrows, and the length of the result gives the chance. Arrows can cancel, and chances can’t, which is why a second slit can make a place less likely to be hit.

A wave held in by a nucleus can only take certain shapes, so an electron in an atom has only certain energies, and the lowest has nothing beneath it to fall to. That’s why atoms last, and why each kind of atom shines and absorbs in its own few sharp colours: the fingerprints that would later tell them what the stars are made of. A wave can’t stop dead at a wall, so it leaks through, which is how a nucleus throws out an alpha particle and how two protons in the Sun ever touch. Electrons give only two answers to spin, and no two of them can ever share a state, which fills the atoms’ shells in order, makes chemistry, and holds John’s hand up off the table. Two particles can share one story however far apart they are, in a way no hidden instructions can explain. And when anything keeps a record of a particle’s way, the stripes vanish, which is why big, warm things never seem to be in two states at once.

“And underneath all of it,” said Cal, “fields. The electron isn’t a little ball. It’s a lump of ripple in a field that fills the whole universe, and every electron is the same field, doing the same thing in a different place. That’s why they’re all identical.”

“Even the empty space between them is not empty,” said Niels, so softly that they all leaned in. “It trembles.” He seemed pleased with this, and drank some tea.

There is a name for the list of fields the quantum series ended with, and it is the most precisely tested account of matter there has ever been.

The Standard Model

Physics’ account of what everything is made of and how it pushes and pulls, apart from gravity. Matter is made of the lumps of a small number of fields: the electron and two of its heavier cousins, three kinds of neutrino, and six kinds of quark, of which the up and down make every proton and neutron. Forces are carried by the lumps of other fields: photons for the electric and magnetic force, gluons for the strong force, and the W and Z for the weak force, with the Higgs field giving the particles their own masses. Worked out in the 1960s and 1970s, it has passed every test since; it leaves out gravity, and says nothing about dark matter.

Albert wrote under the second box: FIELDS, WHICH COME IN LUMPS.

“It is a very good theory,” said Niels.

“You have always said so,” said Albert, and there was a small silence, as though both of them had heard that exchange before, with the parts the other way round.

The plot

“My turn,” said Fred. “Since nobody’s asked me.”

“Nobody asked anybody,” said Cal.

“Exactly my point.” Fred took the napkin, turned it to face him, and looked at the third box with the expression of a man reading a will he intends to contest. “Go on, then. Tell me the story. I’ll stop you when it’s wrong.”

So they told it, John and Cal by turns, and he didn’t stop them once, which was unlike him.

The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, and independent clocks agree: how fast it’s expanding, the ages of the oldest stars, the cooling of dead ones, the decay of radioactive atoms. Run the expansion backwards and everything gets closer, denser and hotter, so the universe was once hot and dense everywhere at once, not an explosion at a point but a beginning everywhere: the Big Bang, a name Fred gave it on the radio in 1949, and has never been forgiven for.

In its first three minutes it was an oven hot enough to fuse nuclei, and it cooled too fast to bake anything but helium, with traces of the next few; the protons it left unbaked are the hydrogen. Then for 380,000 years it was a glowing fog, until electrons settled into atoms and the light flew free from the surface of last scattering; that light, stretched a thousand times, still arrives from every direction. In it are ripples of one part in a hundred thousand, the seeds of everything since. Something unseen, far heavier than everything that shines, gathered first in the dark, and in the long night that followed it pulled the gas into clouds, and squeezed them until their cores grew hot enough for fusion and the first stars lit. Galaxies grew from small pieces, colliding and merging, with black holes growing at their hearts; stars are still being born in their dark clouds.

One of those stars is very ordinary, and holds itself steady for ten billion years, its weight squeezing its core and the fusion there pushing back. Stars live by their mass: the heaviest shine brightest, live fastest and die in explosions that leave neutron stars and black holes behind. In their cores and their deaths, they made every element from carbon to iron, and in their collisions the gold. From the enriched gas they left, 4.6 billion years ago, a spinning cloud flattened into a disc, lit the Sun in its middle, and built planets in the rest. One of them was struck, melted and cooled, its land forever drifting, and on it life began early, changed the air, and eventually built a café on a volcano. Most stars, it turns out, have planets of their own, and whether anything lives on them is still an open question. And the plot has an ending: the stars going out one by one over a hundred trillion years, and the last black holes evaporating over a googol.

“Well?” said John.

Fred put down his tea.

“I don’t like the beginning,” he said. “I never have. But it isn’t wrong.” He looked out of the window at the sea of clouds, now pink on top. “The oldest light saw to that. I said the universe had always been the same, and the universe disagreed, and when the universe disagrees with you, you lose.” He turned back. “That’s the bit of the story I’d keep, if I could only keep one. Not the Big Bang. That.”

Albert wrote under the third box: HISTORY, WHICH CAN BE READ.

Look at the shape of it. Nearly every relativity and quantum journey sits on one line, the line marked now: they are about rules, and the rules are the same today as ever, from the size of a quark to the size of the universe. The astrophysics journeys climb away from that line, back into the history: the first trillionth of a second, three minutes, 380,000 years, a hundred million years. The rules are a single line across every size; the history is what those same rules did, over time.

Where the three meet

“So,” said Albert, and set his pen down across the three boxes, “are they three?”

It’s tempting to think so. Relativity seems to be about the very fast and the very large: spaceships, black holes, the expanding universe. Quantum mechanics seems to be about the very small: atoms, electrons, light in lumps. Astrophysics seems to be about stars and galaxies, and could be taken for a third subject with its own rules. That’s how they’re often taught, in separate courses, in separate years.

Try taking one away, and see what’s left.

Start with the most ordinary star there is, the one now lifting over the sea of clouds. The Sun shines because protons in its core fuse into helium. Take away relativity, and the helium weighs exactly what the four protons did, and there is no energy left over to shine with: the light is E = mc², the Sun’s four million tonnes a second turned into sunlight. Take away gravity as relativity tells it, and nothing squeezes the core to fifteen million degrees. Take away quantum mechanics, and the protons, pushing each other apart, never touch at all: by the old rules the core would have to be about fifty times hotter for as many approaches to end in a touch as do, about one head-on approach in thirteen thousand, and it isn’t. They touch because their waves leak through the wall between them. Take away astrophysics, the star’s balance of its weight against its heat, and the core either blows itself apart or collapses. The Sun needs all three, at once, every second.

A white dwarf is starker still. When a star like the Sun has used up its fuel, its core shrinks to the size of the Earth, with a Sun’s worth of matter in it, and nothing burns there any more. What holds it up against its own crushing weight? Only electrons refusing to share their places: the quantum series’ exclusion principle, the same rule that holds John’s hand up off the table. But squeeze the electrons harder and they move faster, and nothing can move faster than light: relativity’s limit. Put a little more mass on the star and the electrons can’t push back hard enough, because they can’t go any faster, and the star collapses. The limit, about 1.4 times the Sun’s mass, comes out of a calculation with gravity’s strength, the speed of light and Planck’s constant in it together. It’s a number no one series could ever give.

The oldest light needs all three: atoms forming, as electrons drop into the energy levels Why the Atom Survives found; the perfect spread of colours of a hot glow, which only lumps of energy explain; and space, stretching, which has turned that orange glow into microwaves. A black hole’s slow evaporation needs the quantum’s fields at relativity’s horizon. And the first three minutes needed E = mc² to make matter out of energy, tunnelling to fuse it, the antimatter of What the Sugar Is Made Of to wipe almost all of it out again, and the expanding universe to set the clock.

Is there a way out, a way of keeping them as three subjects after all? You’d have to say that each works on its own scale and never needs the others: relativity for the large, quantum mechanics for the small. But every one of these things is a place where the very small and the very large meet. The electrons that hold up a white dwarf against the gravity of a whole star are the same electrons whose colours tell astronomers what it’s made of. The sunlight on the terrace began as fusion between two protons closer together than the width of a nucleus, and arrived having crossed a hundred and fifty million kilometres of spacetime. There’s no scale at which the universe stops needing all three.

So they aren’t three. They’re one account of the world, told in three parts, and the history only makes sense with the stage and the actors together.

“One story,” said John.

“With one serious gap,” said Albert.

The few numbers

Before the gap, one more thing about the account as it stands. Albert wrote it at the bottom of the napkin.

THE SPEED OF LIGHT, THE STRENGTH OF GRAVITY, PLANCK’S CONSTANT

Almost everything the forty-eight journeys found comes from a handful of numbers. The speed of light sets how space and time trade off, and how much energy a mass holds. The strength of gravity, the number Newton first measured the effect of, sets how hard matter bends spacetime. Planck’s constant sets the size of nature’s lumps, and so the size of an atom, the colours of hydrogen and how far a wave leaks through a wall. Add the strengths of the other forces and the masses of a few particles, and you can work out, at least roughly, why an atom is the size it is, why the Sun’s core is fifteen million degrees, why a white dwarf can weigh no more than about 1.4 Suns, and why the oldest light was set free when the universe was three thousand degrees. And the same three numbers, put together, point at the gap.

The gap

There’s one place the stage and the actors have never been made to fit. Relativity describes spacetime as smooth, bending gradually wherever there’s mass. Quantum mechanics says that everything comes in lumps and trembles, even empty space. Usually it doesn’t matter: gravity is so weak that its quantum trembling is far too small to notice, and quantum effects are too small to bend anything. But there are two places where both must matter at once: at the centre of a black hole, where One Way In found the theory running out of answers, and at the very beginning, where Running the Film Backwards found knowing stop. There, enormous mass is crushed into a space so small that its quantum trembling should shake spacetime itself.

Teleport to the Relativity moonOne Way InWhere general relativity runs out of answers

You can even work out how small that space is, from the three numbers on the napkin: about a hundred billion billionth of the width of a proton. Nobody has a theory that works there.

Quantum gravity

The theory nobody yet has: one that would describe gravity, and so spacetime itself, by the quantum rules. It would be needed wherever both matter at once: at a black hole’s centre, and at the very beginning of the universe, on scales of about a hundred billion billionth of the width of a proton. Several ideas exist, among them string theory and loop quantum gravity; none has yet been tested by experiment.

How we know

“And all of it,” said Fred, “every word you’ve just said, is only worth anything because somebody measured it.”

He was right, and it’s the thing the forty-eight journeys have in common. Nothing in them was taken on trust for long. Light’s speed was measured, and found the same for everyone. Moving clocks were found to run slow, in muons and on aircraft. Clocks higher up were found to run fast, up a tower and in orbit. Starlight was seen to bend at an eclipse. Spacetime’s ripples were heard, and a black hole’s shadow photographed. In the quantum series, the zinc plate emptied under a feeble ultraviolet lamp and not under the brightest red one; single electrons built stripes on a screen; atoms showed their sharp colours; entangled photons, their settings chosen by quasars’ light billions of years old, broke the limit that any hidden instructions must obey. And in this series, the light of stars was spread out and read: their make-up, their temperature, their motion, their age. The oldest light was found, by accident, as a hiss two radio astronomers couldn’t get rid of; the leftover deuterium was weighed; galaxies were caught spinning too fast; the Sun’s neutrinos were counted, and found to change on the way.

The simulator had a habit, all through this last series, of saying how sure it was. The same three words do for the whole of physics.

OBSERVED, RECONSTRUCTED, SPECULATION

Much is observed: we have the light, or the ripples, or the particles, themselves. Much is reconstructed: worked out from rules that have passed every test, back to about a trillionth of a second. And some is speculation, honestly labelled: what came before that, what dark matter is, and whether anyone else is out there.

Where knowing stops

“So what don’t we know?” said Cal.

“A great deal,” said Albert, with some satisfaction. “That is the best part.”

They went round the table, and each of the three old friends, it turned out, had a question he cared about more than the others.

Albert’s was the gap: how to make gravity and the quantum one theory, and what happens at a black hole’s centre and at the beginning. He had, he admitted, spent a great many years on something very like it, without getting anywhere at all.

Niels’s was what actually happens when anything is measured: why one result, when the rules give every possibility its arrow. “It may be,” he said, “that the question is about what we can say, and not about what there is.” Fred snorted. Niels smiled at him with great kindness, and didn’t take it back.

Fred’s were the two that astronomers had found and nobody had explained. What the dark matter is, which outweighs everything that shines five times over and has never been caught in any detector. And what dark energy is, which is making the expansion speed up, and which might, if the latest measurements are right, be changing. “The universe,” said Fred, “is under no obligation to be what you expect. That’s the only thing I’ve ever been sure of.”

John and Cal added the last two. Why there’s any matter at all, when energy makes matter and antimatter in exact pairs, and something in the first moments must have favoured matter by one part in a billion. And whether anyone else, on any of the thousands of worlds found round other stars, is sitting at a café table asking the same questions.

“That’s a lot not to know,” said Cal.

“It’s a lot to know that we don’t know it,” said John. “A hundred and fifty years ago nobody could have even asked most of those.”

Fred stood up, went to the counter, and poured himself more tea, so strong it was nearly black.

“And one more,” he said, “that none of you will like. Something in that story you’ve just told, something you’re all perfectly sure of, will turn out to be wrong.” He sat down again. “I don’t know which. That’s the point. It always does.”

Nobody argued with him, not even Niels.

Back to Teide

By the time the first coach came grinding up out of the clouds, the sun was well clear of the ridge, and the sea of clouds below the plateau had begun to break up and drift, as the fog of the young universe once had, letting the light through.

Niels and Fred were still at the window table, arguing about something very small with great enjoyment. Albert had gone back behind the counter. After a while he came out again with a plate, and set it down between John and Cal.

On it were the last two lemon doughnuts.

They were excellent doughnuts. Everyone agreed on this.

Cal looked at his. He turned his plate through ninety degrees. It didn’t help.

“Don’t,” said John.

“I still haven’t said anything.”

“Forty-eight journeys,” said John, “and you never have.”

Out in the car park, through The Van’s windscreen, the speedometer flickered once in the morning light and settled.

17 MPH

Cal saw it first. He looked at the speedometer, then at John, and raised an eyebrow.

“Relative to what?” said John.

“Everything,” said Cal.

“Nearly,” said Albert, Niels and Fred, together, and then looked at each other, astonished to have agreed about anything at all.

John laughed until he had to put his doughnut down. When he had recovered, he looked out past The Van at the mountain, and the road climbing away towards the white domes of the observatory, and the plateau going gold in the sun.

Near the turning for the road that climbs towards the Teide Observatory, John and Cal were sitting outside a café, drinking coffee and considering the last two lemon doughnuts.
