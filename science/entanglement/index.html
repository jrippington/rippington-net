Albert has built a box on the terrace for making photons in pairs, and John takes one of every pair to the Moon. Set the two detectors alike, and the two lists of results match like a pair of gloves. Each list on its own, though, is pure chance, nothing John does can send Cal a message, and nobody, it turns out, can even say who measured first.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Field Guide to the Very Small, part 12 of 16

Twins on the terrace

The thing on the terrace had appeared overnight.

It stood on a tripod by the wall: the crystal, the lamp and the two tubes of the evening before, built into a black box about the size of a toaster, with a violet glow somewhere inside it and the two short brass tubes sticking out of it at different angles, like the barrels of two very small telescopes. One tube pointed along the terrace at a second, smaller box, which had been put on the end of John and Cal’s usual table. The other pointed at the sky, where the Moon hung over the western ridge, pale and a little more than half full, in no hurry to set.

Albert was polishing the upper tube with a napkin.

“Is this what you were doing last night?” said Cal.

“Most of it,” said Albert. “It is something I have wanted to try for a long time.” He stood back to admire it. “It makes twins.”

John bent down to look at the violet glow. Inside the box the little lamp, which was a small laser, of the kind the last journey had explained, was shining into the crystal, clear and no bigger than a sugar cube.

Nearly all of the laser’s light went straight through the crystal and out the other side into a black trap at the back of the box. But not quite all. Once in a long while, far less often than once in a million, one of the violet photons came apart inside the crystal into two. Energy has to be paid for, and each of the two new photons got half of the old one’s. As John and Cal had found in the third journey, a photon’s energy is set by its colour, more for bluer light and less for redder, so half the energy meant a much deeper colour: a red so deep it was just past what eyes can see. The two photons were born at the same instant, in the same place, and they left in slightly different directions, one into each tube.

“Twins,” said Cal. “Because they’re born together?”

“Because they are alike in the one way that matters here,” said Albert. “Their polarisation.”

Light that shakes one way

Polarisation was the subject of the three pairs of sunglasses in the tenth journey. Light is a wave, and its waves shake from side to side across the direction the light is going, like a rope shaken between two people. The direction of the shaking is the light’s polarisation. A polarising filter, like a lens from a good pair of sunglasses, passes light shaking along one direction, its axis, and stops light shaking across it.

Send the light through one photon at a time, and the rule is short and very strange. A photon meeting a polarising filter either goes through or it doesn’t: there is no half a photon. If it is polarised along the filter’s axis, it always goes through. If it is polarised across it, it never does. If it is polarised somewhere in between, it goes through only sometimes, by chance, and more often the closer its polarisation is to the axis: at halfway, forty-five degrees, it’s even chances. And a photon that has gone through is polarised along the filter, whatever it was before.

Albert’s twins, he said, were linked in their polarisation. Test the two photons of a pair at filters turned the same way, and they would do the same thing: both go through, or both be stopped.

“Every time?” said John.

“Every time.”

Two detectors

The small box on the table was a detector. Albert opened its lid to show them. Inside was no ordinary filter but a little glass cube, two prisms cemented together, of a kind that sorts light by its polarisation instead of stopping some of it: photons polarised along its axis go straight on, and photons polarised across it are turned aside, out of one face of the cube. (The clear crystal called Iceland spar does the same thing, which is why everything seen through it looks double.) Behind the cube were two little counters, one on each path, and Albert had labelled them, in his own handwriting, YES and NO.

So every photon that reached the detector gave one of two answers. YES: it went straight on. NO: it was turned aside. The cube sat in a ring with a dial, so that it could be turned to any angle: that was the detector’s setting. A photon polarised along the setting would always answer YES; across it, always NO; anything in between, by chance.

There was a second detector, exactly the same, in a doughnut box on the counter.

“That one,” said Albert, “is John’s. One photon of every pair comes to this detector, here, for Cal.” He patted the box on the table. Then he pointed at the upper tube, and the Moon. “The other goes up there. And so does John.”

A pair of gloves

“What do you expect us to find?” said John.

Albert went to the cupboard behind the counter, where the props of their earlier journeys lived, and came back with a pair of old leather gloves and two more empty doughnut boxes. He went round the end of the counter, where they couldn’t see, and came back with the lids closed. He gave one box to John and the other to Cal.

“One glove in each,” he said. “I shuffled them. Do not open them yet. John, take yours to the Moon, and open it when you get there. If you find a left glove, you will know at once that Cal’s is a right. Instantly. Nearly four hundred thousand kilometres away.”

“Without anything travelling between us,” said John.

“Nothing needed to travel. The gloves were packed before you left. Opening your box only tells you which one you were given.” Albert tapped the black box on its tripod. “That is all my twins will do. Each pair is packed before it leaves.”

Cal turned his box over in his hands. “Your photons do the same as each other,” he said. “Gloves are opposites. Shouldn’t they be socks?”

“Gloves,” said Albert, “are more dignified.”

Gloves or socks, the picture works the same way. Before John opens his box, he has no idea which glove he has: it’s left or right, even chances. Neither has Cal. Neither box, on its own, tells you anything at all. The link is only in the two together: whenever John’s is a left, Cal’s is a right. And it was settled long before either box was opened, by whoever packed them.

Correlation

A link between two sets of results, so that knowing one tells you something about the other. A pair of gloves posted in two boxes is perfectly correlated: open one, find a left glove, and you know the other is a right. A correlation is seen only by comparing the two sets side by side, and it doesn’t mean that one causes the other: more ice creams are sold on the days more people get sunburnt, because both follow the sunshine. The commonest explanation of a correlation is a common cause, something in the past of both, like the person who packed the gloves.

To the Moon

The Van rose from the car park with the sound of a dishwasher changing its mind. Albert had insisted that John take a doughnut for the journey, in case the Moon had no café.

DESTINATION: THE MOON

The new speedometer, faced with a journey to the Moon, went back to the old one’s habit and said 17 mph. Nobody remarked on it. The Van climbed past the last of the haze over Tenerife and out of the blue into the black.

The Moon is about 384,000 kilometres away. Light crosses that in about a second and a quarter, which matters for everything that follows, and The Van took rather longer, though not as long as it should have done. By the middle of the morning it had settled on a wide grey plain on the Moon’s near side, the side that always faces the Earth, and the dashboard had something to say.

ARRIVED. NEAREST DEALER: 384,400 KM

Outside, the sky was black, though the Sun was up, and the Earth hung in it, blue and white and nearly four times as wide as the Moon looks from Teide. Somewhere on it, far too small to see, was an island, and on the island a café, and on the café’s terrace a box on a tripod, pointing up at The Van.

John fixed his detector to the dashboard, facing out through the windscreen at the Earth, set its dial upright, and switched on the radio.

“Can you hear me?”

Nothing happened for a moment. Then Cal’s voice said, “Yes.”

SIGNAL DELAY: 1.3 SECONDS EACH WAY

Radio is light too, a much longer wave of it, and it goes no faster. Every word John said took a second and a quarter to reach the café, and every reply as long to come back, so that each answer arrived after a pause of more than two and a half seconds. It made every conversation sound like a careful negotiation.

The waiting room

There was a second difficulty, and Albert had thought of it. Cal’s photon of each pair had a metre or so to go, along the terrace, and it would reach his detector in a few billionths of a second. John’s had a second and a quarter of flying ahead of it. So Albert had built a waiting room into the box: a long loop of mirrors in which Cal’s photons went round and round, undisturbed, for exactly as long as their twins took to reach the Moon. Cal’s detector measured each photon at the same moment, by the café’s clocks, as John’s detector measured its twin. (The waiting room is the story’s. Real experiments arrange the timing differently, usually by putting the source between the two detectors so that both photons have the same distance to go. Nothing in what follows depends on how it is done.)

And most of John’s photons never arrived. A beam of light sent to the Moon, however carefully, spreads out on the way, and by the time it got there Albert’s was wider than a town; John’s detector caught only the few photons that happened to land on it. That didn’t matter. Each detector recorded the moment every photon arrived, to a billionth of a second, so the two lists could be matched up afterwards, pair by pair, by time, and every photon whose twin had been lost was simply left out. On the Moon, The Van’s dashboard printed John’s list. At the café, Cal’s detector was wired, for reasons Albert didn’t explain, to the till, which printed Cal’s results on a long curl of receipt paper.

“Both detectors upright,” said Albert’s voice, a second and a quarter after he said it.

John read out the first few of his results. “Yes, no, no, yes, no, yes, yes, yes, no.”

The pause went on so long that he wondered whether the radio had failed. Then Cal said, “Mine are the same.”

“The same?”

“Exactly the same. Every one.”

They turned both detectors to thirty degrees. Then to seventy. Then John turned his to a hundred and fifteen degrees, and Cal turned his to match. At every setting, as long as the two settings were alike, the lists were identical: wherever John’s said YES, so did Cal’s, and wherever John’s said NO, so did Cal’s.

“Gloves,” said Albert’s voice, eventually, and he sounded pleased.

An argument from 1935

Albert wasn’t the first to see gloves in a link like this. In 1935, at Princeton, Einstein and two younger colleagues, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, published a short paper with a question for a title: can the quantum-mechanical description of physical reality be considered complete? Their answer was no, and their argument, usually called EPR after their initials, is a very good one. At first it seems to settle the whole matter.

It starts from what they called a criterion of reality. If, without in any way disturbing something, you can predict with certainty what a measurement on it will show, then the thing measured must have been there to be found. That sounds hard to quarrel with. If you can tell what colour a sock in a drawer is without opening the drawer, the colour was there.

Now apply it to the twins.

  • John, on the Moon, measures his photon at some setting and gets, say, YES.
  • He can now predict, with certainty, that Cal’s photon will give YES at the same setting. It always does.
  • Nothing John does on the Moon can disturb Cal’s photon at the café, at least not in time. They are 384,000 kilometres apart, the two measurements happen together, and nothing travels faster than light, which would need a second and a quarter to get from one to the other.
  • So the answer Cal’s photon gives must have been there all along, before anyone measured anything. Like a glove.

There is a sharper step still. John could have chosen any setting. Whatever he had chosen, his result would have let him predict Cal’s at that setting, with certainty, and without disturbing Cal’s photon at all. So Cal’s photon must carry an answer for every setting at once: for upright, for thirty degrees, for seventy, for all of them, decided before it left the box.

And quantum mechanics describes no such answers. It gives a photon of the pair no polarisation of its own, and its predictions for any one measurement are only chances. So, Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen concluded, quantum mechanics must be incomplete: there is more to a photon than the theory knows, and a better theory would one day describe the answers each photon carries.

(Their own example used the positions and speeds of two particles, not polarisation. The version with polarisation, or with spin, came later, starting with the physicist David Bohm in 1951, and it makes the same argument more cleanly.)

“A very reasonable argument,” said Albert, when John had finished explaining it over the radio, with pauses.

“You sound fond of it,” said Cal.

“I have read it many times.”

That year the physicist Erwin Schrödinger, who agreed that something was deeply odd, gave the link between such particles a name. He called it Verschränkung, which became entanglement in English, and he called it “not one but rather the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics”.

Each list on its own

Cal had been looking at his receipt.

It was long now, and he held it up to the light from the window and read along it: YES, NO, NO, YES, NO, YES, YES, YES, NO, NO, YES, NO, YES. Without John’s list beside it, there was nothing to see. No pattern, no runs longer than you’d expect, no favourite. Very nearly half of his results were YES and half were NO, like the tosses of a fair coin. At every setting he had tried, it was the same: his photons, on their own, answered at random.

He pointed this out to Albert.

“It only looks like chance,” said Albert. “The answers were packed. You simply do not know how.” He polished the brass tube rather harder than it needed. “I have never liked games of chance.”

“We know,” said Cal.

Then John tried something different. He turned his detector a quarter of a turn, ninety degrees, so that it was square to Cal’s. The lists changed at once: now they were exact opposites. Wherever John’s said YES, Cal’s said NO. That made sense too, because a photon polarised along one setting is polarised exactly across the other. It was the gloves again, the left-and-right kind.

He turned it to forty-five degrees from Cal’s. Now the lists matched about half the time and differed about half the time, just as two lists of unrelated coin tosses would.

And in between, the agreement slid smoothly from one to the other. With the settings a little apart, the lists agreed most of the time; further apart, less often; at a right angle, never. It didn’t matter how either detector was set: only the angle between them counted. John drew it on the napkin his doughnut had come in: a curve that started at always, fell through half at forty-five degrees and reached never at ninety. (It is the rule from the sunglasses again, as if each photon met a filter turned the angle between the two settings. The maths page has it.)

Entanglement

The link between particles that share a single quantum state, so that quantum mechanics describes the pair as one thing and gives neither particle a state of its own. Measured separately, each particle’s results are pure chance; compared, the two sets of results are correlated, however far apart they were measured. Albert’s photons are entangled in their polarisation: at the same setting they always agree, and each one alone answers at random at any setting. The name is Erwin Schrödinger’s, from 1935. Whether entangled particles secretly carry answers, as Albert believes, is the next journey’s question.

A message to the café

It was John, naturally, who saw the opportunity.

“Wait,” he said, and then waited two and a half seconds for nobody to reply. “If my results match Cal’s, I can send him a message. Instantly. Faster than the radio.”

“How?” said Cal’s voice, eventually.

“I’ll choose YES for yes and…” John stopped. He couldn’t choose. His results came out of his detector one by one, YES or NO, as they liked, and he could no more make one come out YES than he could make a coin come down heads. Each of his answers was as much a coin toss as each of Cal’s.

“All right,” he said. “I can’t choose my results. But I can choose my setting. Upright for yes, a quarter-turn for no. The lists will match, or they’ll be opposites, and Cal will see which.”

There was a long pause, longer than the radio’s.

“How will I see which?” said Cal. “I can only see my own list.”

It’s the right question. Ask what Cal’s list looks like, on its own, while John turns his detector on the Moon.

Whatever John’s setting, his own result is a coin toss: half YES, half NO. When he gets YES, Cal’s photon will behave at Cal’s detector exactly as if it were polarised along John’s setting: that’s what makes the lists match when the settings match. When John gets NO, Cal’s photon will behave as if it were polarised across John’s setting. (This is only a way of describing how the two lists come out, and later in this journey it turns out to be no more than that. But it gives the right numbers.)

Now put Cal’s detector at any angle to John’s. Half the time, his photon behaves as if polarised along John’s setting, and then it answers YES with some chance: high if the two settings are close, low if they are far apart. Say the settings are thirty degrees apart; then it’s three times in four. The other half of the time, his photon behaves as if polarised across John’s setting, square to the first kind, and then everything is turned round: it answers NO three times in four, and YES only once. Half the time three in four, half the time one in four: on average, YES exactly half the time. It comes out the same at every angle, because the two kinds of photon are opposites: whatever chance of YES one kind has at Cal’s detector, the other kind has exactly that chance of NO. Whatever setting John chooses, Cal’s chance of YES is a half.

And if John chooses nothing, and leaves his detector switched off? Then nobody measures John’s photons at all, and Cal’s chance of YES is still a half, at every setting.

So Cal’s list, on its own, is a fair coin whatever John does: whatever setting he chooses, whenever he changes it, and whether or not he measures at all. Nothing on Cal’s receipt depends on anything that happens on the Moon. John can turn his dial all day and Cal will see exactly what he would have seen anyway.

MESSAGE NOT DELIVERED

The link is real. It shows the moment the two lists are laid side by side. But laying them side by side means bringing one of them to the other, by radio at the speed of light, which takes a second and a quarter, or by carrying it home in The Van, which takes longer. Until then, each side has a list of coin tosses, and nothing more. Whatever the link is, nature has arranged that you can only find out it’s there after an ordinary message has arrived.

The agreement changes with the angle between the settings: it falls, passes a half at forty-five degrees, and reaches nothing at a right angle. And Cal’s own share of YES doesn’t change at all. Try to send him a message: turn John’s dial as often as you like, and switch John’s detector off. Cal’s half stays a half.

Who measured first?

John had a story for it by the time he was back in the car park. He told it at the café table, with the gloves in their boxes between them: he had been too busy on the Moon to open his.

“It’s simple,” he said. “When I measure my photon on the Moon, its answer decides the answer Cal’s photon gives, instantly, nearly four hundred thousand kilometres away. My measurement makes his result. That’s why they match.”

“Or mine makes yours,” said Cal.

“Mine happened first.”

“They happened at the same moment. Albert built a waiting room so that they would.”

“Then mine happened…” John stopped. “At the same moment.”

“By whose clock?” said Cal.

That question had cost them a whole journey once. In When Exactly Is Now?, on the Relativity moon, they found that observers moving differently disagree about which distant events happen at the same moment. Two flashes far enough apart, with too little time between them for light to get from one to the other, have no order that everybody agrees on. One observer says this flash came first, another says that one did, a third says they were simultaneous, and all three are right in their own frames. Every observer agrees about the light cones, though: the events that light from a flash can reach, and the events whose light could have reached it. Only events inside each other’s light cones have an order that everyone agrees on, and only those can be cause and effect.

Teleport to the Relativity moonWhen Exactly Is Now?Why distant events have no order everyone agrees on

The two measurements are that kind of pair. By the café’s clocks they happen at the same moment, 384,000 kilometres apart, and light would need a second and a quarter to get from either one to the other. Each lies outside the other’s light cone. So:

  • by the café’s clocks, and by the Moon’s, John and Cal measured together;
  • a spaceship passing the Earth towards the Moon at half the speed of light, its clocks set as When Exactly Is Now? set them, finds that John measured first, by about three quarters of a second;
  • a spaceship going the other way finds that Cal measured first, by just as much.

Each of those is right. None of them is more right than the others, because, as The Day the Mountain Moved found, no frame of reference is the true one.

Now look at John’s story again. “My measurement makes Cal’s result” means that John’s measurement is the cause and Cal’s result the effect. A cause has to come before its effect, and here, for the spaceship heading towards the Earth, John’s measurement comes after Cal’s. A story in which John’s measurement causes Cal’s result is true only for observers moving one way, and Cal’s version, in which his causes John’s, only for observers moving the other way. What actually causes what can’t depend on who happens to be flying past. So neither story can be the true one. What every observer finds, whichever order they see, is only the correlation itself: at the same setting, the two lists match.

That’s also why it matters that no message can be sent. In When Exactly Is Now?, John and Cal found that a message faster than light would arrive before it was sent, for some observers, and that a reply sent back the same way could arrive before the question was asked. If John could signal Cal by turning his dial, the twins would be just such a message, and the universe would fill with paradoxes. Nature keeps relativity’s order of cause and effect safe here in the only way it could: whatever links the two photons, it can’t carry a message.

One of John’s light lines, going back up and to the left, is also the radio: the earliest moment his list could reach the café.

Then a spaceship flies past. The slider sets its speed, towards the Moon or towards the Earth, and the dashed line through Cal’s measurement is the ship’s “now”: everything on it, its clocks say, happens at the same moment as Cal’s measurement. If John’s dot is below the line, the ship says John measured first; above it, Cal. Sweep the speed one way and back, and watch the order flip, while the light cones stay exactly where they are.

“So there’s no first,” said John.

“Not one that everyone agrees on,” said Cal. “And without a first, there’s no one doing it to the other.”

Albert had been listening from behind the counter. “Someone once called it spooky action at a distance,” he said. “In a letter to a friend.”

“Did he mean it as a compliment?”

“He did not.” Albert set down two coffees. “Which is why the answer is the gloves. No action, no spooks. Everything was packed before the photons left: an answer for every setting, carried in each photon, the same in both. Then nobody needs to have measured first, and nothing needs to travel.”

It’s a fair point, and the right place to be careful. Everything John and Cal saw on the Moon would happen with gloves, if a photon’s gloves could be a little more complicated than a left and a right: an answer for every possible setting, packed in each photon before it left. The lists would match at the same settings, because both photons of a pair would be carrying the same answers. Each list on its own would look like chance, because the answers would be packed at random. Nothing would travel, nobody would go first, and no message could be sent, because Cal’s photons would have been packed before John had chosen anything.

“And the angle?” said Cal. “The way the agreement slides down as the settings move apart?”

“A longer glove,” said Albert. “With a great many fingers.”

Up the road

That evening they drove up the observatory road, which climbs from the turning by the café to the domes of the Teide Observatory, white against the darkening sky. One of them was different from the others. It belongs to the European Space Agency, and inside it is a telescope a metre across, built in the 1990s to talk to satellites with beams of laser light. It’s called the Optical Ground Station, and it has been one end of an experiment very like the one John and Cal had just done.

In 2007 a team of physicists from Austria, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands and Britain, led from Vienna, published the results of an experiment between two islands. On the Roque de los Muchachos, La Palma’s highest mountain, about 2,400 metres up, they built a source of entangled photon pairs very like Albert’s. One photon of each pair they measured there, on La Palma. The other they sent, through a small telescope, 144 kilometres across the sea to the Optical Ground Station on Tenerife, which caught it, at the same height on the far side of the water. On a clear day, from parts of the plateau, La Palma shows on the horizon, and the photons crossed that stretch of sea.

Most of them never arrived, just like John’s. The beam spread out to several metres across by the time it reached Tenerife, sometimes to twenty, and the air took its share, so that typically only about one photon in a thousand was caught. Each station stamped every photon it detected with the time, by clocks kept in step with GPS satellites, and the list from Tenerife was sent to La Palma, by ordinary internet, to be matched with the list there. Twenty to forty pairs a second matched. Their correlations were exactly what quantum mechanics predicts for entangled photons, after 144 kilometres of sea and sky, and they passed a sharper test too, of a kind the next journey explains.

Nothing seems to wear the link out. In 2017 a Chinese satellite, Micius, sent entangled pairs from orbit to two ground stations about 1,200 kilometres apart, and they were still entangled when they arrived.

Then, in 2012, the same island-to-island link was used for something with a better name. A team from the same group in Vienna, with colleagues in Canada, Germany and Norway, sent a photon’s polarisation from the Jacobus Kapteyn Telescope on La Palma to the Optical Ground Station on Tenerife, 143 kilometres, without sending the photon. It’s called quantum teleportation. At the La Palma end, the photon whose polarisation was to be sent was measured together with one photon of an entangled pair, in a single joint measurement, which destroys its polarisation. The result went to Tenerife by an ordinary signal. There, the other photon of the entangled pair was waiting, and depending on the result, it was either left alone or given a particular twist. Afterwards it had exactly the polarisation the first photon had had, and the first photon no longer had it.

Nothing went faster than light, because nothing could be done in Tenerife until the ordinary signal had arrived. Until then the waiting photon was a coin toss, like Cal’s. And nothing was copied: the original’s polarisation was destroyed in the sending, and appeared only in the photon at the other end.

“Can they teleport doughnuts?” said Cal.

“Only the state of a doughnut,” said Albert, “and only by destroying the original.”

“Then I’ll eat the original.”

Back to Teide

By the time they came back down to the café the stars were out, and the Moon had long since set. The box on the tripod was dark, and its two tubes pointed at nothing in particular.

The doughnut boxes were still on the table where they’d left them, both unopened. John opened his. It held a left glove.

Cal opened his. It held a right.

“Gloves,” said Albert, with satisfaction, collecting the boxes. “Always gloves.”

Cal didn’t answer straight away. He was thinking about the curve on John’s napkin, the agreement sliding down from always to never as the settings turned apart.

“Could anyone ever tell the difference?” he said. “Between gloves with a great many fingers, and whatever else it might be?”

Albert stopped with the boxes in his hands. For a moment he looked almost pleased to be asked.

“A man named Bell thought so,” he said. “In 1964.” He put the boxes on the counter, next to the upturned glass and its grain of sugar. “A friend of mine is coming for tea in the morning. He has never agreed with me about the gloves.” He smiled. “Perhaps we should make it interesting.”

Whether a pair of gloves could explain everything they had seen was a question for the next journey.
