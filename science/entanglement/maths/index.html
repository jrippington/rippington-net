The angle and the agreement

The pair’s state. Write ∣H⟩|H\rangle∣H⟩ for a photon polarised horizontally and ∣V⟩|V\rangle∣V⟩ for one polarised vertically. Albert’s twins are made in the state

∣Φ+⟩=12(∣H⟩J∣H⟩C+∣V⟩J∣V⟩C),|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(|H\rangle_J |H\rangle_C + |V\rangle_J |V\rangle_C\Big),∣Φ+⟩=2​1​(∣H⟩J​∣H⟩C​+∣V⟩J​∣V⟩C​),

where JJJ labels John’s photon and CCC Cal’s: both horizontal and both vertical, added as amplitudes. Neither photon has a state of its own. (Real pair-making crystals make this state, or close cousins of it, such as the one in which the twins always disagree at the same setting, like a left glove and a right: the 2007 experiment between La Palma and Tenerife used that one.)

A detector at an angle. A detector whose crystal is set at angle aaa from upright sends a photon polarised along aaa straight on (YES) and one polarised across it turned aside (NO). Those two polarisations are

∣a⟩=cos⁡a ∣H⟩+sin⁡a ∣V⟩,∣a⊥⟩=−sin⁡a ∣H⟩+cos⁡a ∣V⟩.|a\rangle = \cos a\,|H\rangle + \sin a\,|V\rangle, \qquad |a_\perp\rangle = -\sin a\,|H\rangle + \cos a\,|V\rangle .∣a⟩=cosa∣H⟩+sina∣V⟩,∣a⊥​⟩=−sina∣H⟩+cosa∣V⟩.

The four outcomes. With John’s detector at aaa and Cal’s at bbb, the amplitude for both to say YES is

(⟨a∣J⟨b∣C)∣Φ+⟩=cos⁡acos⁡b+sin⁡asin⁡b2=cos⁡(a−b)2,\big(\langle a|_J \langle b|_C\big)|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{\cos a \cos b + \sin a \sin b}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\cos(a - b)}{\sqrt{2}},(⟨a∣J​⟨b∣C​)∣Φ+⟩=2​cosacosb+sinasinb​=2​cos(a−b)​,

and the others follow in the same way:

P(YES,YES)=P(NO,NO)=12cos⁡2(a−b),P(YES,NO)=P(NO,YES)=12sin⁡2(a−b).P(\text{YES},\text{YES}) = P(\text{NO},\text{NO}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cos^2(a - b), \qquad P(\text{YES},\text{NO}) = P(\text{NO},\text{YES}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sin^2(a - b).P(YES,YES)=P(NO,NO)=21​cos2(a−b),P(YES,NO)=P(NO,YES)=21​sin2(a−b).

So the chance that the two results agree is

P(agree)=cos⁡2(a−b),P(\text{agree}) = \cos^2(a - b),P(agree)=cos2(a−b),

which depends only on the angle between the two settings. At the same setting it is 1, whatever the setting: the gloves. At right angles it is 0: always opposite. In between:

Angle between the settings0°30°45°60°90°
Chance the two agree10.750.50.250

It’s Malus’s law, the sunglasses’ rule of the tenth journey, cos⁡2\cos^2cos2 of the angle, as though one photon met a filter turned the angle between the two settings.

Why each side alone sees chance

Cal’s list on its own. The chance that Cal’s detector says YES, whatever John’s says, is

PC(YES)=P(YES,YES)+P(NO,YES)=12cos⁡2(a−b)+12sin⁡2(a−b)=12,P_C(\text{YES}) = P(\text{YES},\text{YES}) + P(\text{NO},\text{YES}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cos^2(a - b) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sin^2(a - b) = \tfrac{1}{2},PC​(YES)=P(YES,YES)+P(NO,YES)=21​cos2(a−b)+21​sin2(a−b)=21​,

for every aaa and every bbb. John’s setting has dropped out entirely.

The same, told from John’s side. If John measures first and gets YES, Cal’s photon is left polarised along aaa, and passes Cal’s detector with chance cos⁡2(a−b)\cos^2(a - b)cos2(a−b); if John gets NO, Cal’s photon is left polarised along a⊥a_\perpa⊥​, and passes with chance sin⁡2(a−b)\sin^2(a - b)sin2(a−b). Each happens half the time, so Cal’s chance is 12cos⁡2(a−b)+12sin⁡2(a−b)=12\tfrac{1}{2}\cos^2(a-b) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sin^2(a-b) = \tfrac{1}{2}21​cos2(a−b)+21​sin2(a−b)=21​, the same sum. With the settings 30° apart, that’s half of 34\tfrac3443​ and half of 14\tfrac1441​: a half.

If John measures nothing. Cal’s photon on its own is described by the pair’s state with John’s photon left out (its reduced density matrix):

ρC=Tr⁡J∣Φ+⟩⟨Φ+∣=12(∣H⟩⟨H∣+∣V⟩⟨V∣)=12 1,\rho_C = \operatorname{Tr}_J |\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+| = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(|H\rangle\langle H| + |V\rangle\langle V|\big) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{1},ρC​=TrJ​∣Φ+⟩⟨Φ+∣=21​(∣H⟩⟨H∣+∣V⟩⟨V∣)=21​1,

unpolarised light, which passes a polariser at any angle half the time: ⟨b∣ρC∣b⟩=12\langle b|\rho_C|b\rangle = \tfrac12⟨b∣ρC​∣b⟩=21​.

Why no message can be sent. Everything Cal can measure on his own photon is fixed by ρC\rho_CρC​, and nothing John does to his own photon, measuring it at any setting, or not at all, changes ρC\rho_CρC​. (Measuring at aaa and averaging over the two results gives 12∣a⟩⟨a∣+12∣a⊥⟩⟨a⊥∣=121\tfrac12|a\rangle\langle a| + \tfrac12|a_\perp\rangle\langle a_\perp| = \tfrac12\mathbb{1}21​∣a⟩⟨a∣+21​∣a⊥​⟩⟨a⊥​∣=21​1 again.) This is the no-signalling theorem: in quantum mechanics, operations on one particle of an entangled pair never change the statistics of measurements on the other. The correlation appears only when the two lists are compared, and one list has to be carried to the other, by radio at the speed of light, or slower.

Who measured first

The events. Put the café at x=0x = 0x=0 and the Moon at x=Lx = Lx=L, where

1.155×0.5×1.282 s≈0.74 s.1.155 \times 0.5 \times 1.282\ \text{s} \approx 0.74\ \text{s}.1.155×0.5×1.282 s≈0.74 s.

For a ship going the other way, β<0\beta < 0β<0, and Cal’s comes first by the same amount. For any speed at all, the order is one or the other, or (at rest) neither.

The radio. The soonest John’s list could reach the café is along his light cone’s edge: it arrives at t=2L/c≈2.56t = 2L/c \approx 2.56t=2L/c≈2.56 s, a second and a quarter after both measurements, which is when the two lists can first be compared.
