Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 13 of 14

The mysterious ringing

The evening after their simulated visit to a black hole, John and Cal were sitting in The Van in the café car park, with the doors shut and the simulator running.

Albert had sent them out there with two coffees, a memory stick and an instruction to listen carefully. He himself had stayed behind the counter and joined them by video link, which he said was so that he could keep an eye on the doughnuts. The café was about twenty metres away. The simulator had turned The Van’s windows into screens full of stars, so the only sign of the café was Albert’s face on the dashboard, slightly too close to his camera.

There was a knocking noise inside The Van. It came in short, irregular pulses, each slightly faster than the last.

“Exhaust manifold,” John said confidently.

“This doesn’t have an exhaust manifold,” said Cal.

“That’s probably why it’s broken.”

The knocking quickened. A light on the dashboard came on and stayed on.

CHECK SUSPENSION

Cal checked the sensors instead. “It’s not actually a sound. The computer is converting a signal into audio.”

On the screen, Albert leaned closer to his camera and studied the trace the signal was drawing: a wavy line whose wiggles grew taller and more crowded towards the right, faster and faster, until they stopped.

“Ah,” he said. “A chirp.”

“It’s not a bird,” John said.

“Not that sort.”

Outside the windows, the simulated stars seemed perfectly still. The signal kept rising in pitch and intensity, then vanished.

“That was slowed down,” said Albert. “Here it is at the speed it arrived.”

This time there was no knocking at all: just a low thump that swept upwards into a quick, rising whoop, and silence. The whole thing was over in about a fifth of a second.

The universe had rung, and the instrument had listened.

“What was it?” Cal asked.

“Two black holes,” said Albert, “becoming one.”

John looked at the windows, at the steady simulated stars. “But we didn’t see anything. And there’s nothing out there to carry a sound. Space hasn’t any air.”

“Quite right,” said Albert. “So what do you suppose was waving?”

What could be waving?

Everyday life is full of waves, and nearly all of them are a disturbance in some stuff. Drop a stone into a pond and a ring of ripples spreads outwards; the water itself only bobs up and down, but the pattern travels across the surface. Speak, and you set the air near your mouth alternately squeezing and stretching, and that pattern of squeezes travels through the air to someone’s ear. In each case there is a material, the water or the air, and the wave is a pattern moving through it. The second journey gave that material a name, the medium, and found that light is the odd one out: it needs no medium at all. Light is a pattern of electric and magnetic fields, and it crosses empty space perfectly happily.

So it’s natural to assume that anything you could call a wave must be either a disturbance of some material or, like light, a disturbance of some field that fills space. A gravitational wave is neither: it is a ripple in space and time themselves, in the distances between things and in the rates of the clocks beside them.

That sounds like a very strange idea until you remember where the last few journeys have taken us. In the tenth journey we found that gravity isn’t a force reaching across space at all, but the shape of spacetime: mass and energy curve it, and things moving freely follow the straightest paths that its curved shape allows. The Earth bends spacetime around itself, and the Moon, following the straightest path it can, goes round and round.

Why gravity can’t change instantly

Now ask a question that Newton’s gravity never had to face. What happens to that curved shape when the mass that makes it moves?

Newton’s law of gravity says that every mass pulls on every other mass instantly, however far away. If the Sun vanished, the Earth would fly off in a straight line at that very instant. But the special relativity journeys taught us that no message, and no cause, can travel faster than light: the speed of light is built into the shape of spacetime itself, as the boundary of what can affect what (the light cone of the fourth journey). So in relativity the news that the Sun had gone could only spread outwards at the speed of light. The Earth would carry on going round the empty place where the Sun had been for about eight minutes and twenty seconds, which is how long light takes to reach us from the Sun. It would leave its orbit at the same moment that the sunlight went out.

Think of a boat sitting in a calm lake. The water around it has a shape: it is pushed down a little where the boat sits. Move the boat, and the water near it changes shape at once, but the water far away hasn’t heard the news yet. The change spreads out from the boat as a wake. A mass moving through spacetime is a little like that. Its curvature is reshaped near it first, and the reshaping spreads outwards at the speed of light, as a ripple.

The analogy has a limit: the lake is made of water, and a wake is a disturbance in that water, while spacetime isn’t made of anything. A gravitational wave is a change in the geometry itself, in how far apart things are and how fast their clocks run.

Gravitational wave

A ripple in the shape of spacetime itself, spreading outwards from moving masses at the speed of light. It doesn’t travel through any material. As it passes, it stretches and squeezes the distances between objects floating freely, very slightly, first one way and then the other. Einstein’s general theory of relativity predicted gravitational waves in 1916; they were first detected directly in 2015.

What makes the waves

Not every motion makes gravitational waves. A planet moving steadily in a straight line doesn’t: that’s just a planet at rest seen from a different frame, and the first journey showed us that steady motion makes no difference to anything. A perfectly round star swelling and shrinking evenly doesn’t either, because outside it, its gravity is exactly the same whatever its size. It takes something lopsided and changing: above all, two heavy things whirling round each other. As a pair turns, the shape of the gravity around it turns too, and every half orbit the arrangement looks the same again, so the waves go out at twice the pair’s orbital rate.

The waves carry energy away, and they are astonishingly feeble. Spacetime is very stiff: it takes enormous masses moving at enormous speeds to ripple it noticeably. The Earth going round the Sun does make gravitational waves, but their total power is about 200 watts, roughly that of a couple of old-fashioned light bulbs. Losing energy at that rate, the Earth would take millions of millions of times the present age of the universe to spiral into the Sun.

Albert adjusted the simulator. Two dark objects appeared against the stars, circling each other, the starlight behind them smeared into arcs by their bending of light (you met that in the eleventh journey).

“Two black holes,” he said, “each about thirty times the mass of the Sun, in orbit around each other.”

As they watched, the two drew slowly closer, and as they came closer they went round faster. Each orbit sent out a ripple; each ripple carried away a little energy; with less energy, the pair sank a little closer together, and went round faster still. Faster orbits meant stronger waves and a higher note. The simulator turned the ripples into sound, and the knocking from before began again, quickening, until the pulses ran together into a rising tone.

That runaway is the chirp. In the last fraction of a second the two black holes were going round each other more than a hundred times a second, at a good fraction of the speed of light. Then they touched, merged, and became a single black hole, lopsided for an instant, which shuddered and settled into a smooth, quiet shape. Its last ripples died away like the note of a bell after it has been struck. Astronomers really do call this last part the ringdown.

John watched the animation. “If spacetime can bend and clocks can disagree, I suppose it can also vibrate.”

“Ripple,” said Cal. “Vibrate sounds as though it’s a guitar string.”

Albert shrugged. “The mathematics is more interesting than the metaphor.”

Even before anyone detected a gravitational wave directly, there was strong evidence that they exist. In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor found a pulsar, a collapsed star spinning on its axis and sweeping a beam of radio waves past the Earth like a lighthouse, in orbit around another collapsed star. A pulsar’s flashes arrive as regularly as an excellent clock, so the pair’s orbit could be timed very precisely. Over the following years the orbit was found to be shrinking, the two stars spiralling slowly together, at just the rate general relativity predicts for a pair losing energy to gravitational waves. Hulse and Taylor were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1993. But that was evidence that the waves were leaving. Nobody had yet caught one arriving.

Stretch one way, squeeze the other

“So when a wave like that goes past,” said Cal, “what does it actually do?”

“Try it and see,” said Albert. “Put some screws in the cabin.”

The Van had never been short of loose screws. It produced them at a steady rate, in the way that some trees produce leaves, and Cal kept a jar of them under the passenger seat in case anyone ever discovered where they came from. The simulator had its own copy of The Van’s cabin, drifting in empty space with its engines off, and its own copy of the jar.

Cal set sixteen simulated screws floating in a ring about a metre across, and a seventeenth at its centre. Nothing touched or pushed them, so they simply hung there, as the screw in the very first journey had. Each one was in free fall, in the sense of the eighth journey: moving under gravity alone, with no floor, string or hand to push it anywhere. A free-falling screw is about the simplest instrument there is. It does nothing but follow the shape of spacetime around it, which is why physicists call objects like this test masses.

Then Albert sent a gravitational wave through the simulated cabin, aimed straight at the screen, so that it would pass through the ring face on.

The ring became an oval, stretched from side to side and squeezed from top to bottom. A moment later it was round again. Then it was an oval the other way, stretched from top to bottom and squeezed from side to side, then round again, then side to side, over and over, for as long as the wave kept coming.

“Something’s pushing them,” said John.

“Is it?” said Albert. “Fix one of your accelerometers to any screw you like.”

Cal did. The little spring instrument from the first journey read zero, on every screw, the whole time. Not one of them was being pushed. What was changing was the distance between them. If Cal had timed a flash of light going from the centre screw to one of the outer ones and back again, the time would have grown and shrunk with the wave, because there was more distance to cross and then less.

You have met this kind of effect before, in a gentler form. In the eighth journey you met tidal effects: a large enough room falling freely towards the Earth is very slightly stretched from top to bottom and squeezed from side to side, because the Earth’s gravity is a little stronger at its floor than at its ceiling, and points a little inwards at its sides. A gravitational wave is a tidal stretch and squeeze that travels, and that keeps changing direction as it passes.

Which way does the wave stretch?

All the stretching and squeezing happens across the wave’s direction of travel. In the simulation the wave came straight out of the screen, and the ring changed shape in the plane of the screen; nothing at all happened along the wave’s own path. That is like a wave on a skipping rope, where the rope moves up and down while the wave runs along it, rather than like sound, where the air squeezes and stretches along the direction the sound is going.

There are also two basic patterns. In one, called plus, the stretching and squeezing run side to side and top to bottom, like the arms of a + sign. In the other, called cross, they run along the diagonals, like the arms of a ×. They are the same pattern turned through 45 degrees, and any gravitational wave is some mixture of the two, depending on how its source is oriented as seen from where you are.

How much a wave stretches and squeezes is described by a single number.

Strain

How much a length changes, as a fraction of the length itself: the change divided by the original length. A metre-long rod stretched by a millimetre has a strain of 0.001. A gravitational wave’s strain says by what fraction it stretches and squeezes the distances it passes through, and the fraction is the same for every distance it crosses: short or long, each changes by the same proportion. That is why the longest instruments see the largest changes.

The figure below shows Cal’s ring of screws face on, with a gravitational wave coming straight towards you, out of the screen. Two of the outer screws, a quarter of the way round the ring from each other, are joined to the centre screw by lines: call them arm 1 and arm 2. The dashed circle shows where the screws sit with no wave passing. Beside the ring is a record of how much each arm’s length changes through one complete cycle of the wave, with a marker showing where in the cycle you are. The stretch is magnified enormously, so that you can see it at all; the table at the bottom tells you by how much, and gives each arm’s change.

Play the wave, or pause it and step through its cycle a little at a time. Watch the two arms: as one stretches, the other squeezes, by exactly the same amount. Now switch to the cross wave. The ring still changes shape, but the two arms swing a little and don’t change length at all, because this wave stretches along the diagonals. An instrument that measured only along arm 1 and arm 2 would feel nothing from it. Turn the arms through 45 degrees, and they feel the cross wave fully, and the plus wave not at all. Finally, look at the dashed line on the record: the difference between the two arms’ lengths. It swings twice as far as either arm alone, and it is what a real detector measures.

How small?

The figure needs every bit of that magnification. A strong gravitational wave reaching the Earth from a distant merger has a strain of about 10⁻²¹: it changes lengths by about one part in a thousand billion billion. If such a wave stretched the distance between the Earth and the Sun, the 150 million kilometres would change by about the width of a single atom. The whole diameter of the Earth would change by about the width of an atomic nucleus. Cal’s metre-wide ring of screws would change by less than a millionth of the width of a proton.

Why so small? Part of the reason is distance. The waves from the merger Albert had played set out from about 1.3 billion light-years away, and like the ripples on a pond they weaken as they spread. But the main reason is that spacetime is so stiff. That merger turned about three times the mass of the Sun into gravitational-wave energy, by Einstein’s E = mc² from the seventh journey, in a fraction of a second. For that fraction of a second it was giving out energy faster than all the stars in the visible universe put together. And when the ripples reached the Earth, they moved things by less than a proton’s width.

“This is absurd,” John said.

“Absurdly ingenious,” said Cal. “If anyone measured it.”

Albert smiled. “Come and have a doughnut.”

How can anyone measure that?

They crossed the car park to the café. Albert had cleared one end of the counter and set out, beside a plate of lemon doughnuts, a small and rather precise arrangement: a laser pointer clamped to a block, a square of glass standing at an angle in front of it, two small mirrors on blocks at right angles to each other, a hand’s width away, and a white card propped against the sugar bowl. On the card was a pattern of light and dark stripes.

Cal put down his coffee cup. The stripes jumped.

John leaned on the counter to look more closely. The stripes slid sideways, and slid back when he stood up.

A lorry went past on the observatory road. The stripes shivered.

“It is listening to everything,” said Albert. “That is the difficult part.”

Cal moved the plate of doughnuts to a safer distance.

Two waves meeting

Albert’s arrangement is an interferometer, and to see why its stripes are so jumpy we need one more fact about waves: what happens when two of them meet.

When two sets of ripples cross on a pond, they pass straight through each other, and where they overlap, their heights simply add. Where a crest of one meets a crest of the other, the water rises twice as high. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel, and the water there stays almost flat. Noise-cancelling headphones work this way. A microphone listens to the noise around you, and the headphones play a sound wave that is the noise’s mirror image, a trough wherever the noise has a crest. The two cancel in your ear, and the drone of the aeroplane fades away.

Light is a wave too, so two beams of light can do the same thing. Where their crests coincide, you get brighter light. Where crest meets trough, the light cancels, and you get darkness.

Albert’s square of glass is a beam splitter: a mirror so thinly coated that it reflects half of the light that falls on it and lets the other half through. It splits the laser beam into two, sending the halves off at right angles, along two arms, to the two small mirrors. Each half bounces back, and the splitter brings them together again on their way to the card. Whether the card is bright or dark depends on whether the two returning waves arrive in step, crest with crest, or out of step, crest with trough. And that depends on the difference between the two arms’ lengths. (Albert’s beams meet at a very slight angle, so across the card they drift in and out of step, and you see stripes: bright where they are in step, dark where they are not.)

Move one mirror away by a quarter of a wavelength of the light, and that half of the beam has an extra half wavelength to travel, a quarter on the way out and a quarter on the way back. Crests now arrive where troughs used to be: every bright stripe turns dark, and every dark one bright. The red light of a laser pointer has a wavelength of about 650 nanometres, so a quarter of a wavelength is about 160 nanometres, hundreds of times thinner than a human hair. When Cal put his cup down, the counter flexed by about that much, and the stripes jumped.

Interferometer

An instrument that measures tiny changes in distance using light. A beam is split in two, the halves travel along different paths, and they are brought back together. Where the returning waves meet crest to crest they add up to bright light; where crest meets trough they cancel to darkness. A change in one path of a small fraction of the light’s wavelength changes the brightness, so an interferometer can detect movements far smaller than anything you could see.

This kind of instrument may ring a bell. Albert Michelson and Edward Morley used one in 1887 to look for the ether wind of the second journey; they found none, and helped start the whole of relativity.

Why the detector is L-shaped

Now put the interferometer together with Cal’s ring of screws. The beam splitter is the centre screw. The two end mirrors are two of the outer screws, a quarter of the way round the ring from each other. The two arms of the interferometer are arm 1 and arm 2 from the figure. A gravitational wave lined up with them stretches one arm while it squeezes the other. And what an interferometer measures is the difference between the arms’ lengths, which changes twice as much as either.

Better still, anything that changes both arms equally leaves the difference, and the light at the detector, untouched. If the whole building warms up a little and both arms grow by the same amount, the two returning waves keep in step with each other just as before. If the laser’s colour wavers, both halves of the beam waver together. An interferometer is naturally deaf to many of the disturbances that would ruin any other ruler, and especially alert to the one pattern a gravitational wave makes.

The real detectors are set up to run dark. Their arms are adjusted so that the two returning beams arrive exactly out of step at the detector, crest on trough, and cancel completely. Nothing reaches it. Any light at all means that the difference between the arms has changed.

Below is an L-shaped interferometer seen from above, with what reaches its detector: the wave coming back from each arm, the two together, and a record of how bright the detector has been over the last few seconds. The table at the bottom says what is disturbing it, and how much light reaches the detector. Everything here is enormously magnified: the arms’ changes are shown as a few hundredths of a wavelength of the light, where a real wave changes them by a few trillionths.

With the gravitational wave passing, watch arm 1 lengthen while arm 2 shortens: the two returning waves slide out of their perfect cancelling, and the detector flickers in time with the wave. Switch the wave off, and the detector goes dark. Then warm the building. Both mirrors move outwards together, often by more than the wave ever moved them, and yet the detector stays dark: the two returning waves move together and still cancel. Last, bump one mirror. The detector flickers, just as it would for a gravitational wave. That is the real difficulty.

LIGO

The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory, LIGO, is two of these instruments in the United States, one at Hanford in Washington State and one at Livingston in Louisiana, 3,002 kilometres apart. Each is an L with arms 4 kilometres long, and the light travels inside steel tubes pumped almost empty of air, so that not even drifting air can disturb the beams. Extra mirrors keep the light bouncing back and forth along each arm about 300 times before it returns, so it travels about 1,200 kilometres in each arm instead of 4. The laser is infrared, with a wavelength of 1,064 nanometres.

The end mirrors are the test masses: cylinders of extremely pure glass weighing 40 kilograms each, the heirs of Cal’s floating screws. To let them behave as though they were floating freely in space, each hangs at the bottom of a chain of pendulums, which hangs in turn from a platform steadied against the ground’s tremors in the way noise-cancelling headphones deal with an aeroplane’s drone: sensors feel the ground move, and the platform is moved to cancel it. Any vibration reaching the top of a pendulum chain is about a hundred million times smaller by the time it reaches the mirror. Cal thought of The Van’s dashboard, which at the first sound of the chirp had flashed:

CHECK SUSPENSION

For once, he decided, it might have had a point.

Even so, a single detector can’t tell a gravitational wave from a lorry, a small earthquake or a bump. That is why there are two, thousands of kilometres apart. A lorry in Louisiana does nothing to the mirrors in Washington. A gravitational wave, crossing the Earth at the speed of light, passes through both, no more than about ten milliseconds apart, and makes the same pattern in each.

What arrived on 14 September 2015

“The chirp you heard,” said Albert, “was recorded by those two instruments, on a Monday morning in September 2015.”

At 09:50:45 universal time on 14 September 2015, just before five in the morning in Louisiana, a signal swept through the Livingston detector, and seven milliseconds later through the one at Hanford. The detectors had only just been switched back on after a major upgrade. In about a fifth of a second, the signal rose from about 35 to about 250 cycles a second, growing stronger as it rose, then died away: the chirp and ringdown that general relativity predicts for two black holes spiralling together and merging. Its peak strain was about one part in a thousand billion billion.

Its frequencies happened to fall within the range of human hearing, which is why The Van’s computer could turn it into sound simply by sending it to a loudspeaker. The whoop Cal and John heard was the real signal at its real speed.

From the shape of the chirp, the LIGO scientists worked out what had made it: two black holes, about 36 and 29 times the mass of the Sun, which had merged into one of about 62. The missing three Suns’ worth of mass had left as gravitational waves. The collision had happened about 1.3 billion years ago, and the ripples had been on their way ever since: for longer than there have been animals on Earth. The scientists spent five months checking that it was real before they announced it, on 11 February 2016. In 2017 Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne and Barry Barish were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for their parts in building LIGO and making the detection.

John looked at Albert’s laser, and the stripes on the card, and the plate of doughnuts at a respectful distance.

“A ruler,” he said, “four kilometres long, that measures a change smaller than a proton.”

Albert looked delighted. “You see why I enjoy experimentalists.”

What gravitational waves tell us

Back in The Van, Albert’s face reappeared on the dashboard. “There is another recording,” he said. “Listen to this one for longer.”

This chirp went on and on, a thin rising whine, for more than a minute and a half, climbing slowly and then faster and faster until it ended in a rush.

The difference was mass. Black holes of thirty Suns apiece spiral together quickly, and their chirp is short and low. Lighter objects take longer to spiral in, and their chirp is longer and climbs higher before the end. This one came from two neutron stars: the collapsed cores of stars that were once much more massive than the Sun, each squeezed to about the size of a city, so dense that a teaspoonful of one would weigh about a billion tonnes on Earth.

On 17 August 2017, the two LIGO detectors and Virgo, a European detector near Pisa in Italy, recorded about a hundred seconds of this signal as two neutron stars, each between about 1.1 and 1.6 times the mass of the Sun, spiralled together and collided. About 1.7 seconds after the gravitational waves ended, NASA’s Fermi space telescope recorded a short burst of gamma rays from the same part of the sky. The three gravitational-wave detectors had narrowed down where the waves came from, and in less than twelve hours telescopes in Chile had found a new point of light in a galaxy called NGC 4993, about 130 million light-years away. Over the following weeks about seventy observatories, on the ground and in space, watched the same spot in every kind of light from gamma rays to radio waves.

They saw the glowing wreckage of the collision, a kilonova, shining with the heat of freshly made, radioactive heavy elements. Collisions like this one are now thought to make much of the universe’s gold and platinum.

The timing told its own story. The gravitational waves and the gamma rays had travelled side by side for 130 million years and arrived within two seconds of each other, the gamma rays having set off a moment after the collision. So gravitational waves travel at the speed of light, to within about one part in a thousand million million. It was the first precise measurement of the speed of gravity, and it came out where relativity said it would.

Multi-messenger astronomy

Studying a single event in the universe with more than one kind of messenger: light of any kind, from radio waves to gamma rays; gravitational waves; and the ghostly particles called neutrinos. Each messenger carries different information. The first event seen in both gravitational waves and light was the collision of two neutron stars detected on 17 August 2017.

Gravitational waves carry news that light often can’t. A pair of black holes gives out no light at all, so until 2015 nobody knew for certain that pairs of black holes thirty times the Sun’s mass existed, let alone that they collide. The shape of each chirp gives the masses of the objects that made it, and how fast they were spinning. And the last fraction of a second, when two black holes merge, is general relativity at its most extreme, a place no telescope can see into; so far, every signal has matched Einstein’s equations. The LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA detectors (KAGRA is in Japan) have now caught hundreds of signals, almost all of them from black holes merging. The clearest yet, recorded in January 2025, showed that the area of the final black hole’s event horizon (the boundary of the twelfth journey) was larger than the two original horizons put together, as Stephen Hawking had predicted in 1971 it must be.

John watched the final waveform fade. “First we had the stars’ light. Now we can measure a distant catastrophe shaking spacetime.”

“Welcome,” Cal said, “to the most improbable listening device ever built.”

Back to Teide

The Van’s speakers fell silent, and the simulated stars faded from its windows. Outside were the real ones.

They walked out into the car park beneath a sky alive with stars. The mountain was quiet. The air was still, and so clear that the Milky Way looked close enough to touch. The café’s last customers had left, and Albert was bringing in the chairs.

John looked up. “There may be black holes merging somewhere tonight. We don’t hear them with our ears. But their waves could cross this very place long after the merger happened.”

“Some of them are crossing it now,” said Albert, stacking a chair. “Passing through the mountain, and the café, and you. Too faint to notice, most of them. But there.”

John found himself standing a little more carefully, as if he might feel it.

Cal nodded. “And the universe has become a little less silent.”

For most of history, nearly everything anyone knew about the sky had arrived as light. Now there was a second way of finding out, more like listening than looking. The bell had been ringing for a very long time, and it had only just been heard.

Albert switched off the café light. For a moment, the three of them stood together under the darkening sky.

“We’ve learned how space and time behave,” John said. “Now perhaps we should ask what the universe is doing as a whole.”

“An interesting question,” said Albert. “Especially because it is not staying the same size.”
