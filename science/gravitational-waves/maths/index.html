The Earth’s gravitational waves

Two masses m1m_1m1​ and m2m_2m2​ going round each other in a circle, a distance rrr apart, give out gravitational waves with a power of

P=325 G4c5 (m1m2)2 (m1+m2)r5,P = \frac{32}{5}\,\frac{G^4}{c^5}\,\frac{(m_1 m_2)^2\,(m_1 + m_2)}{r^5},P=532​c5G4​r5(m1​m2​)2(m1​+m2​)​,

where GGG is Newton’s gravitational constant and ccc the speed of light. (It is general relativity’s answer for a pair in a circular orbit, as Philip Peters and Jon Mathews worked it out in 1963.) For the Sun and the Earth, m1=1.989×1030m_1 = 1.989 \times 10^{30}m1​=1.989×1030 kilograms, m2=5.972×1024m_2 = 5.972 \times 10^{24}m2​=5.972×1024 kilograms and r=1.496×1011r = 1.496 \times 10^{11}r=1.496×1011 metres; with G=6.674×10−11G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}G=6.674×10−11 and c=2.998×108c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}c=2.998×108, all in metres, kilograms and seconds, P≈196P \approx 196P≈196 watts.

Losing energy at that rate, the orbit shrinks, and the time to spiral all the way in is

T=5256 c5 r4G3 m1m2 (m1+m2)≈3.4×1030 seconds,T = \frac{5}{256}\,\frac{c^5\,r^4}{G^3\,m_1 m_2\,(m_1 + m_2)} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{30}\ \text{seconds},T=2565​G3m1​m2​(m1​+m2​)c5r4​≈3.4×1030 seconds,

about 102310^{23}1023 years: nearly 8 million million times the universe’s age of 13.8 billion years.

How big is a ripple?

Strain is a fraction, so it converts between lengths easily. For a length LLL and a strain hhh, the change in length is roughly

ΔL≈h L.\Delta L \approx h\,L.ΔL≈hL.

With h=10−21h = 10^{-21}h=10−21, the distance from the Earth to the Sun, 1.5×10111.5 \times 10^{11}1.5×1011 metres, changes by about 1.5×10−101.5 \times 10^{-10}1.5×10−10 metres, about the width of an atom; the Earth’s diameter, 1.27×1071.27 \times 10^{7}1.27×107 metres, by about 1.3×10−141.3 \times 10^{-14}1.3×10−14 metres, about the width of a large atomic nucleus; and Cal’s metre-wide ring by 10−2110^{-21}10−21 metres, about 6×10−76 \times 10^{-7}6×10−7 of a proton’s width.

Strictly, a wave of strain hhh lined up with an interferometer’s arms lengthens one arm by 12hL\tfrac{1}{2}hL21​hL and shortens the other by 12hL\tfrac{1}{2}hL21​hL, so the difference between them changes by hLhLhL. For GW150914, as the first signal is known, with h≈10−21h \approx 10^{-21}h≈10−21 and arms of 4 kilometres:

ΔL≈10−21×4000 m=4×10−18 m.\Delta L \approx 10^{-21} \times 4000\ \text{m} = 4 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{m}.ΔL≈10−21×4000 m=4×10−18 m.

A proton is about 1.7×10−151.7 \times 10^{-15}1.7×10−15 metres across, so this is about one four-hundredth of a proton’s width. Compared with the wavelength of LIGO’s laser light, 1.064×10−61.064 \times 10^{-6}1.064×10−6 metres, it is about 4×10−124 \times 10^{-12}4×10−12 of a wavelength.

How does that change the light at the detector? A change ΔL\Delta LΔL in the difference between the arms changes the difference in the round trips by 2ΔL2\Delta L2ΔL, and shifts the two returning waves relative to each other by 2ΔL/λ2\Delta L / \lambda2ΔL/λ of a wave. With the arms set so that, undisturbed, the waves cancel, the fraction of the full brightness that reaches the detector is

PPmax⁡=sin⁡2 ⁣(2π ΔLλ),\frac{P}{P_{\max}} = \sin^2\!\left(\frac{2\pi\,\Delta L}{\lambda}\right),Pmax​P​=sin2(λ2πΔL​),

which is the formula behind the article’s second figure. Moving one mirror by a quarter of a wavelength, ΔL=λ/4\Delta L = \lambda/4ΔL=λ/4, makes the angle π/2\pi/2π/2 and the detector fully bright: for the red light of Albert’s laser pointer, λ≈650\lambda \approx 650λ≈650 nanometres, that’s about 160 nanometres, which is why his stripes jumped. For a real wave the angle is so small that the light reaching the detector is minute; the 300 bounces along each arm multiply the shift about 300 times, and a great deal of further ingenuity, in the lasers, the mirrors and the way the output is read, makes it measurable. (The real detectors run very slightly off perfect darkness, which makes the signal grow in step with the strain rather than with its square. The principle is the same.)

The speed of gravity

The gravitational waves and the gamma rays from the neutron stars’ collision crossed about 130 million light-years, which takes light about 130 million years: 1.3×108×3.16×107≈4.1×10151.3 \times 10^{8} \times 3.16 \times 10^{7} \approx 4.1 \times 10^{15}1.3×108×3.16×107≈4.1×1015 seconds. They arrived 1.7 seconds apart. Even if the whole 1.7 seconds were a difference in speed, rather than the gamma rays setting off a moment after the collision, the two speeds would differ by no more than

1.74.1×1015≈4×10−16,\frac{1.7}{4.1 \times 10^{15}} \approx 4 \times 10^{-16},4.1×10151.7​≈4×10−16,

less than one part in a thousand million million. (The LIGO, Virgo and Fermi teams’ careful version, allowing for the uncertain distance and for the gamma rays setting off up to ten seconds after the waves, puts the difference between −3×10−15-3 \times 10^{-15}−3×10−15 and +7×10−16+7 \times 10^{-16}+7×10−16 of the speed of light.)
