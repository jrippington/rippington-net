Looking back

The Van was a very long way from home, and facing the wrong way.

Albert had given them their orders the evening before, under the stars, when Cal asked whether anyone was out there. Before looking for life on anyone else’s world, he had said, learn to see this one as they would: from very far away, with nothing to go on but its light. So after breakfast John had asked the simulator to put them where a stranger might be, and the simulator had chosen a place, and a day, of its own.

It was 14 February 1990, and The Van hung in the dark six billion kilometres from the Sun, forty times as far out as the Earth, beside a spacecraft about the size of a small car with a white dish on its back. Voyager 1 had been travelling for twelve and a half years, past Jupiter and Saturn, and was on its way out of the Solar System for good. Its cameras were about to be switched off for ever, to save power for the long years ahead. But first, at the astronomer Carl Sagan’s urging, it had been turned round to photograph the planets it had left behind.

VOYAGER 1, 14 FEBRUARY 1990: OBSERVED. THESE ARE ITS PICTURES

The Sun was behind them, and it was a star: the brightest in the sky by a long way, too bright to look at for long, but a point, with no face at all. The Earth took longer to find. The Van’s screen showed them where to look, and enlarged Voyager’s own picture beside the view: a dark frame crossed by a band of scattered sunlight, and in the band, if you knew where it was, a single speck, less than one dot of the picture across. Sagan called it a pale blue dot.

“That’s us?” said Cal.

“All of us,” said John.

He meant it literally, and it took a while to sink in. Every ocean was in that speck, and every mountain and city, Teide and the café on its plateau, the lemon trees, the grain of sugar in Cal’s pocket. Everyone who had ever lived had lived on it. And from here it was smaller than a crumb on a dark tablecloth seen from the far side of a room.

From another star it would be less than that. The last journey had found that a planet beside its star is a moth beside a lighthouse: seen from even the nearest stars, the Earth would be lost in the Sun’s glare, ten billion times fainter in visible light and no farther from it than the width of a coin seen from five kilometres away. Whatever a stranger out there could learn about it, they would have to read from its light, spread into its colours, as the first journey had read the stars’.

“So what would they see?” said Cal. “If they could read the light. Would they know anyone was here?”

Ten months after Voyager’s picture, somebody had asked exactly that, and the simulator took them there.

A planet everyone knew

On 8 December 1990 a spacecraft called Galileo, on its long way to Jupiter, swung close past the Earth to borrow some of its speed. Its instruments had been built to read the air of Jupiter and its moons, and Carl Sagan, with four colleagues, had an idea for them. They would point them at the Earth as though it were a planet nobody had ever seen, and ask the question honestly: from these measurements alone, could anyone tell that there was life here?

The Van rode alongside as Galileo swept past, the Earth swelling to fill the windscreen and shrinking again behind them.

The answer was yes. Galileo found the Earth’s air full of oxygen. It found methane in it too, a trace, about one or two parts in a million. It found that something on the land soaked up red light strongly and reflected the infrared just beyond it, which no rock does: plants, though nothing in the data said so. And it picked up radio, narrow in frequency, pulsing, carrying patterns, which no planet makes by itself. The team published it in 1993, as a test of the methods for searching other worlds, and it remains one of the very few scientific papers whose conclusion is that there is life on the Earth.

LIFE ON EARTH: OBSERVED. CONFIDENCE: HIGH

“I should hope so,” said Cal.

“So the first time anyone searched a planet for life properly,” said John, “it was this one.”

“And it worked. Which is a relief.”

But the clues aren’t equally good. The radio is only a century old; for nearly all of the Earth’s history it had none. The plants on the land are less than five hundred million years old, as the fourteenth journey found. For billions of years, though, there has been a clue in the air, and an astronomer round another star could hope to read it.

A flame left burning

Methane is the gas in a kitchen’s gas ring, and oxygen is what burns it. Put the two together and they react: slowly, without any flame, with sunlight high in the sky helping them along, but they react, and the methane turns into carbon dioxide and water. A molecule of methane in the Earth’s air lasts, on average, about nine years. So the methane in the air today was nearly all made in the last few decades, and something must be making it as fast as it’s destroyed.

On the Earth, that something is mostly life: microbes in wetlands and rice fields and the stomachs of cows, and, lately, the gas and oil industry, whose methane was made by living things long ago. Volcanoes and hot rocks give off some too, but nowhere near enough to keep up. And oxygen, as the fourteenth journey found, doesn’t last either. It’s put there by living things, by the photosynthesis of plants and microbes, and left alone it would react with rock and volcanic gas and be gone within a few million years.

Think of it like this. You walk into a room and find a gas ring alight, burning steadily. You know at once that someone has turned the gas on, because a flame can’t keep itself going: it uses up what it burns. An air with oxygen and methane in it together is a flame that has been burning for millions of years. Something is keeping the gas on. The idea was put most clearly by the British scientist James Lovelock, in 1965, while he was working on how to look for life on Mars: a living world keeps its air out of balance, in a way a dead one can’t.

Biosignature

A sign of life that can be detected from a distance: something in a planet’s light which only life, as far as anyone knows, could produce or keep up. The best-known is a planet’s air holding gases that would react and use each other up unless something kept making them, such as oxygen and methane together, as the Earth’s does. No single gas is enough on its own, because each can also be made without life; a biosignature is a case to be argued, with every other way of making it ruled out.

It’s the Earth’s own infrared glow, the warmth every planet gives off, as every warm thing does, and across it are the dark bands where the gases in its air absorb: carbon dioxide, water, ozone, which is made from oxygen, and methane. Seen from the ground

Nobody needs a spacecraft to look at the Earth from outside. The Moon will do it for them.

Cal had seen it without knowing what he was looking at, four evenings before, when the new crescent hung over the ridge at the end of the twelfth journey. Look at a thin crescent Moon a few evenings after new, and the rest of its face isn’t quite black: it glows a faint ashen grey, so that the whole round Moon can be made out with the bright crescent along one edge. That glow is earthshine. It’s sunlight that has bounced off the Earth, its oceans, clouds and continents, travelled out to the Moon, and bounced back. So the dark part of a young Moon is lit by the whole sunlit face of the Earth at once, mixed into one light, exactly as a stranger far away would see it. Spread earthshine into its colours, and the Moon hands back the Earth as a distant planet.

Astronomers have done it, and they find the Earth’s oxygen and water in it. In 2011 a team with one of the great telescopes of the European Southern Observatory, in Chile, went further: from earthshine alone, they could tell how much of the face turned towards the Moon was cloud, how much was ocean, and how much was green with plants. One of the team, Enric Pallé, works at the Canary Islands’ astrophysics institute, down in La Laguna, in the north of Tenerife.

An eclipse of the Moon gives the other view. When the Moon passes into the Earth’s shadow it doesn’t vanish: the shadowed part turns a deep coppery red. The only sunlight reaching it then has grazed past the edge of the Earth, through its air, bent a little inwards on the way, and it has been reddened as a sunset is. A Moon in eclipse is lit by every sunrise and sunset on the Earth at once. And that’s just the light the last journey’s astronomers read when a planet crosses its star: starlight that has come through a ring of air round a dark world. During a partial eclipse on 16 August 2008, Pallé’s team pointed two telescopes on La Palma, across the sea from the café, at the reddened part of the Moon and spread its light into colours. Ozone was in it, and oxygen, water, carbon dioxide, methane, and the nitrogen most of the air is made of, all more strongly than in the light the Earth reflects. Seen through its own air, the pale blue dot is a pale red one.

Why one gas isn’t enough

So does oxygen in a planet’s air mean life? Not on its own, and it matters to be careful, because the history of this subject is full of claims made too soon.

A planet can make oxygen without any life at all. Picture a planet close to a flaring star, losing its oceans. High in its air, the star’s ultraviolet light breaks molecules of water apart into hydrogen and oxygen. Hydrogen is so light that it escapes into space; the oxygen, heavier, stays behind and builds up. Such a planet, dry and dead, could have an air full of oxygen and ozone, and astronomers expect that some planets round small, flaring stars may be exactly that. Methane can be made without life too: hot water reacting with certain rocks deep underground gives some off. That’s why the pair counts for more than either gas alone, why even the pair is only a case to be argued, and why what else is in the air, and what kind of planet and star it is, all have to be weighed.

Where water can stay liquid

Where would anyone look? Life on the Earth needs liquid water, and so, as far as anyone can imagine, would life anywhere: water dissolves things and carries them about, as chemistry needs. So the first place to look is a planet where water can stay liquid on the surface: not so close to its star that the oceans boil away, nor so far out that they freeze.

What decides it is how much warmth a planet gets from its star, and that falls off fast with distance. Sit by a campfire and you know the rule already: step back to twice the distance and the warmth on your face drops to a quarter, because the same heat is spread over four times the area. A planet’s air matters as well, the blanket of the thirteenth journey, which kept the young Earth’s seas from freezing under a fainter Sun. Carbon dioxide and water vapour let sunlight in but slow the warmth from getting out again, so a planet with thick air is warmer than its distance alone would make it. The Earth, with no air, would be frozen, about eighteen degrees below zero on average; its blanket makes it fifteen above.

The Sun’s own planets show what can go wrong. Venus, closer to the Sun than the Earth, is the lesson in being too close. It may once have had oceans, but as the Sun brightened, its water rose into its air as steam, and steam traps heat, which raised more steam, until the oceans were gone; their hydrogen escaped to space, and today Venus is a dry world under an air of carbon dioxide ninety times as heavy as ours, hot enough at the surface to melt lead. Mars, farther out, is the lesson in being too small. It’s at a distance where water could be liquid, given the right air, and it once had rivers and lakes. But it was too small to hold on to a thick air, and lost most of it to space, and its water is now frozen in the ground or gone.

Habitable zone

The range of distances from a star at which a planet like the Earth, with the right air, could have liquid water on its surface: closer in, its oceans would boil away; farther out, they would freeze. Round the Sun it runs from a little inside the Earth’s orbit to a little beyond Mars’s. Round a fainter star it is closer in, and round a brighter one farther out. Being in the zone doesn’t make a planet habitable, or tell us it has life: Mars is inside the Sun’s, and frozen.

The best reckoning puts the zone’s inner edge where a planet like the Earth would get about a tenth more sunlight than the Earth does now. Any closer, and more water rises into the air than the planet can shed, and the oceans begin their runaway climb into the sky, as Venus’s did. The outer edge is where even the thickest blanket of carbon dioxide can no longer keep the oceans from freezing.

The zone isn’t fixed. It moves with the star. Round a small, dim red star it huddles close in, and round a brighter one it lies farther out. The figure below shows it. The zone moves with time too. The Sun has been brightening since it was born, and the zone has been creeping slowly outwards. One day its inner edge will reach the Earth, and the next journey follows it there.

Where else

And yet the zone isn’t the only place liquid water can be. The Solar System has more water outside it than in it, and the simulator took them on a tour.

Mars came first, as it was billions of years ago, with a blue lake in a crater and a river winding into it across a red plain.

MARS, 3.5 BILLION YEARS AGO: RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM

That crater is Jezero, and the river’s delta is still there in the rocks, fanned out across its floor. In February 2021 NASA landed its rover Perseverance in it, to look for signs that anything lived in the lake, and to drill out cores of the most promising rocks and seal them in tubes for some later mission to bring home.

Europa was next, one of Jupiter’s large moons, a ball a little smaller than our Moon, white and cracked all over like an eggshell, and far too cold for any surface water: about a hundred and sixty degrees below zero. But Europa is squeezed and stretched by Jupiter’s tidal effects each time it goes round, as a ball of putty warms if you squeeze it over and over, and the heat keeps an ocean of liquid salt water under its ice, kilometres down. The evidence came in the late 1990s from Galileo, the spacecraft which had looked back at the Earth on its way: Europa disturbs Jupiter’s magnetism as a body of salt water would. That ocean is thought to hold about twice as much water as all the Earth’s oceans together.

EUROPA’S OCEAN: RECONSTRUCTED. NOBODY HAS SEEN IT

NASA’s Europa Clipper, launched on 14 October 2024, is on its way, to arrive in April 2030 and fly past Europa forty-nine times; Europe’s JUICE, launched in April 2023, reaches Jupiter in 2031.

Enceladus was the surprise. It’s one of Saturn’s moons, only about five hundred kilometres across, small enough to drive round in a day if it had roads. In 2005 the Cassini spacecraft found it spraying water into space, in great plumes from cracks near its south pole, out of an ocean under the ice. Cassini flew through the spray, more than once, as low as about fifty kilometres above the surface, and tasted it: salty water, tiny grains of rock of a kind made in hot water, molecules of carbon chemistry, and in 2017, hydrogen. Hydrogen matters. On the Earth’s sea floor, round the hot vents of the fourteenth journey, there are microbes living in the dark on just that: hydrogen from hot rock and carbon dioxide from the water, which they turn into methane. In 2023 phosphorus was found in the spray as well, one of the elements life is built from.

ENCELADUS’S SPRAY: OBSERVED. BY CASSINI, FROM INSIDE IT

“So there’s an ocean,” said Cal, “with food in it, and nobody’s been to see what’s eating it.”

Nobody has. No spacecraft is on its way.

Titan, Saturn’s largest moon, bigger than the planet Mercury, was the strangest of all: a world with a thick orange haze of air, mostly nitrogen, half as heavy again as the Earth’s, where it rains. But at a hundred and seventy-nine degrees below zero the rain is methane, the gas of the kitchen’s gas ring, liquid in that cold, and it runs in rivers into lakes and seas of methane and ethane, round the north pole. Water there is rock, as hard as granite. On 14 January 2005 the Huygens probe parachuted down through the haze and landed on a damp plain strewn with pebbles of ice. Deep under Titan’s crust, too, there seems to be an ocean of water. Nobody knows what chemistry might do in a sea of methane. NASA’s Dragonfly, a flying machine with eight rotors, is to launch in July 2028 and fly from place to place across Titan from 2034.

Venus was the last, and the odd one out. Its surface is far too hot for anything. But fifty to sixty kilometres up, in its clouds, the air is at about the Earth’s pressure and temperature, and some people have wondered whether microbes could float there. The trouble is that the clouds are droplets of strong sulphuric acid, and hardly any water.

Claims, told as claims

The history of the search is a history of claims, honestly made and honestly argued about, and none has yet become a discovery.

In 1996 a team at NASA announced that a meteorite found in Antarctica, a rock blasted off Mars by an impact long ago, held tiny worm-like shapes and minerals they thought could be the traces of microbes. The President of the United States spoke about it on television. Others looked hard at the same rock and found ways for every one of its features to form without life, and most scientists now think that is how they formed.

In 2020 a team reported phosphine, a gas made by microbes on the Earth, in the clouds of Venus; other teams, looking again, found much less of it or none at all, and the argument continues.

In 2023, and more strongly in April 2025, a team using the Webb telescope reported hints of a gas called dimethyl sulphide in the air of K2-18 b, a planet about eight times the Earth’s mass, 124 light-years away, in its star’s habitable zone. On the Earth, that gas is made almost only by sea life. Other teams analysing the same light found no reliable sign of it, and a reanalysis led from NASA in July 2025, with four more crossings of the star, put the hint well below anything anyone would call a detection. Whether K2-18 b even has an ocean is argued over.

And in September 2025 NASA announced that Perseverance had found, in a mudstone in an old river valley running into Jezero, a pattern of small dark spots ringed with minerals, iron phosphate and iron sulphide, which on the Earth are often made by microbes feeding on the remains of living things in mud. NASA called it a “potential biosignature”, and chose the words with care: the rocks show no sign of the heat or the acid which could have made the spots without life, but nobody can rule those out from Mars. The rover drilled a core from the rock and sealed it in a tube, called Sapphire Canyon. It’s still there. In January 2026 the United States Congress declined to pay for the mission meant to bring Perseverance’s samples home, and none has replaced it; China plans to launch its own mission around 2028, to bring back rock from another part of Mars about three years later.

The simulator had a line for all of it.

LIFE ELSEWHERE: SPECULATION. CONFIDENCE: NONE, EITHER WAY

“Either way,” said Cal. “So it doesn’t think there’s nobody, either.”

“It doesn’t think anything,” said John. “It just won’t pretend.”

Drake’s questions

That leaves the biggest question of all: is anyone out there to talk to?

In the spring of 1960 a young radio astronomer, Frank Drake, pointed the 85-foot dish at Green Bank, in West Virginia, at two nearby stars much like the Sun, Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani, about eleven light-years away, and listened, six hours a day for months, for a signal that couldn’t be natural. He heard nothing, apart from a false alarm that turned out to be an aircraft. The next year, in the autumn of 1961, he gathered ten people at Green Bank to talk about how anyone might search properly. They included a young Carl Sagan, a man who studied how dolphins talk to each other, and the astronomer Su-Shu Huang, who two years before had given the habitable zone its name. Another, the chemist Melvin Calvin, was expecting to hear any day that he had won the Nobel Prize, for working out how plants build sugar from the carbon dioxide in the air; somebody had brought champagne, and when the news came they drank it.

Needing an agenda for the meeting, Drake wrote down everything you’d need to know to work out how many civilisations in our galaxy might be sending signals now, and put it on the blackboard. It became known as the Drake equation, and it’s really a list of questions:

  • how many new stars our galaxy makes in a year;
  • how many of them have planets;
  • of those, how many planets could hold life;
  • on how many of those life actually begins;
  • on how many life becomes intelligent;
  • how many intelligent kinds of life send signals out into space;
  • and for how long each keeps sending, before it stops, or dies out, or loses interest.

Multiply them together, and you have the number of signal-senders in the galaxy at any one time.

In 1961 every one of those was a guess. Now the first three are known, or nearly. The galaxy makes a few new stars a year. The six thousand planets of the last journey say that nearly every star has planets, and that a good share of stars have one about the Earth’s size at a distance where water could be liquid. Those three questions have moved from SPECULATION to RECONSTRUCTED. The other four haven’t moved at all. We know of one planet where life began, one where it became intelligent, one where it sends signals, and we don’t yet know for how long. One example can’t tell you how often something happens.

Signals, and false alarms

Drake didn’t stop at questions. On 16 November 1974 the great radio dish at Arecibo, in Puerto Rico, sent a short burst of radio, 1,679 pulses long, towards the globular cluster M13, a ball of several hundred thousand old stars. Drake had designed it, with Sagan and others. Laid out in a grid of 73 rows of 23, the pulses make a picture: the numbers from one to ten, the atoms life is built from, a figure of a person, the Solar System and the dish itself. John has photographed M13 from his garden, and his page gives its distance as about 24,200 light-years, so the message arrives around the year 26,200. So far it has gone about 52 light-years, a fifth of a per cent of the way.

“Not much of a conversation,” said Cal. “If they answer straight away, we hear back in about the year 50,000.”

“It was never meant to be a conversation,” said Albert, who had come out with the coffee. “It was meant to show it could be done.” The dish itself collapsed in December 2020, its cables worn out. The message is still on its way.

Listening has gone on ever since, and it has heard one thing nobody can explain. On 15 August 1977 Ohio State University’s radio telescope, Big Ear, picked up a signal from the direction of Sagittarius, narrow in frequency, close to the 21-centimetre line hydrogen gives out, which radio astronomers had long thought the obvious channel for anyone wanting to be heard. It lasted seventy-two seconds, exactly as long as the Earth’s turning took to carry the telescope’s gaze across that patch of sky, as a star would. A few days later Jerry Ehman, going through the printouts, circled it and wrote “Wow!” in the margin. Nobody has ever picked it up again, though many have looked. In 2025 a team going back through old records suggested that a cold cloud of hydrogen may have brightened for a moment, lit up by some distant flare; they admit the idea is speculative too.

Most alarms have been nearer home. For seventeen years the radio dish at Parkes, in Australia, recorded strange short bursts which nobody could explain, until in 2015 they were traced to the observatory’s kitchen: a microwave oven, opened by someone too impatient to wait for it to finish. Albert, who had never owned a microwave oven, looked as though this proved something. In 2019, at the same dish, a narrow signal turned up which seemed to come only from the direction of Proxima Centauri, the nearest star. It took two years of work to show it was interference from ordinary electrical equipment, here on the Earth.

The biggest search ever made, Breakthrough Listen, has been running since 2016, on great dishes in West Virginia and Australia, listening to the nearest million stars, the plane of the galaxy, and a hundred galaxies beyond it. In 2026 radio telescopes in New Mexico and South Africa listened to K2-18 b, in case. Nothing.

Where is everybody?

In the summer of 1950, walking to lunch at Los Alamos, the physicist Enrico Fermi and his colleagues were joking about flying saucers. Later, over the meal, Fermi suddenly asked something like “Where is everybody?” The accounts of his words differ, but everyone at the table understood him at once.

It’s a better question than it sounds. Suppose a civilisation, somewhere in the galaxy, built ships which could cross between stars at a hundredth of the speed of light, a slow pace by the standards of science fiction. Even at that speed, settling one star after another, its descendants could spread across the whole galaxy in tens of millions of years. And the galaxy is more than ten billion years old: long enough for that to have happened hundreds of times over. If even one civilisation, anywhere, had done it, they should be everywhere by now, here included. There’s no sign of them.

For the mathematically inclinedWhere is everybody?

There are as many answers as there are people who have thought about it. Perhaps life is rare, or minds are rarer; perhaps civilisations don’t last, or don’t travel, or keep quiet. Or perhaps we simply haven’t looked. In 2018 a team worked out how much of the possible searching has been done, every direction, distance and radio channel together, and found that it’s about as much as a hot tub’s worth of water from all the Earth’s oceans. Look in a hot tub’s worth of seawater and find no fish, and you haven’t learned much about the sea.

“So we don’t know,” said John.

“Nobody knows,” said Albert. “That is a perfectly good answer, as long as you say it out loud.”

Back to Teide

By evening the coaches had gone, and the café smelled of frying. Albert was making the last batch of the day, and on the counter, beside the tray of lemon sugar, was a lemon.

Cal looked at it as though he had never seen one before.

“There’s actual lemon in the sugar,” he said.

“Of course there is,” said Albert. “Where did you think the lemon came from?”

“I didn’t think about it at all.”

“All this time,” said John, “and nobody has asked.”

Albert took a small grater and drew it down the lemon’s skin, and a fine yellow snow fell into the sugar, and with it a smell which filled the café: sharp, clean and bright. That smell is a single kind of molecule, mostly, ten carbon atoms and sixteen hydrogen atoms, made in tiny pockets in the lemon’s skin, and the sharpness of its juice is another, citric acid, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, like the sugar.

Cal picked the lemon up and turned it in his hand, and they followed the whole story of it, back from the counter, through every journey they had made.

Its hydrogen was made in the first three minutes of the universe, in the third journey’s oven. Its carbon and its oxygen were made in stars, by the eleventh journey’s step which only just works, and thrown out into space as the stars died. That gas, enriched by billions of years of stars, fell together into the twelfth journey’s disc, and the disc made the Earth. A planet the size of Mars struck it, and it glowed; the rock breathed out its air and its oceans, through volcanoes like the one under the café, as the thirteenth journey had watched. In the oceans, something began to copy itself, and some of its descendants learned to live on sunlight and filled the air with oxygen, as the fourteenth had found, and under a shield of ozone life came out onto the land: mosses, ferns, forests, flowers, and among them, eventually, in the foothills of the Himalayas, a small tree whose fruit was a cross between a citron and a bitter orange. People carried it west, to Persia and Egypt and round the Mediterranean, and Spanish ships carried it out to these islands and on across the ocean: Columbus took lemon seeds to the Americas on his second voyage, in 1493, by way of the Canaries. And in a café on a volcano, at the end of all of that, someone was grating its skin onto a doughnut.

“I bought it in La Orotava,” said Albert, “on Tuesday.”

“That’s the bit the simulator isn’t sure about,” said Cal.

They sat by the window with the new doughnuts while the light went off the mountain. It was a long, strange history, and every step of it was written in something you could hold: the hydrogen in the lemon’s juice, the iron in the old red rocks, the oxygen in the café’s air, the starch in the doughnut’s flour. And all of it had happened on a speck less than one dot of a picture across, which from the nearest star couldn’t be seen at all.

The first stars came out over Teide, and John looked at them differently again. Somewhere among them, perhaps, a planet’s air was out of balance too, and somebody was looking this way and wondering about a faint yellow star with a blue dot beside it. Or nobody was, anywhere, and the café’s terrace was the only place in the galaxy where anyone had ever wondered at all. He couldn’t decide which was the more astonishing.

“The Sun’s been getting brighter all this time,” he said. “And it’ll keep getting brighter. The oceans, the lemons, the stars, all of it.” He looked up at the mountain, black now against the sky. “How does it all end?”

Albert brushed the lemon sugar off his hands.

“Tomorrow,” he said, “we run the film the other way.”
