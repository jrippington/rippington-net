Why methane can’t last

Methane and oxygen react, CH4+2 O2→CO2+2 H2O\text{CH}_4 + 2\,\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}CH4​+2O2​→CO2​+2H2​O, giving out about 890 kilojoules for every mole (16 grams) of methane burned. In air with as much oxygen as the Earth’s, chemical balance would leave essentially no methane at all; it survives only because the reaction is slow, helped along in the air mostly by sunlight-made molecules of hydroxyl, OH.

How long a molecule lasts. If each molecule has the same small chance of being destroyed every year, the amount left after a time ttt, with nothing new added, falls as

N(t)=N0 e−t/τ,N(t) = N_0\, e^{-t/\tau},N(t)=N0​e−t/τ,

where τ\tauτ is the average lifetime, about 9 years for methane in today’s air. After 9 years about 37 per cent is left; after 30 years, 4 per cent; after a century, about two parts in a hundred thousand.

How fast it must be made. For the amount to stay steady, it must be made as fast as it’s destroyed:

sources=Nτ.\text{sources} = \frac{N}{\tau}.sources=τN​.

The air holds about 1.8×10201.8 \times 10^{20}1.8×1020 moles of gas, of which about 1.9 parts in a million are methane: 3.4×10143.4 \times 10^{14}3.4×1014 moles, or

3.4×1014×16 g≈5.4×1015 g=5,400 million tonnes.3.4 \times 10^{14} \times 16\ \text{g} \approx 5.4 \times 10^{15}\ \text{g} = 5{,}400\ \text{million tonnes}.3.4×1014×16 g≈5.4×1015 g=5,400 million tonnes.

Divided by nine years, that’s about 600 million tonnes of methane made every year, near what budgets of the world’s methane find (wetlands, farming, fossil fuels, landfill, termites and the rest). Volcanoes, seeps and hot rocks supply only a small share of it.

Oxygen. The air holds about 1.2×10181.2 \times 10^{18}1.2×1018 kilograms of oxygen. Against the slow geological sinks, the weathering of rocks and the gases from volcanoes, it would last a few million years if nothing replaced it (A Spark in the Water’s maths page works it out); life cycles it far faster, through plants and the animals and microbes that breathe it. So a methane molecule lasts less than a hundred-thousandth as long as the oxygen around it, and both must be renewed for the two to stay side by side.

Where water can stay liquid

Sunlight and distance. A star of brightness LLL spreads its light over a sphere of area 4πd24\pi d^24πd2 at distance ddd, so the sunlight falling on each square metre there is

F=L4πd2,FF⊕=L/L⊙(d/1 AU)2.F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}, \qquad \frac{F}{F_\oplus} = \frac{L/L_\odot}{(d/\text{1 AU})^2}.F=4πd2L​,F⊕​F​=(d/1 AU)2L/L⊙​​.

Twice as far, a quarter as much. Venus, at 0.723 AU, gets 1.91 times the Earth’s sunlight; Mars, at 1.524 AU, 0.43 times.

For the Earth, A≈0.3A \approx 0.3A≈0.3, so T≈255T \approx 255T≈255 K, about −18-18−18 °C; its air’s carbon dioxide and water vapour raise the average to about 288 K, +15+15+15 °C. A dark ball at Jupiter’s distance, with no air, would settle at 278 K/5.20≈122278\ \text{K}/\sqrt{5.20} \approx 122278 K/5.20​≈122 K, and at Saturn’s at about 90 K; icy moons, which reflect most of their light, are colder still. So no sunlight keeps Europa’s or Enceladus’s oceans liquid: the heat comes from inside, from the flexing their planets’ tidal effects give them on every orbit (and, for Titan’s deep ocean, mostly from the slow decay of radioactive atoms in its rock).

Drake’s questions

Suppose civilisations that send signals arise in the galaxy at a steady rate, rrr a year, and each keeps sending for LLL years. Then, at any time, the number sending is

N=r L,N = r\, L,N=rL,

as the number of people in a queue is the rate they join it times how long each waits. Drake’s list builds rrr from the stars:

r=R⋆ fp ne fl fi fc,r = R_\star \, f_p \, n_e \, f_l \, f_i \, f_c,r=R⋆​fp​ne​fl​fi​fc​,

the rate of star formation R⋆R_\starR⋆​ (stars a year), the share with planets fpf_pfp​, the number of planets fit for life per such star nen_ene​, and the shares where life begins flf_lfl​, where it becomes intelligent fif_ifi​, and where it sends signals fcf_cfc​. So

N=R⋆ fp ne fl fi fc L.N = R_\star \, f_p \, n_e \, f_l \, f_i \, f_c \, L.N=R⋆​fp​ne​fl​fi​fc​L.

(Using today’s star formation for stars billions of years old is an assumption: the galaxy made stars faster when it was young.)

The message to M13. It was 1,679 pulses long, and 1,679=23×731{,}679 = 23 \times 731,679=23×73, a product of two prime numbers, so there are only two ways to lay it out as a rectangle: 23 rows of 73, which is gibberish, or 73 rows of 23, which is a picture. M13 is about 24,200 light-years away, so the Arecibo message of 16 November 1974 arrives about 24,200 years later, around the year 26,200. A reply sent at once would take as long again, arriving around the year 50,400. By October 2026 the message had travelled about 52 light-years, 52/24,200=0.252/24{,}200 = 0.252/24,200=0.2 per cent of the way.

Where is everybody?

Crossing the galaxy. The Milky Way’s disc is about 100,000 light-years across. A ship at a hundredth of the speed of light covers a light-year in a hundred years, so it would cross the disc in

100,000 light-years0.01 c=107 years.\frac{100{,}000\ \text{light-years}}{0.01\ c} = 10^7\ \text{years}.0.01 c100,000 light-years​=107 years.

Settling as it goes, stopping a few centuries at each new star before sending ships on to the next, slows the front of the spread by a few times, to some tens of millions of years. The galaxy’s oldest stars are more than ten billion years old, so

1010 years3×107 years≈300:\frac{10^{10}\ \text{years}}{3 \times 10^7\ \text{years}} \approx 300:3×107 years1010 years​≈300:

time for the galaxy to have been filled hundreds of times over, by anyone who started early.

How much has been searched. Wright, Kanodia and Lubar (2018) counted the searches made so far across every direction, distance, radio channel, sensitivity and the rest, as a share of everything that could be searched, and expressed it as water: about 7,700 litres, a large hot tub, out of the Earth’s oceans’ 1.3×10211.3 \times 10^{21}1.3×1021 litres,
