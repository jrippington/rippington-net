For sixteen journeys The Van's simulator has mostly run history backwards. Now it runs it forwards, and its confidence drains away: the Sun swells and dies, the galaxies leave, the last small stars go out, and even the black holes evaporate. Then, at the café that night, Cal takes the grain of sugar out of his pocket, and they tell its whole story, from the first second of the universe to a leaf. And then he eats it.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Natural History of the Universe, part 17 of 18

The other way

For sixteen journeys, The Van’s simulator had mostly been running the film backwards.

It had rewound the expanding universe to its hot beginning, watched the first nuclei cook in the first three minutes, cleared the fog of the oldest light, lit the first stars, built galaxies out of small ones, and followed a cloud of gas, already rich with the ashes of earlier stars, as it fell together into the Sun and its planets. The day before, it had shown them the young Earth, molten, breathing out its air, and coming alive. Every step of it had been worked out from light that was still arriving, and from rocks and meteorites that could still be held.

This morning John asked it to run the other way.

DIRECTION: FORWARD. CONFIDENCE: HIGH, FOR NOW

“For now?” said Cal.

“It’s never been asked to go this far before,” said John. “It’s being careful.”

It was right to be. Everything the simulator had shown them so far was history: it had happened, and it had left evidence, light from distant galaxies and the oldest light of all, the make-up of old stars, the clocks in radioactive rocks. The future has left no evidence of anything, so nothing in it can ever be OBSERVED. All anyone can do is take the physics of the past, which works so well, and ask what it says comes next. Some of that is about as safe as physics gets: the Sun is a star like millions of others, which can be seen at every stage of their lives, and its future can be read from theirs. Some of it is a long, careful guess. And some of it is a guess about physics nobody yet knows. That left the simulator two of its three words, RECONSTRUCTED and SPECULATION, and over the course of the day they watched its confidence drain from the first to the second.

John had a theory of his own before they started, and it was a comfortable one.

“The stars will just keep going,” he said. “Old ones die, new ones are born. That’s what half this series has been about. The cloud collapses, the stars light, they burn, they blow their gas back out, and the next lot are made from it. Round and round.”

Albert, who had come out to The Van with the coffee, looked at him over his cup.

“And the gas,” he said. “Where does more of it come from?”

The lights go out, slowly

That circle of stars and gas is real, but it doesn’t go round for ever. Follow the gas, one step at a time.

All the hydrogen there is was made in the first three minutes of the universe, as the third journey found. Nothing has made any more since, and nothing can: stars only use it up, fusing it into helium and heavier atoms, which is how they shine. And when a star dies, it doesn’t give everything back. A star like the Sun puffs off its outer layers, but leaves its core behind as a white dwarf, a lump of carbon and oxygen the size of the Earth, which will never burn again. A massive star throws out more of itself in its supernova, but leaves a neutron star or a black hole, which hold on to their matter for good. So every generation of stars returns less gas than it took, and what it returns has less hydrogen in it. Each time round, the stock of fuel gets smaller.

It’s like Albert’s flour. He bakes every morning from the sack in the store room, and the scraps go back into the next batch, but most of every batch is eaten, and nobody brings him more. However carefully he works, a day will come when the sack is empty.

So the lights go out. Not suddenly: the stock of gas will last a very long time, and the smallest stars will burn for far longer still. But every star there will ever be is made from a fixed stock, and the stock is running down. The only way out would be a new supply of hydrogen from somewhere, and that is exactly what Fred’s steady universe had: new atoms of hydrogen turning up in the gaps, one here and one there, to keep the galaxies topped up for ever. The evidence of the second journey and the fourth killed it. The universe had a beginning, and there is no new supply. The stars are spending a fortune made once, in the first three minutes, and never added to.

The Sun’s future

The simulator began close to home.

It showed them the Sun as it is now, ordinary and middle-aged, as the ninth journey had found it, turning about four million tonnes of itself into sunlight every second. Then it ran the Sun forward a billion years at a time.

The Sun brightened. It has been doing so since it was born: it was about seventy per cent as bright as it is now when the Earth was young, as the thirteenth journey found. In its core, every second, hydrogen is turned into helium, and helium is heavier, so the core slowly fills with ash that can’t burn yet; the core shrinks and heats under the weight, burns what hydrogen it has a little faster, and the Sun grows a little brighter, about ten per cent brighter every billion years.

That’s not much, but it’s enough. In about a billion years, the Sun will give the Earth about a tenth more warmth than it does now, and by the last journey’s reckoning that is the edge of the habitable zone: more water rises into the air, and water vapour traps heat, which raises more, until the oceans begin to boil away into the sky, where sunlight splits them and their hydrogen escapes into space, as Venus’s did. The Earth’s oceans have, by the best reckoning, about a billion years left. Long before the Sun is anything but an ordinary star, the Earth will be a hot, dry rock.

THE EARTH’S OCEANS, A BILLION YEARS FROM NOW: RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM

Then, in about five and a half billion years, the hydrogen in the Sun’s core runs out. As the tenth journey found for stars like it, the core shrinks and heats, hydrogen burns in a shell around it, and the Sun swells into a red giant. Over the next two billion years it grows, slowly at first and then faster and faster, until, about seven and a half billion years from now, it is more than 250 times as wide as it is today and nearly three thousand times as bright, and its swollen surface has reached out past where Venus goes round. Mercury and Venus are swallowed. So much of its outer gas streams away in a wind that it loses a third of its mass, and as its pull weakens, the planets’ orbits widen: the Earth’s would widen by half. But the Earth would be skimming through the edge of the giant’s thin outer gas, and the drag of that gas, and the Earth’s tidal effects on the giant, pull it in. By the best calculation, made in 2008 by Klaus-Peter Schröder and Robert Connon Smith, the Earth is swallowed too, about half a million years before the Sun reaches its largest. It’s close, and not certain.

THE EARTH, 7.6 BILLION YEARS FROM NOW: PROBABLY SWALLOWED. CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM

“Probably,” said Cal.

“I’m not sure whether that’s better or worse,” said John.

At its largest, the giant Sun lights the helium in its core, shrinks back into a steadier giant about ten times its present width, burns the helium for about a hundred and thirty million years, swells a second time, and gives the last of its outer layers away to space. Lit by what’s left in the middle, they may glow for a few thousand years as a planetary nebula, though a faint one: the Sun, by a calculation of 2018, is only just heavy enough to make one at all. Then they fade. What’s left is the core: a white dwarf of carbon and oxygen, a little over half the Sun’s mass, about the size of the Earth, held up against its own weight by nothing but electrons that won’t share, the floor of Room for Only Two pushed as hard as it can go. It won’t burn again. It starts out white-hot, and spends the rest of time cooling.

Two galaxies become one

Meanwhile, the galaxy is changing round it.

Andromeda, the nearest big galaxy, is coming towards us. The seventh journey found that galaxies grow by colliding and merging, and the simulator showed the two great spirals, ours and Andromeda’s, falling together, swinging past each other, flinging out long tails of stars, and falling together again, until they settled into one great, smooth, rounded galaxy, with all their stars swarming round its middle like bees round a hive.

It won’t be a crash, for the stars. Stars are so small and so far apart that when two galaxies merge, almost none of them ever hit each other: if the Sun were a grain of sugar, the nearest star would be another grain about fourteen kilometres away. The Sun would be swung onto a new orbit, perhaps far out in the merged galaxy’s halo, perhaps closer in, and the night sky over whatever is left of the Earth would change completely. But the Solar System itself would sail through untouched.

Whether it happens at all is less certain than it used to be. John has photographed Andromeda’s core from his garden, and his page has the latest: for decades the two galaxies were expected to collide in about four and a half billion years; in 2025 a new study of their motions gave only about an even chance of their merging in the next ten billion years; in 2026 a reanalysis put it at about nine chances in ten.

The galaxies leave

Then the simulator showed them something stranger, and John recognised it at once.

In Further Apart Every Day, the last journey of the relativity series, they found that the universe’s expansion is speeding up, driven by something nobody understands, called dark energy, and that because of it there is a horizon: a distance beyond which nothing happening now can ever be seen by us, however long we wait, because the space between will grow too fast for its light ever to arrive. That horizon is about sixteen billion light-years away. And as the expansion carries the galaxies outwards, one by one they cross it.

The simulator ran on, and the galaxies began to leave. Not all at once: the merged galaxy kept its small family of neighbours, held to it by gravity, as Albert’s lump of three currants had stayed a lump while the dough rose round it. But every other galaxy, every one in Markarian’s Chain and the Virgo Cluster, every faint smudge and distant quasar in the corners of John’s pictures, was carried slowly outwards. Within about a hundred billion years, every one of them has been carried out over the horizon. The old light they sent before they crossed it is still arriving, but stretched further and dimmed more every year, until, long before the last star goes out, no telescope could ever pick it up. The oldest light itself, the glow of the fourth journey, fares no better. Its waves, about a millimetre long today, are stretched with everything else, and in about 340 billion years, some twenty-five times the universe’s present age, they are hundreds of kilometres long: too long to get through the thin gas between the stars to reach anyone’s instruments.

For the mathematically inclinedThe horizons’ distances

Albert had gone quiet.

“Think of the astronomers then,” he said. “If there are any. They would look out from their one great galaxy, and see nothing beyond it. No other galaxies, no expansion, no oldest light. Just their own stars, in an empty, dark, unchanging universe.” He turned his cup round on its saucer. “They would think, as I once did, that it stands still. And nothing they could ever measure would tell them otherwise.”

It’s one of the strangest results in cosmology, and as far as anyone can tell it’s true. The evidence that the universe had a beginning, the galaxies rushing apart and the oldest light, is here now, and will not be here for ever. Astronomers a hundred billion years from now, however clever, would have to piece the story together from far fainter clues, if they could find any at all. We live at a lucky time to read it.

All of this assumes that dark energy stays as it is, and that is an open question. In 2025 the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, mapping millions of galaxies, found a hint that dark energy might be weakening over time. It was a hint of the strength physicists call three sigma, the kind a fluke would produce only a few times in a thousand: worth watching, not yet worth believing. A recalibration of some of the exploding stars used alongside it has since weakened the hint. It’s a question being asked, not a discovery. If dark energy changes, the far future changes with it: it could fade and let gravity draw the galaxies back, or strengthen. Nobody knows which.

The last stars

The galaxies gone, the simulator came back to the stars.

Star making slows down. As the gas thins, fewer stars are made, and the big ones, which live only millions of years, become rare and then stop being made at all; with them go the brilliant blue-white stars, the great glowing nurseries of the eighth journey, and the supernovae. In a few trillion years, the galaxies’ gas is nearly used up. The sky over a planet in the merged galaxy turns slowly redder and dimmer, until the only stars left shining are the smallest, faintest and reddest of all.

Those smallest stars are the commonest stars there are. About three quarters of all the stars in our galaxy are small, cool, dim red stars, too faint to see without a telescope: even the brightest is at the very edge of what an eye can see, on the darkest night, from the café. The nearest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is one. So is TRAPPIST-1, of the fifteenth journey.

Red dwarf

A small, cool, dim star, between about a twelfth and a half of the Sun’s mass, glowing a deep red, its surface at about 2,000 to 3,600 degrees Celsius against the Sun’s 5,500. Red dwarfs are by far the commonest stars, about three in every four, and the longest-lived: they burn their hydrogen so slowly, and mix it so thoroughly through themselves, that the smallest will shine for about ten trillion years. The universe is too young for a single red dwarf ever to have burned out.

They last so long for two reasons. A small star has only a gentle squeeze in its core, so it burns its fuel very slowly, and as the tenth journey found, the lighter a star, the longer it lives. And the smallest are churned through and through by convection, the boiling of the ninth journey, from core to surface, so that all their hydrogen is carried down into the core in time, not just the tenth or so that a star like the Sun ever burns. The smallest, about a twelfth of the Sun’s mass, will shine for about ten trillion years, more than seven hundred times the present age of the universe; one of a tenth of the Sun’s mass, for about six trillion. Every red dwarf ever born is still shining.

But they go out in the end. By about a hundred trillion years from now, the last of them has burned out, slowly growing hotter and bluer as it goes, and then fading, and no new ones are being made. The age of stars is over.

LAST STAR, 100 TRILLION YEARS FROM NOW: RECONSTRUCTED. CONFIDENCE: FALLING

“A hundred trillion years,” said John. “And the universe is fourteen billion years old.”

“So we’re at the very start,” said Cal.

They were. If the whole age of the stars, from the first star to the last, were squeezed into a single day, everything that has happened so far, the first stars, the galaxies, the Sun and the Earth, life, the café, the doughnuts, would have taken up the first twelve seconds after midnight.

Embers

After the last star there are only the remains: white dwarfs cooling, neutron stars cooling, the cold failed stars called brown dwarfs, planets that were never lit, and black holes. Now and then, very rarely, two brown dwarfs collide and make a small new star, so that at any one time there are perhaps a hundred of those in the whole galaxy, the last sparks of a fire. The simulator showed the sky over a planet then, a dark sky with almost nothing in it, and now and then a dim red point appearing and slowly fading.

The white dwarfs, the Sun’s included, go on cooling, from white to yellow to red to black, until after about a thousand trillion years they’re barely warmer than the space round them: black dwarfs, as they are called, though none exists yet, because no white dwarf has had nearly long enough to cool. Over the same time, stars passing close to one another pull their old planets away, and send them wandering.

The galaxy itself comes apart. Over about ten billion billion years, the near misses between its dead stars add up, flinging most of them out of the galaxy altogether, into the dark between, while the rest sink inwards and are swallowed by the great black hole at its centre.

And then the question is whether matter itself lasts. The protons in every nucleus have never been seen to decay: experiments watching enormous tanks of water, deep underground, show that if they do, they last more than a one followed by thirty-four noughts of years. Some theories say they must decay in the end; nobody knows.

PROTONS, IN THE VERY LONG RUN: SPECULATION. NOBODY HAS EVER SEEN ONE GO

If they do, the dead stars and planets slowly dissolve into light and a thin spray of particles. If not, the cold remnants simply go on, scattered through the dark. Either way, the last things of any size are the black holes.

A black hole evaporates

Nothing escapes a black hole. In One Way In, John and Cal followed the probe down through an event horizon and found it a boundary of one direction only: anything can go in, and nothing, not even light, can come out. So it seems a black hole must last for ever, swallowing what comes near it and losing nothing.

Teleport to the Relativity moonOne Way InOne Way In: the event horizon, and why nothing comes out

In 1974 Stephen Hawking, then thirty-two, found that it doesn’t. A young physicist, Jacob Bekenstein, had argued that a black hole ought to have something like a temperature. Hawking set out to show that it couldn’t, and found instead that it must: that a black hole glows, very faintly, and that the glow carries its mass away, a little at a time, until it is gone. Three years earlier he had proved that a black hole’s horizon can never shrink, and in 2025 the gravitational waves of two black holes merging, the kind heard in Echoes of Spacetime, bore him out: the horizon of the one they made was bigger than theirs together. Now he showed that, once the quantum rules are taken into account, it can. He had found the exception to his own rule, which is rarer than it sounds.

How can anything come out of a place nothing can leave? The answer needs both of the earlier series at once.

Begin with empty space. In What the Sugar Is Made Of, John and Cal found that it isn’t empty: every field there is, the electromagnetic field and the electron field and all the rest, fills all of space, and even with no particles in it at all, each one keeps its lowest jiggle, trembling faintly everywhere, a restlessness that can never be taken away. But it can’t give anything away either. It is the lowest a field can be, with nothing beneath it to fall to, so no instrument, however sensitive, sees empty space glow.

Teleport to the Quantum Mechanics moonWhat the Sugar Is Made OfEmpty space isn’t empty: every field, at its lowest, still faintly trembling

Now put a horizon in the middle of it. In Time Runs Faster Upstairs, John and Cal found that light climbing out of gravity arrives stretched, its waves longer and redder; and near an event horizon the stretching grows without limit, so that light starting out just above the horizon arrives far away stretched almost beyond measure. The horizon does the same to the field’s trembling. The very fine, fast trembling of the field just outside the horizon is stretched as it climbs away, and reaches the outside world as long, slow waves. The field’s quietest state close to the horizon, the lowest it can be there, isn’t the quietest state as seen from far away: the stretching has lifted some of the trembling into real ripples, lumps of the field, photons. Far away, they arrive as real light, a little at a time, as though the black hole were a warm body glowing in the dark.

Teleport to the Relativity moonTime Runs Faster UpstairsLight climbing out of gravity arrives stretched

There’s a picture of this that Hawking himself used, and nearly everyone else since. Empty space, by the quantum rules, is always briefly making pairs of particles out of nothing, a particle and its antiparticle, which vanish again almost at once, before anyone could notice the loan. Right at the edge of a horizon, one of a pair can fall in while the other escapes. The escaping one carries energy away; the one falling in carries less than none, and the black hole is a little lighter for it. It’s a picture, not the calculation, and it shouldn’t be pushed too far; but it gets the important things right. The glow comes from the horizon, it’s paid for by the black hole’s mass, and nothing has to come out from inside.

Hawking radiation

The faint glow every black hole should give off, worked out by Stephen Hawking in 1974 from quantum fields near an event horizon. It is the glow of a hot body, at a temperature that is lower the heavier the black hole: a black hole of the Sun’s mass would be about sixty billionths of a degree above absolute zero. The energy it carries away comes from the black hole’s mass, so a black hole slowly shrinks, grows hotter, glows faster, and in the end vanishes in a final burst. Nobody has yet seen it from a real black hole; it is too faint to detect from any black hole known.

It’s very faint. The heavier a black hole, the colder it glows: one with the Sun’s mass would glow at about sixty billionths of a degree above absolute zero, far colder than the oldest light, at nearly three degrees, which is falling into it. For now, every black hole anyone knows of takes in more of the oldest light than it gives out, and grows. Only when the universe has expanded and cooled for some hundreds of billions of years more will the oldest light be colder than the black holes, and they begin, at last, to lose.

And it is very slow. A black hole of the Sun’s mass would take about two followed by sixty-seven noughts of years to evaporate. The one at the heart of M87, six and a half billion times the Sun’s mass, the size of the black hole One Way In’s simulator dropped the probe into, would take about six followed by ninety-six noughts. The biggest there will ever be, perhaps a hundred billion Suns, swollen by swallowing the remains of whole galaxies, would last a googol years or so, a one followed by a hundred noughts. Ten times the mass lasts a thousand times as long.

Small ones go fast. As a black hole shrinks it gets hotter, and the hotter it is the faster it glows, so it shrinks faster still, and the end comes in a rush. A black hole as heavy as The Van, if there could be one, would last about a millionth of a second. One that started out about as heavy as a hill, if any were made in the hot beginning, would be finishing about now, in a burst of gamma rays; astronomers have looked for such bursts, and seen none.

Nobody has seen Hawking radiation, and nobody can, from any black hole known: it’s far too faint, drowned by the oldest light. It’s worked out from two theories that have passed every test so far, quantum fields and general relativity, used together in a way most physicists trust, though nobody can yet check it at a real horizon. In 2019, Jeff Steinhauer’s group in Haifa made a kind of horizon for sound, in a stream of atoms cooled almost to absolute zero and flowing faster than sound can travel through it, and saw the same kind of glow coming off it, at the temperature the calculation predicts. It’s a horizon for sound, not for light, and it can’t prove anything about real black holes. But the idea works where it can be tested.

Nothing left to simulate

The simulator ran on to the end.

The last black holes shrank, and grew hot, and went out in their last brief flashes, scattered across a dark so wide that no two of them would ever have seen each other’s light. Then there was nothing left of any size: a thin, cold glow of light and a few scattered particles, growing thinner and colder for ever as the dark expanded.

SIMULATION COMPLETE. NOTHING LEFT TO SIMULATE

Cal had seen that line once before, on the first day of the last series, when the simulator had built an atom by the rules of 1911 and watched it fall in on itself. Then it had been wrong. This time, a googol years from now, it might be right.

Might, because the simulator’s grade had changed long before the end. The glow of a black hole is RECONSTRUCTED: the physics is well understood, even if nobody has seen it. But a story told over a googol years assumes that nothing new turns up in all that time, that protons last or decay as the simulator guessed, that dark energy never changes, that there is no physics waiting to be found. Beyond about a hundred billion billion years, when the galaxy has scattered, the simulator called it SPECULATION, and it was right to.

Back to Teide

That night the sky over the plateau was clear, and there were more stars over Teide than either of them had ever seen. Every one of them, John knew now, was running down. And none of them, in the scale of things, had got very far.

The café was shut, but the light over the counter was on, and Albert let them in and made coffee, and set a plate of the day’s doughnuts between them. The far future hadn’t quite worn off. John found he was holding his cup in both hands, for the warmth.

“Come early tomorrow,” said Albert. “Before the sun is up. Some old friends of mine are coming, and I should like you to meet them together.”

Cal said nothing for a while. Then he reached into his shirt pocket and took out the napkin, folded small, with the three words on the outside in Albert’s handwriting, as they had been since the night under the stars, at the end of the last series, when he had folded the grain into it.

DO NOT EAT

He unfolded it on the table. In the crease, glittering and slightly crooked, was the grain of sugar.

“I asked what it was made of,” he said. “And then where it came from. I think we know.”

So they told it, the three of them, the whole story, in the light over the counter, while the stars went round outside.

Most of what the grain weighs isn’t the weight of anything in it. Its quarks and electrons are lumps of ripple in fields that fill the whole universe, and their own masses come to about a hundredth of the grain. The rest, nearly all of it, is energy: the energy of quarks rushing about inside its protons and neutrons, and of the field of the strong force that holds them, weighing what E = mc² says energy weighs. That was the last journey of the quantum series, What the Sugar Is Made Of.

Its protons were made before the universe was a second old, out of a hotter soup of quarks, in the hot, dense beginning of the second journey. Matter and antimatter had been made in pairs, and had wiped each other out almost exactly, as the third journey found, leaving one particle in a billion over. That one in a billion is the grain, and everything else. Every hydrogen atom in it, twenty-two to a molecule, has a nucleus that is a single one of those protons, which came through the oven of the first three minutes without being fused into anything. They are the oldest things on the table: 13.8 billion years old, older than every star.

For 380,000 years those protons had no electrons of their own. Then the glowing fog of the fourth journey cleared, at the surface of last scattering, and the electrons settled round them, and they became atoms of hydrogen.

Its carbon was made in a dying star. Three nuclei of helium had to meet, in the hot heart of an ageing giant, and two of them alone fall apart almost at once; it only works because carbon has a state at just the right energy to catch the third. Fred worked out in 1953 that the state must be there, from how much carbon there is in the universe, and he was right: the eleventh journey. Every carbon atom in the grain, twelve to a molecule, is three heliums that met in a star that has since died.

Its oxygen, eleven atoms to a molecule, was made late in the life of a massive star, carbon and helium joined in its furnace, and thrown out across space when the star blew itself apart, as the tenth journey and the eleventh found.

That gas, carbon and oxygen and the old hydrogen, drifted through the galaxy for billions of years, through generations of stars, until a cloud of it fell together into the twelfth journey’s disc, and the disc made the Sun and the Earth, and the Earth’s volcanoes breathed some of the carbon and oxygen out into its first air, as carbon dioxide, as the thirteenth found.

Its atoms have lasted all that time because an electron is a wave, held in by its nucleus, which can take only certain shapes, and has a lowest shape with nothing beneath it to fall to: Why the Atom Survives. And the grain is solid, and the atoms in it hold together as sugar, because electrons won’t share a place: they pair up, one up and one down, between neighbouring atoms, and hold the molecule together, and refuse to let the next one in: Room for Only Two.

“That’s every atom,” said John. “But not the sugar.”

“No,” said Albert. “Who made the sugar?”

It was a plant. Sugar is made in leaves, out of nothing more than air, water and light.

A leaf takes in carbon dioxide from the air, through tiny pores in its skin, and water from the ground, through its roots. In its cells is a green pigment, chlorophyll, which does what the first journey’s cool gas did to starlight: it absorbs certain colours, the reds and the blues, and lets the green through, which is why leaves are green. Each lump of red or blue light it catches, each photon, helps to pull apart molecules of water, and the energy is stored, step by step, in the bonds of a new molecule, built from the carbon and oxygen of the carbon dioxide and the hydrogen of the water. The water’s oxygen is let go as waste, into the air. The new molecules are sugars. Every breath of oxygen in the café was given off that way, by a plant or a microbe in the sea.

It’s photosynthesis, the trick the fourteenth journey found microbes learning in the young sea, which filled the air with oxygen. So the energy in the grain’s bonds, the energy Cal would get from eating it, is sunlight, caught by a leaf and stored. The chemist Melvin Calvin, whose Nobel Prize was toasted with champagne at Green Bank in the last journey, won it for working out the steps by which a leaf builds the carbon into sugar.

And the sunlight was made in the Sun’s core. Two protons can only fuse by tunnelling through the electric hill between them, as Through the Wall found; four of them, step by step, become a nucleus of helium that weighs about seven tenths of one per cent less than they did, and the difference comes out as energy, by E = mc², as The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe found. That energy didn’t come straight out. It was passed from one atom to the next, absorbed and given out again, a step at a time, through the Sun’s crowded insides, for about 170,000 years, as the ninth journey found, until it reached the surface and left as sunlight. Then it crossed to the Earth in eight minutes and twenty seconds, and fell on a leaf, and was caught.

“So it’s starlight,” said Cal. He looked at the grain. “I’ve been carrying round a bit of the Sun.”

“About three joules of it,” said John, who had worked it out on the edge of a clean napkin. “Eat it, and there’d be enough in it to lift you about four millimetres.”

The Van’s simulator, which had been listening through the window, had a view.

“Cane,” said Albert. “It says so on the packet.” He considered the grain. “The first sugar cane in the Americas went from these islands, with Columbus, on the same voyage as the first lemons.”

They looked at the grain for a long time. Its weight, the energy of fields. Its protons, from the first second. Its carbon, from a dying star; its oxygen, from a dead one. Its atoms, waves that couldn’t fall; its solidity, electrons that wouldn’t share. Its energy, sunlight, made by protons tunnelling in the Sun’s core, climbing out for 170,000 years, eight minutes to a leaf. Nearly fourteen billion years, and three series of journeys, and all of it in one glittering crumb, on a napkin, on a café table, on a volcano.

“And it’s going to end,” said John. “All of it. The stars, the galaxies, everything. A googol years.”

“Not tonight,” said Albert.

Cal licked the tip of his finger, and touched it to the grain, and the grain stuck to it. He looked at it once more, very closely, a crystal of sugar from the edge of a lemon doughnut, carrying the whole history of the universe. Then he put it in his mouth.

It tasted, very faintly, of lemon.
