The Sun’s future

The Sun’s future comes from computer models of a star of its mass and make-up, checked against the many stars like it seen at every stage of their lives. The numbers here are from one careful model, Schröder and Smith’s of 2008, its Table 1; others differ in the details, not the story.

Brightening. On the main sequence the core turns hydrogen into helium. Four protons become one helium nucleus, so the number of particles in the core falls, and to hold the Sun up against its own weight the core must squeeze and heat a little, which makes it burn faster. The brightness LLL climbs from today’s value, L⊙L_\odotL⊙​, to about 1.1 L⊙1.1\,L_\odot1.1L⊙​ in a billion years, 1.26 L⊙1.26\,L_\odot1.26L⊙​ in 2.5 billion and 1.84 L⊙1.84\,L_\odot1.84L⊙​ at the end of the main sequence, about 5.4 billion years from now.

The zone leaves the Earth. A planet at a distance ddd from a star of brightness LLL gets sunlight

so, in units of what the Earth gets today, S=(L/L⊙)/(d/1 AU)2S = (L/L_\odot)/(d/1\ \text{AU})^2S=(L/L⊙​)/(d/1 AU)2, and the zone’s edges are at d=(L/L⊙)/Sedged = \sqrt{(L/L_\odot)/S_{\text{edge}}}d=(L/L⊙​)/Sedge​​. For a star like the Sun, Kopparapu and colleagues (2014) put the inner edge, where the oceans would begin to run away into the air, at Sedge≈1.107S_{\text{edge}} \approx 1.107Sedge​≈1.107, and the outer edge at about 0.356. The red giant. At the tip of the red giant branch, 7.59 billion years from now, the model has the Sun at L=2,730 L⊙L = 2{,}730\,L_\odotL=2,730L⊙​ and a radius R=256 R⊙R = 256\,R_\odotR=256R⊙​. With 1 AU=215 R⊙1\ \text{AU} = 215\,R_\odot1 AU=215R⊙​, that’s

a deep orange-red, which is why it’s called a red giant.

The orbits widen. By then the Sun has lost 0.3320.3320.332 of its mass in its wind. The wind blows over hundreds of millions of years, far longer than a year, so each planet’s orbit changes slowly, keeping its angular momentum. For a circular orbit of radius aaa round a mass MMM, the angular momentum of a planet of mass mmm is mGMam\sqrt{GMa}mGMa​, so MaMaMa stays the same:

a=a0M/M⊙.a = \frac{a_0}{M/M_\odot}.a=M/M⊙​a0​​.

With M=0.668 M⊙M = 0.668\,M_\odotM=0.668M⊙​: Mercury’s orbit widens from 0.39 to 0.58 AU, Venus’s from 0.72 to 1.08 AU, and the Earth’s from 1 to 1.50 AU, half as wide again. The giant’s surface, at 1.19 AU, is past Mercury and Venus. It falls about 0.3 AU short of the Earth, but the Earth’s tidal effects on the giant, and its skimming through the giant’s thin outer gas, both drag it inwards. Schröder and Smith find that it is drawn in and swallowed when the Sun still has about 0.25 AU to grow, about 500,000 years before the tip. A planet would need to start at least 1.15 AU out to survive.

What’s left. The helium in the core lights at the tip, and the Sun settles at about 11 R⊙11\,R_\odot11R⊙​ and 54 L⊙54\,L_\odot54L⊙​ for about 130 million years, swells once more, and loses the rest of its envelope. The core left behind is a white dwarf of about 0.54 M⊙0.54\,M_\odot0.54M⊙​, the Earth’s size, held up by its electrons, as Lives and Deaths of Stars found.

The horizons’ distances

The far future of the universe’s expansion follows from the same model as Further Apart Every Day’s, a flat universe of matter and dark energy, here with Planck’s numbers: the expansion rate today H0=67.4H_0 = 67.4H0​=67.4 kilometres a second per megaparsec, matter Ωm=0.315\Omega_m = 0.315Ωm​=0.315 and dark energy ΩΛ=0.685\Omega_\Lambda = 0.685ΩΛ​=0.685 of the total. (The radiation, which mattered only in the first few hundred thousand years, is left out.) The universe’s scale, 1 today, grows with time as

which gives today’s age, where a=1a = 1a=1, as 13.8 billion years.

With Further Apart Every Day’s rounder numbers (70, 0.3 and 0.7) it comes to 15.9 billion; both are “about sixteen billion”.

Late on. Once matter has thinned out, dark energy is all that matters, and the scale grows by the same factor in every equal stretch of time:

and the event horizon settles at c/HΛ≈17.5c/H_\Lambda \approx 17.5c/HΛ​≈17.5 billion light-years: already there, to three figures, in a hundred billion years. By then the scale has grown about 320 times. A galaxy 54 million light-years away today, as far as Markarian’s Chain, carried along with the expansion, would be about 17 billion light-years off: at the horizon. (The Virgo Cluster’s own pull holds it back a little, so that’s rough; the farther galaxies go sooner.) Their light already on its way still arrives, but each step of its journey is stretched more than the last, so its waves grow longer, and its brightness falls, without limit.

The oldest light’s waves are that long when

The energy in a grain of sugar

How much. The grain weighs about a fifth of a milligram (What the Sugar Is Made Of). Sugar, sucrose, C12H22O11\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}C12​H22​O11​, gives out 5,644 kilojoules for every mole of it, 342.3 grams, burned to carbon dioxide and water, which is about 16.5 kilojoules a gram. The body, by slower steps, gets the same energy out of it. So the grain holds

E=2×10−4 g×16.5 kJ/g≈3.3 J.E = 2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{g} \times 16.5\ \text{kJ/g} \approx 3.3\ \text{J}.E=2×10−4 g×16.5 kJ/g≈3.3 J.

That would lift a person of 80 kilograms a height

h=Emg=3.3 J80 kg×9.81 m/s2≈4.2 mm.h = \frac{E}{mg} = \frac{3.3\ \text{J}}{80\ \text{kg} \times 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2} \approx 4.2\ \text{mm}.h=mgE​=80 kg×9.81 m/s23.3 J​≈4.2 mm.

(Muscles turn only about a quarter of what they burn into work, so in practice it’s nearer a millimetre.)
