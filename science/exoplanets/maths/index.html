How deep the dip is

A star’s light comes from its face, a disc of radius R⋆R_\starR⋆​. A planet of radius RpR_pRp​ in front of it blocks a disc of radius RpR_pRp​. If the star were equally bright all over its face, the share of light lost would be the share of the face covered:

δ=πRp2πR⋆2=(RpR⋆)2.\delta = \frac{\pi R_p^2}{\pi R_\star^2} = \left(\frac{R_p}{R_\star}\right)^2.δ=πR⋆2​πRp2​​=(R⋆​Rp​​)2.

Seen from close by. That holds when the planet and star are, to all intents, the same distance away. From a distance DDD from the star, with the planet a distance aaa from the star on our side of it, the planet is D−aD - aD−a away and looks larger against the star by D/(D−a)D/(D - a)D/(D−a):

The edge of the star. A real star is dimmer towards its edge (limb darkening), so the face isn’t evenly bright and the dip’s bottom is rounded, a little deeper in the middle than δ\deltaδ and shallower near its ends. Astronomers fit it with a law for how the brightness falls towards the edge; the figure leaves it out, as the article says.

How long it lasts, and how far out

The planet’s year. A planet of small mass in a circular orbit of radius aaa round a star of mass MMM is held in by gravity:

GMa2=v2a⇒v=GMa,P=2πav=2πa3GM.\frac{G M}{a^2} = \frac{v^2}{a} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{a}}, \qquad P = \frac{2\pi a}{v} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a^3}{G M}}.a2GM​=av2​⇒v=aGM​​,P=v2πa​=2πGMa3​​.

That is Kepler’s third law, P2∝a3/MP^2 \propto a^3/MP2∝a3/M; measuring PPP in years, aaa in astronomical units (the Earth’s distance from the Sun) and MMM in Suns, P2=a3/MP^2 = a^3/MP2=a3/M. Four times as far out, PPP is 43/2=84^{3/2} = 843/2=8 times as long; nine times, 93/2=279^{3/2} = 2793/2=27. Knowing MMM and measuring PPP gives a=(MP2)1/3a = (M P^2)^{1/3}a=(MP2)1/3.

The wobble’s speed

A star of mass MMM and a planet of mass mmm go round their balance point (their centre of mass), a distance aaa apart. Its distances from the two are in the inverse ratio of their masses, so the star goes round a circle of radius

a⋆=mM+m a,a_\star = \frac{m}{M + m}\, a,a⋆​=M+mm​a,

and the two go round each other at a relative speed G(M+m)/a\sqrt{G(M + m)/a}G(M+m)/a​, so the star moves at

v⋆=mM+mG(M+m)a.v_\star = \frac{m}{M + m} \sqrt{\frac{G (M + m)}{a}}.v⋆​=M+mm​aG(M+m)​​.

For Jupiter (m=1.90×1027m = 1.90 \times 10^{27}m=1.90×1027 kg, a=5.20a = 5.20a=5.20 AU) and the Sun: m/(M+m)=9.54×10−4m/(M + m) = 9.54 \times 10^{-4}m/(M+m)=9.54×10−4 and the relative speed is 13.06 km/s, so

v⊙=9.54×10−4×13,060 m/s=12.5 m/s,v_\odot = 9.54 \times 10^{-4} \times 13{,}060\ \text{m/s} = 12.5\ \text{m/s},v⊙​=9.54×10−4×13,060 m/s=12.5 m/s,

about the fastest sprinter’s top speed. The balance point is 9.54×10−4×778.59.54 \times 10^{-4} \times 778.59.54×10−4×778.5 million km =743,000= 743{,}000=743,000 km from the Sun’s centre, 1.07 times the Sun’s radius: just outside its surface. For the Earth, m/(M+m)=3.00×10−6m/(M + m) = 3.00 \times 10^{-6}m/(M+m)=3.00×10−6 and the relative speed 29.8 km/s, so the Sun moves at 8.9 cm/s.

For 51 Pegasi b, with a year of 4.2308 days round a star of about 1.1 Suns, Kepler’s law gives a=(1.1×0.011582)1/3=0.0528a = (1.1 \times 0.01158^2)^{1/3} = 0.0528a=(1.1×0.011582)1/3=0.0528 AU; a planet of 0.47 Jupiters there swings its star at about 55 m/s.

What’s measured. The star’s lines shift by the Doppler effect, by a share of their wavelength

Δλλ=vc,\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{v}{c},λΔλ​=cv​,

which for 12.5 m/s is 4.2×10−84.2 \times 10^{-8}4.2×10−8, one part in 24 million, and for 8.9 cm/s, one part in 3.4 billion. Only the speed along our line of sight shifts the lines, so an orbit tilted by an angle iii from face on shows v⋆sin⁡iv_\star \sin iv⋆​sini, and since iii is usually unknown, what is measured is msin⁡im \sin imsini: the least the planet’s mass can be.

Size and mass together. With RpR_pRp​ from the dip and mmm from the wobble, the planet’s density is ρ=m/43πRp3\rho = m/\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R_p^3ρ=m/34​πRp3​: the Earth’s is 5.5 grams a cubic centimetre, Saturn’s 0.69, less than water’s 1.

A planet’s air in its dip

Round a planet of radius RpR_pRp​, air with a scale height HHH (the height over which its pressure falls by a factor of eee) absorbs, at the colours of its gases’ lines, out to a few scale heights, nHn HnH. That adds a thin ring of area 2πRp nH2\pi R_p\, n H2πRp​nH to the planet’s dark disc, and so to the dip:

Δδ≈2Rp nHR⋆2,H=kTμ mH g,\Delta\delta \approx \frac{2 R_p\, n H}{R_\star^2}, \qquad H = \frac{k T}{\mu\, m_\text{H}\, g},Δδ≈R⋆2​2Rp​nH​,H=μmH​gkT​,
