The Van parks in space to watch Venus cross the face of the Sun, and the Sun hardly notices: a tenth of a per cent, and from far enough away, one part in thirteen thousand. That is how six thousand worlds round other stars have been found. By evening John and Cal have weighed planets nobody has ever seen, read the air of one of them, and visited seven more, with the simulator admitting that every view is a guess.

A black dot on the Sun

The Van had been parked in space since a little after breakfast, and nothing had happened yet.

Its windscreen had gone as dark as welding glass, as it did whenever the Sun was in front of it, and through it the Sun was a pale orange disc, its edge a little dimmer than its middle, about as wide as a pea held at arm’s length. Behind them, a long way off, the Earth was a small blue star with a fainter grey one beside it, the Moon. Ahead, so close to the Sun’s edge that it was hard to find in the glare, was a thin silver crescent: Venus, nearly between them and the Sun, with its night side turned their way.

The question had come up the evening before, at the end of the fourteenth journey. They had spent three days watching the Solar System born, the Earth made, and the Earth come alive, and Cal had looked up at the stars over the plateau and asked the obvious thing. If the Sun’s disc made planets, didn’t every star’s? And if so, where were they?

“You will not see them,” Albert had said. “A planet beside its star is a moth beside a lighthouse, a long way off. The lighthouse is all anyone sees.” He had thought about it for a while. “But a moth can fly in front of the lamp.”

So in the morning he had given them a flask of coffee, a paper bag of lemon doughnuts and a time and a place, and they had come to watch a moth fly in front of a lamp.

The time was the difficulty. Venus goes round the Sun inside the Earth’s orbit, and every year and seven months or so it passes between the Earth and the Sun. Nearly always it passes a little above or a little below the Sun’s disc, because its orbit is tilted slightly against the Earth’s, and nobody on the Earth sees anything. Only when the two line up exactly does Venus cross the Sun’s face, and from the Earth that happens in pairs eight years apart, more than a century between the pairs. The last two were in June 2004 and June 2012. In nine days’ time, on 24 October, Venus would pass between the Earth and the Sun again, and go by a little to one side of the Sun’s disc, as it nearly always does, and nobody on the Earth would see a thing.

John hadn’t wanted to wait ninety-one years. But a crossing only needs you to stand on the line through Venus and the Sun, and the Earth is the one place it can’t choose. Out in space, that line is always pointing somewhere. The Van’s navigation had worked out where it would point that morning, and taken them there.

Warning

Never look at the Sun without a proper solar filter, made for the job and fitted over the front of whatever you’re looking through, and never point binoculars, a telescope or a camera at it without one. A glimpse unprotected can blind you in an instant, and you won’t feel it happening. Transits of Venus and Mercury, like the partial phases of an eclipse, are watched through such a filter, or by projecting the Sun’s image onto a card.

At eleven minutes past the hour, by The Van’s clock, a small round notch appeared at the Sun’s left-hand edge. It grew into a black bite out of the bright disc, and within twenty minutes the whole of Venus was on the Sun: a perfectly round black dot, about a thirtieth of the Sun’s width, sliding very slowly across its face.

“It’s the blackest thing I’ve ever seen,” said Cal.

It was. A planet seen against its star is the night side of a whole world, and nothing in the sky is darker. For the next three hours they watched it creep across the Sun, drinking Albert’s coffee, while the dashboard graded the view.

VIEW FROM WINDOW: OBSERVED

“It’s out of the window,” said Cal to the dashboard. “Of course it’s observed.”

The Van had a light meter, one of the simulator’s instruments, which measured exactly how much sunlight fell on the windscreen. Before the crossing it had read a hundred per cent of the sunlight they started with. With Venus fully on the Sun, it read this:

SUNLIGHT: 99.9 PER CENT

“A tenth of a per cent,” said John. “A whole planet, the size of the Earth, and it takes a tenth of a per cent off the Sun.”

“From here,” said Cal. “We’re close to Venus. It looks big because it’s near.”

He was right, and it mattered. Venus is far smaller than the Sun. Here, at about the Earth’s distance from the Sun, it was more than three times nearer to The Van than the Sun was, and that made it look more than three times larger against the Sun than it really is. A planet round another star is seen from so far away that the planet and its star are, to all intents, the same distance off. Then a planet looks exactly as large against its star as it really is.

So John asked what seemed to him a simple question.

“What would this look like,” he said, “to someone round another star?”

The Sun as a star

To see the Sun as a stranger sees it, The Van had to go a long way out.

It went two hundred times as far from the Sun as the Earth is, five times as far as Pluto, so far that sunlight took more than a day to reach them. The Van’s navigation found them a place on the line through Venus and the Sun again, where a crossing was about to begin, and they parked there and looked back.

The Sun was a star. It was still by far the brightest thing in the sky, about ten times brighter than the full Moon looks from the Earth, bright enough to cast shadows inside The Van. But it had no face. It was a single point of light, far too small for any eye to see as a disc, and through the dark windscreen it looked like an ordinary star, only unbearably bright. Nothing could be seen crossing it, because there was nothing to see it cross.

“So from here,” said John, “nobody could ever see Venus at all.”

“They could see the light go down,” said Cal.

That was the whole idea. Nobody watching the Sun from another star can see Venus, or the Earth, or Jupiter; the planets are lost in the Sun’s glare, far too faint and far too close to it. But when one of them passes in front, it blocks some of the Sun’s light, and a careful enough watcher with a good enough light meter sees the star grow very slightly dimmer, and then, hours later, recover.

They didn’t have to wait long. A few minutes after the crossing began, the light meter had stopped falling, and needed more decimal places. This crossing would last about eleven hours, far longer than near the Earth, because so far out The Van was sitting still while Venus moved across the whole width of the Sun at its own speed, about 35 kilometres a second; they didn’t stay to see the end.

SUNLIGHT: 99.992 PER CENT

From two hundred times the Earth’s distance, Venus took about one part in thirteen thousand off the Sun. The Earth would take one part in twelve thousand. That is the size of the shadow every planet like ours casts on the light of its star, as seen by anybody who might be watching from somewhere else, and it is the size of the shadow astronomers have learned to see.

Measuring a shadow

Begin with something you already know: a star’s light comes from its face. Every part of the glowing surface you can see sends light your way, and the star’s brightness is all of it together. So when a planet slides in front and covers part of the face, the light from that part is blocked, and the star dims by just that share. A planet that covers a hundredth of the star’s face takes away a hundredth of its light. (Not quite exactly: a star is a little dimmer towards its edge than in the middle, as the Sun was through The Van’s windscreen, so the dip is a little shallower near the start and end of a crossing and a little deeper in the middle. That rounds off the dip’s corners, and astronomers allow for it.)

That gives the planet’s size, if you know the star’s. The share of the face covered is the share of the light lost, and the planet’s width is the star’s width times the square root of that share: a dip of one in ten thousand means a planet a hundredth of the star’s width, since a hundredth of the width covers a hundredth of a hundredth of the area. And the star’s size can be found without going there. The first journey found that a star’s colour gives its temperature, and the tenth that a star’s colour and brightness together place it among the stars of its kind, and give its size and its mass. So a dip in the light of a star, carefully measured, gives the width of a world nobody has seen.

Exoplanet

A planet going round a star other than the Sun. The first round a star like the Sun was found in 1995, and by October 2026 there were 6,445 confirmed, of almost every size and kind, from worlds smaller than the Earth to giants many times heavier than Jupiter. Almost none has been seen directly: nearly all were found by what they do to their star’s light. The word is short for “extrasolar planet”, a planet outside the Solar System.

The dip comes back. A planet goes round and round, and every time it comes round to the same place it crosses again, so the dips repeat, once a year for the planet’s year. The time between them is how long the planet takes to go round its star.

And from that comes its distance from the star. In 1619 Johannes Kepler found the law the Sun’s planets keep: the farther out a planet is, the longer its year, by a fixed rule. Four times as far out, a planet takes eight times as long to go round; nine times as far out, twenty-seven times as long. It’s set by the star’s pull, so a heavier star swings its planets round faster at the same distance. Know the star’s mass, from its colour and brightness, and time the dips, and the rule gives how far out the planet goes round, and so how much warmth it gets from its star. How long each dip lasts is a check: a planet farther out moves more slowly, and takes longer to cross.

So one repeating dip in a star’s light, and nothing else, tells you the size of a planet, the length of its year, and its distance from its star.

Transit method

Finding a planet by the small, regular dip in its star’s brightness as the planet passes in front of it, a “transit”. How deep the dip is gives the planet’s size compared with its star; how often it comes back gives the planet’s year, and so its distance from the star. It only works for the few planets whose orbits happen to be lined up edge on to us, so that they cross their star’s face as seen from here. Most planets known were found this way.

Lined up, or not

There’s a catch, and Cal saw it on the way back in.

“We had to go and find the one place where Venus crosses,” he said. “Anyone sitting anywhere else would never see it.”

That’s the price of the method. A planet only crosses its star as seen from directions close to the plane of its orbit. Think of the planet’s shadow, thrown out into space by its star: as the planet goes round, the shadow sweeps round with it, a thin band across the sky, as wide as the star seen from the planet. Only an astronomer somewhere inside that band ever sees a crossing. The closer the planet is to its star, the bigger its star looks from the planet and the wider the band; the farther out, the thinner.

For the Earth, the band is thin. From a star chosen at random, the chance of being lined up to see the Earth cross the Sun is about one in two hundred. For Jupiter, five times as far out, it’s about one in a thousand. So for every planet anyone sees cross its star, there must be a great many more, round other stars, whose crossings are seen only by somebody else, or nobody. There’s no way round it, and it’s why the counts of planets found by their shadows have to be multiplied up: the planets found are the lucky few that happen to face us edge on.

The answer to the catch is to watch a great many stars at once. From 2009 to 2018 the Kepler space telescope, named after him, did just that. It stared at one patch of the sky, among the stars of Cygnus and Lyra, about the size of a fist held at arm’s length, measuring the brightness of about 150,000 stars every half hour for four years, and looking for dips. Over its working life it found more than 2,600 planets, and thousands more candidates still being checked. Its successor, TESS, launched in 2018, surveys nearly the whole sky, looking at nearer, brighter stars for a month or so at a time; in October 2026 the number of confirmed planets found with its data passed a thousand.

Each needs its dips to repeat. One dip could be anything: a speck of noise, a spot on the star, a satellite. Astronomers like to see a dip at least three times, evenly spaced and the same depth each time, before they trust it. For a planet like the Earth, round a star like the Sun, the dip is so shallow that even three crossings don’t quite lift it clear of the jitter in the measurements: it takes about four, three years after the first, which is why Kepler was built to watch for four years.

Try it: a doughnut in front of a lamp

You can measure a transit yourself, at a table. Many phones will tell you how bright the light falling on them is, with a free light-meter app that reads the little sensor beside the screen’s camera. Put a lamp with a round, frosted shade at one end of a table, in a dark room, and the phone at the other end, its sensor facing the lamp, and note the reading. Then move a ball slowly across between them, in front of the lamp, and watch the number dip as the ball crosses and come back as it leaves. The bigger the ball, the deeper the dip.

John did it that evening with the café’s reading lamp, and a doughnut.

The dip came, and recovered, and had a curious little bump in the middle of it, where the reading rose for a moment before falling again. Cal said he could think of only one explanation, and declined to give it. (Planets with rings, seen side on, make dips of strange shapes too, and astronomers have looked hard for them.)

The wobble

The first planet ever found round a star like the Sun wasn’t found by its shadow at all. It was found by the way it swung its star about.

Everything that goes round goes round together. Think of a father swinging a small child round by the hands, in the park: the child flies out in a wide circle, but the father isn’t standing still in the middle. He leans back, and his feet trace a small circle of their own, always on the opposite side from the child. Both of them are turning about a point between them, nearer the heavier one. It’s the same for a star and a planet. The planet’s pull on the star is exactly as strong as the star’s on the planet, so as the planet goes round, the star moves too, in a much smaller circle, always on the far side of a balance point between them.

The Sun does it. Jupiter, the heaviest of the planets, has about a thousandth of the Sun’s mass, and the point the two go round together is a thousandth of the way out to Jupiter: just outside the Sun’s surface. So the Sun swings round a small circle once every twelve years, Jupiter’s year, moving at about 12 metres a second. That’s the top speed of the fastest sprinter ever timed. The Earth, three hundred times lighter than Jupiter and much closer in, swings the Sun round at about 9 centimetres a second, the amble of a giant tortoise.

How could anyone see a star move at a sprinter’s speed, from many light-years away? Watching it shift sideways against the other stars, from light-years away, is far harder. But they can see it move towards them and away from them. In Chasing a Beam of Light, John and Cal found that light from something coming towards you is squeezed, its waves arriving closer together, so that it looks a little bluer, and light from something going away is stretched a little redder: the Doppler effect. And the first journey found that every star’s light is crossed by dark lines, the fingerprints of its atoms, each at its own exact colour. When a planet swings its star towards us, every line in the star’s light shifts very slightly towards the blue; half its year later, as it swings it away, towards the red. The lines rock to and fro, once a year for the planet’s year.

The shift is tiny. For a star moving at a sprinter’s speed, the lines move by about one part in twenty-four million of their own wavelength. To measure it, a spectrograph spreads the starlight into its colours, with the lines across them, so finely that a single line covers many pixels of its camera, and then measures where thousands of lines have moved to, all at once, while comparing them with lines from a lamp of known colours shining through the same instrument. In 1995 the best could measure a star’s speed to about 10 metres a second.

That was enough. Michel Mayor and his student Didier Queloz, of the University of Geneva, had built a spectrograph called ELODIE for the observatory at Haute-Provence, in the south of France, and with it they found that the star 51 Pegasi, about fifty light-years away and much like the Sun, was rocking to and fro at about 55 metres a second, about 200 kilometres an hour, once every 4.2 days. Something at least half the mass of Jupiter was going round it in four days. They announced it on 6 October 1995.

51 PEGASI B: OBSERVED. BY ITS STAR’S WOBBLE

Nobody had expected anything like it. A planet as heavy as half of Jupiter, going round its star in four days, must be very close to it, about a twentieth of the Earth’s distance from the Sun: far closer than Mercury is to the Sun. And the twelfth journey had found that giant planets can only grow far out, beyond the frost line, where ice survives to build them. So 51 Pegasi b must have formed far out and moved in, dragged inwards by the disc it was born in, which theorists had worked out could happen but nobody had expected to go so far. It was the first “hot Jupiter”, and many more have been found since; they are the easiest planets to find, because they swing their stars hardest and most often, though they turn out to be rare. Mayor and Queloz were awarded half of the Nobel Prize in Physics for it in 2019.

Radial velocity method

Finding a planet by the to-and-fro motion it gives its star, as the two go round their balance point together. The star’s dark lines shift slightly towards the blue as it moves towards us and towards the red as it moves away, once round for each of the planet’s years (the Doppler effect). How far they shift gives the star’s speed, and from that the planet’s mass, or at least the least it can be, since the orbit’s tilt is usually unknown. “Radial velocity” is a star’s speed along our line of sight, towards or away from us.

Notice which way the lines go when the planet is coming towards you: the star is always going the other way.

Spectrographs have improved since 1995. One on La Silla, in Chile, since 2003, measures stars’ speeds to about a metre a second, and one on Paranal, since 2018, to better than ten centimetres a second over an hour. But over years its measurements drift by several times that, and a star’s own surface, boiling and ringing as the ninth journey found the Sun’s does, rocks its lines by more than an Earth would. No planet like the Earth, going round a star like the Sun once a year, has yet been found by its wobble.

Size and weight together

The two methods together do something neither can alone. The dips give a planet’s size; the wobble gives its mass. Put them together, and you know how much matter is packed into how much room: its density. That tells you, without ever seeing it, what kind of world it is.

A planet as dense as rock, about five times as dense as water, as the Earth is, is made of rock and metal. One as light as Saturn, which would float in water if you could find a bath big enough, is mostly gas. Many of the planets found are between the two: a little bigger than the Earth and a few times heavier, perhaps rock with thick air, perhaps rock wrapped in deep oceans or ice, perhaps small balls of gas round a rocky core. The Solar System has nothing of the kind, and yet planets of that size, between the Earth’s and Neptune’s, are the commonest kind found. Nobody yet knows why the Sun didn’t make one.

Other ways to find a world

Shadows and wobbles have found most of them, but not all.

The very first planets ever found outside the Solar System, in 1992, went round a dead star. Aleksander Wolszczan and Dale Frail, with the great radio telescope at Arecibo, in Puerto Rico, were timing the pulses of a pulsar, the spinning remnant of a star that exploded (the tenth journey), which flashes so regularly that it keeps time almost as well as an atomic clock. Its pulses came a little early, then a little late, in a pattern that repeated: the pulsar was being swung about by two planets, and a third was found later, the smallest about twice the mass of the Moon. Nobody knows how planets come to be round a star that blew itself apart; perhaps they formed afterwards, from the wreckage.

Some are found by lensing. In Starlight Bent by the Sun, John and Cal found that mass bends light, and that a star passing almost exactly in front of a more distant star acts as a lens, brightening the star behind for a few weeks as the two drift into line. If the lens star has a planet, the planet adds a brief flicker of its own, a few hours or days long, to the brightening. It only happens once: the stars drift on and never line up again. But it finds planets nothing else can, far out from their stars, and even planets wandering through space with no star at all. The first found this way was announced in 2004.

And a few have been photographed. It’s very hard. In visible light the Sun outshines the Earth about ten billion times, and seen from the nearest star the two would be no farther apart than the width of a coin seen from five kilometres away. But young giant planets are still hot from being made, and glow in the infrared, as Red Hot, White Hot found every warm thing does, and in the infrared the gap is far smaller. With a mask in the telescope to blot out the star’s own light, in 2008 a team photographed three giant planets round the young star HR 8799, about 130 light-years away, and found a fourth two years later; they have been photographed every year since, and you can watch them go round. The James Webb Space Telescope has photographed others.

The biggest transit anyone on the Earth can see, incidentally, is the Moon crossing the Sun. The Moon is far smaller than the Sun, but so much closer that it looks almost exactly the same size, and when it crosses exactly in front, at a total eclipse, it takes away all the Sun’s light from its face and leaves only the faint glow round it. John and Cal saw one from Soria, in Spain, in August 2026.

Six thousand worlds

The count is kept by NASA’s Exoplanet Archive, in California, and it goes up most weeks. On 8 October 2026 it reached 6,445 confirmed planets, round more than 4,500 stars.

They’re more varied than anyone expected, and the Solar System turns out to be only one way of arranging planets among many. There are hot Jupiters, going round their stars in days, so hot that some have iron vapour in their air. There are planets going round two stars at once, which would see two sunsets. There are worlds of lava, so close to their stars that their rock is molten, and families of small planets packed so close together that each would see the next as large as the full Moon, or larger. There are planets with no star at all.

The counts say something about the galaxy as a whole, once they’ve been multiplied up for planets turned the wrong way to us, or too small or too far out to find yet: on average, every star in the Milky Way has at least one planet. There are more planets in our galaxy than there are stars, and the stars number a few hundred billion.

Reading their air

Finding a planet’s size and mass is a great deal. But in the last twenty-five years astronomers have learned to do something more, which the first journey made possible: they read what a planet’s air is made of, from light that has passed through it.

When a planet crosses its star, most of the starlight it blocks is simply stopped by the planet’s body. But if the planet has air, a thin ring of it surrounds the dark disc, lit from behind, and some starlight passes through that ring on its way to us. As it does, the atoms and molecules in the air absorb their own colours, exactly as the cool gas in front of a star’s hot surface did in the first journey. So at those colours, and only at those, the planet’s air is dark, and the planet looks very slightly bigger: the dip is a little deeper. Measure the dip colour by colour, and the colours where it deepens are the fingerprints of what the air is made of.

The first planet seen to cross its star, HD 209458 b, a hot Jupiter about 160 light-years away in Pegasus, was caught crossing its star in September 1999; its dip was a little under two per cent. Two years later, with the Hubble Space Telescope, a team measured that dip colour by colour round the yellow lines of sodium, the colour of La Palma’s old streetlights, and found it deeper there, by about two hundredths of one per cent. There was sodium in the air of a planet no one had ever seen, 160 light-years away. Since then the James Webb Space Telescope, which sees in the infrared, where molecules have most of their fingerprints, has found water, carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide and, in the air of the giant planet WASP-39 b in 2022, carbon dioxide, a clear fingerprint seen in a planet’s air for the first time.

For small, rocky planets it’s far harder. Their ring of air is thin, a few thousandths of the planet’s width, and a planet like the Earth crossing a star like the Sun would change the dip at its air’s colours by about a millionth of the star’s light. In June 2012 astronomers tried the method on Venus itself. The Hubble Space Telescope can’t be pointed at the Sun, so they pointed it at the Moon instead, using it as a mirror for the sunlight, and watched the transit of Venus in the reflected light, as if Venus were a planet round another star. Only about a hundred-thousandth of the light had passed through Venus’s air.

Seven worlds round a small star

Small stars help. A planet the Earth’s size crossing a star a tenth of the Sun’s width covers a hundred times as much of its face, and makes a dip a hundred times deeper. And the smallest, coolest stars, which are by far the commonest kind (the seventeenth journey comes back to them), are so dim that a planet has to go round very close to be warm, which makes its year short and its crossings frequent.

The best example is a small, cool, red star about 40 light-years away in Aquarius, called TRAPPIST-1, after the small telescope at La Silla, in Chile, that found its first planets in 2016. (The telescope’s name, worked out by a team from Liège, in Belgium, is a nod to the beer the country’s Trappist monks brew.) In February 2017 the team announced that the star had seven planets, every one about the size of the Earth, crossing its face over and over. The star has less than a tenth of the Sun’s mass and is not much bigger than Jupiter. All seven planets go round it far closer than Mercury goes round the Sun: the outermost is six times closer to its star than Mercury is to ours. The innermost goes round in a day and a half; the outermost in under nineteen days. Each makes a dip of around half a per cent, about sixty times deeper than the Earth’s in the Sun’s light. And three of them are at distances from their star where, if they have air, they could be neither too hot nor too cold for water to stay liquid, which is the next journey’s subject.

John asked the simulator to take them there.

The screen showed the little star, dull red and huge in the sky, and seven worlds round it. The simulator chose the fourth, TRAPPIST-1 e, and put them on its surface: a rocky plain under a dim red sun that never moved, because a planet so close to its star is expected to keep one face to it always, as the Moon keeps one face to the Earth. Across the sky hung the next planet out, as large as the full Moon, and another beyond it, a pale crescent. Below the readout, the simulator added a line it had not been asked for.

TRAPPIST-1 E, AS SHOWN: SPECULATION. NOBODY KNOWS IF IT HAS AIR

“Then what is it showing us?” said John.

“A guess,” said Cal. “With a red sun in it.”

It was honest. Everything known about TRAPPIST-1 e is its size, its mass, its year and its distance from its star, worked out from its dips, and from the way its six neighbours tug it slightly early and late across its star’s face. Whether it has air at all is the question Webb has been asked, and so far Webb has said: not a thick blanket of hydrogen, and probably not a thick blanket of carbon dioxide, but beyond that it can’t yet tell. The two innermost planets seem to have little or no air at all. The star flares, and the spots on its face confuse the colours of the dips. More crossings are being watched.

Back to Teide

They were back in the car park by the evening, and the plateau was in shadow, the last of the light on the top of the mountain. The Van’s windscreen had cleared. Albert came out to meet them, and they told him about the black dot, and the two hundred times the Earth’s distance, and the seven worlds round the small red star, and he listened with the air of a man hearing about a place he had always meant to visit.

“And the doughnuts?” he said.

“Eaten,” said Cal. “Somewhere past Pluto.”

They sat on the terrace wall while the stars came out. The stars were the same as always, but they looked different now. John had grown up thinking of them as lamps hung in an empty sky. Now each one he looked at was, in all likelihood, somebody’s sun, with worlds going round it that nobody here could see: hot giants skimming their stars, rocky worlds in thick air, ice worlds far out in the dark, families of small planets packed close together. Six thousand of them had been found by their shadows and their wobbles, and there must be hundreds of billions more in the Milky Way alone.

And somewhere among those stars, it occurred to him, someone with a good enough telescope, if anyone was there, might be watching the Sun grow very slightly dimmer, once a year, for thirteen hours, by one part in twelve thousand.

Cal had been thinking the same thing, by the look of him. He patted his shirt pocket, where the grain of sugar was still folded in Albert’s napkin, the words on the outside.

“Thousands of worlds,” he said. “Billions, probably. Is anyone there?”

Albert didn’t answer at once. He looked out across the dark plateau, towards the mountain, and the old lava at its foot.

“You have spent two days on the only world where we know the answer,” he said at last. “Tomorrow, look at it the way they would. From a very long way off.”
