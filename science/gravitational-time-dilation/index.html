One atomic clock stays near the café. Another goes to the coast. The difference is tiny, real, and much more unsettling than any malfunction on The Van's dashboard.

Fantastic Journeys with John and Cal: A Traveller's Guide to Space and Time, part 9 of 14

Two clocks and a mountain

Albert brought the clocks out in polished wooden cases, each carefully labelled.

One said UP. The other said DOWN.

“It’s a good thing you marked them,” Cal said. “Otherwise we might forget which way gravity works.”

Albert ignored this, which was one of his talents. He set the two cases side by side on the table, between the coffee cups and the plate on which, as usual, two lemon doughnuts were waiting to be discussed.

They were atomic clocks, and very good ones. An ordinary clock counts the swings of something that swings regularly: a pendulum, or a sliver of quartz that vibrates when a current runs through it. An atomic clock counts something steadier still. The atoms of an element absorb light only at certain exact frequencies, the same for every atom of that element anywhere in the universe, so a clock can tune itself to one of those frequencies and count the waves. The world’s second is defined this way: 9,192,631,770 waves of the particular microwave radiation that caesium atoms absorb. Albert’s clocks, he said, were rather better than caesium, and nobody felt like asking where he had got them.

Each case had three other things besides the clock. A small lamp in its lid flashed once every second, by that clock. A sensor beside the lamp noted, to a tiny fraction of a second, when it saw a flash from the other clock. And a little logbook, built into the case, recorded how high the clock was and how fast it was moving, all the time, wherever it went.

“They’ve been sitting next to each other under the counter for a week,” said Albert. “In that time they have not drifted apart by so much as a billionth of a second.” He turned both cases round so that John and Cal could see the displays. The numbers ticked over together, perfectly in step.

“Take one down to the sea,” he said. “Leave the other here. Then bring it back and compare them properly.”

“Will the one at the top run faster?” John asked.

“Yes,” said Albert. “If you compare them properly.”

“But why?”

Albert smiled. “That is why you are taking the clocks.”

Outside, The Van’s engine began coughing like an elderly farm vehicle attempting a particularly steep hill. Cal looked at the long road winding down from the plateau, then at the mountain.

“Couldn’t we take an ordinary car?”

“Where would be the adventure in that?” asked John.

“Fewer warning lights, for a start.”

Down to the sea

The Van could, on a good day, carry two people to the far side of the Moon in less time than it takes to boil its kettle. For this journey it was asked to do something much harder: drive slowly down a mountain road without doing anything unexpected.

Albert had been firm about that. A clock flown through the sky at speed, he said, would be measuring the journey as much as the destination. Better to take the road, gently, and let the logbook keep track of everything else.

Cal carried the clock marked DOWN out to The Van and strapped it into the passenger seat. As he did, a new message appeared on the dashboard.

UNAUTHORISED ACCURACY DETECTED

“I think it’s jealous,” said Cal.

The road from the plateau down to the coast is long and winding, through pine forest and then through villages and farmland, and The Van took it at a stately pace. The speedometer reported 17 mph throughout, uphill, downhill, round hairpin bends and once, memorably, while stopped at a junction. Fortunately, the clock’s own logbook was keeping a far more reliable record of its height and speed than anything on The Van’s dashboard.

They found a quiet spot by the sea, a few metres above the waves, and set the clock down on a flat rock where it could sit undisturbed. Then they waited. Albert had asked for a whole day and a night at the coast, so they swam, ate, read, argued about whether The Van’s engine noise counted as a mechanical fault or a personality, and slept.

In the morning Cal noticed the clock on The Van’s dashboard. It said 07:52. His watch said 07:31.

“Is that relativity?” he asked.

“No,” said John. “That’s The Van.”

The dashboard clock had never agreed with any other clock in its life. It gained several minutes a day, sometimes forwards, occasionally backwards, and once it had shown the time in what appeared to be Roman numerals. It was, in other words, simply broken.

They drove the clock back up the mountain, as gently as they had brought it down, and set its case beside its twin on Albert’s table.

The two displays no longer agreed.

The clock that had stayed at the café, the one marked UP, was ahead. Not by much. When Albert had read both logbooks and allowed for the hours on the road, the difference came to about twenty billionths of a second: twenty nanoseconds.

John checked the readout and whistled. “So anyone who stays up here gets older faster?”

“By a tiny amount,” said Cal. “Not enough to excuse your behaviour.”

Is the difference real?

It would be very easy to shrug at twenty nanoseconds. Clocks drift, batteries run down, and things get jostled on mountain roads. Most people’s first instinct, on being told that two clocks disagree, is that one of them must be faulty, and that’s a perfectly sensible instinct, because almost every disagreement between clocks in ordinary life is just that. The Van’s dashboard clock is a fine example.

Why compare them side by side?

First, notice how the comparison was made. A reading phoned up from the coast, or any signal sent up the mountain, takes time to travel, and then you have to argue about how long it took. So John and Cal carried the clock back and set it beside the other one. Two clocks on the same table, read at the same moment, can be compared without any argument at all. Whatever the difference is, it’s real, and both clocks agree about it.

Each clock had simply measured the time that passed for it, along its own journey: what A Clock Made of Light called its proper time. One clock’s journey stayed at the café the whole time. The other’s went down to the sea, stayed there a day and a night, and came back. They set off together, they met again, and they had recorded different amounts of time in between.

Was the clock at the coast faulty?

A faulty clock disagrees with the clocks around it. The Van’s dashboard clock disagreed with Cal’s watch, with the café’s clock, with every clock it ever met. If John and Cal had set a second atomic clock beside the one on the rock at the coast, the two would have kept perfect step all day and all night. Down by the sea, nothing about the clock looked odd. A second there felt like a second, measured like a second, and lasted exactly as long as a second always does.

The clock wasn’t damaged by the drive, either. The logbook recorded every bend of the road, and the small effects of the journey itself were allowed for. Moving clocks run slow, as A Clock Made of Light showed, but at road speeds the effect is thousands of times smaller than what was measured, and the hours spent on the road at in-between heights are just arithmetic. And it wasn’t the sea air or the temperature: a good atomic clock is built to shrug off both, and anyway, they would have affected a second clock beside it at the coast in the same way, and the two would still have agreed.

What remained was this: down at the coast, everything ran slightly slower than up at the café, atomic clocks and all. Pendulums, quartz watches, heartbeats, the rate at which a lemon doughnut goes stale: every process, of every kind, at the coast, kept pace with the atomic clock beside it. Nobody at the coast could ever notice, because everything they could compare it with was slowed by exactly the same amount. The difference only shows when you compare clocks at different heights.

Gravitational time dilation

Clocks lower down in a gravitational field run slower than clocks higher up, when the two are compared. It isn’t a fault in the clocks: every kind of clock, and every process, runs at the same slower rate lower down, so nobody there can notice anything. The difference shows only when clocks at different heights are compared, for instance by bringing them back together. Near the Earth’s surface the effect is very small: a clock raised by one metre runs faster by about one part in ten million billion.

How small is small?

Twenty nanoseconds in a day is a fraction of about two parts in ten million million, a number that is hard to picture.

A good quartz wristwatch is accurate to within about half a second a day. That sounds very good, and it is, but its daily error is about twenty-five million times larger than the difference the clocks found. No wristwatch, however carefully you carried it up and down the mountain, would show you anything at all, except its own drift.

A nanosecond is a billionth of a second. In twenty nanoseconds, light travels about six metres: the length of The Van.

And over a lifetime? Someone who lived eighty years a couple of kilometres up a mountain, while their twin lived those eighty years at the coast, would end up older by about half a thousandth of a second. It wouldn’t show on anybody’s birthday cake.

The amount depends on the height difference. The café is a couple of kilometres above the sea, and the exact figure would also depend on the precise heights, the shape of the Earth’s gravity around Tenerife, and even the Earth’s spin. So the twenty nanoseconds in this story is an illustration of the right size; a measurement at the café would give its own figure.

The figure below lets you run the experiment yourself, with as much patience as you like. One clock stays at the coast, at sea level. The other you can carry up the mountain, from the shore to the summit, by dragging it or with the slider. Choose how long to leave them, a day, a month or a year, and let the time run: the upper clock pulls ahead, and the table at the bottom gives the difference in nanoseconds, the distance light travels in that time, and how far a good wristwatch drifts in the same time.

Put the upper clock at the café, about two kilometres up, and leave it for a day: about nineteen nanoseconds. Leave it for a year and the difference grows to about seven microseconds (seven millionths of a second). Then bring it down to the shore, and watch the difference shrink to nothing: two clocks at the same height keep the same time. Halve the height and the difference halves: near the Earth, it grows in proportion to the height.

Measured with real clocks

You don’t need a mountain, or a café owner with a cupboard full of atomic clocks, to see this.

In September 2005, Tom Van Baak, an American enthusiast for precise timekeeping, took three caesium atomic clocks, and his three children, on a family trip up Mount Rainier, near Seattle. They parked about 1,650 metres up and stayed for two days, while more atomic clocks stayed at home. The prediction was that the travelling clocks would come home about 22 nanoseconds ahead. On average, they came home about 23 nanoseconds ahead. The laboratory for this experiment was the family minivan.

Professional laboratories have gone much further. In 2010, physicists at the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) compared two clocks, each based on a single aluminium ion, and raised one of them by just 33 centimetres: the higher clock ran faster, by the amount predicted. In 2020, a team in Japan put one clock at the bottom of the Tokyo Skytree and another about 450 metres up, on its observation floor, and measured the difference precisely. And in 2022, physicists at JILA, in Colorado, measured the effect across a single cloud of about 100,000 strontium atoms, between the top and the bottom of the cloud, about a millimetre apart. The difference in rate across that millimetre was about one part in ten billion billion.

This means, strictly speaking, that your head is ageing faster than your feet. Over eighty years, for someone of ordinary height, by about half a microsecond. It’s probably not worth adjusting your skincare routine.

Why should height matter?

Back at the café, John was not satisfied.

“I believe the clocks,” he said. “I just don’t see why. Nothing at the coast was different. Nothing up here is different. Why should a clock care how high it is?”

“We could ask The Van,” said Cal.

John looked at him. Cal was not usually the one who suggested asking The Van anything.

“Remember the rooms with the blinds down?” Cal went on. “With its engine pushing at one g in empty space, The Van was just like The Van parked in the car park. From inside, we couldn’t tell the difference.”

He was remembering the lesson of Einstein’s Happiest Thought: the equivalence principle. In a small, closed room, no experiment can tell the difference between standing still on the ground and being pushed steadily through empty space by an engine.

“So,” said Cal, “if clocks behave strangely at different heights on Teide, they ought to behave strangely at different heights in The Van. And in The Van, we can see what’s going on.”

Albert, who had been listening, set the two clocks side by side again until they agreed to the last digit, and handed them over without a word.

The experiment in The Van

The Van left the car park with a noise like a goods train shunting in a tunnel, climbed through the atmosphere, and stopped in an empty stretch of space, well away from the Earth.

Before anything else, Cal fed the battered probe from the first journey out through the airlock. It drifted a few metres off and stopped, ready to watch from outside.

Last time, they had flown The Van floor first, like a lift, which gave them a room about two metres high. This time John wanted as much height as he could get. So he pointed The Van’s nose away from the probe and set the engine to push steadily, just hard enough to imitate the Earth’s gravity. Everything loose in the cabin slid to the back. With the engine pushing from behind, the back wall of the cabin had become the floor, and the front of the cabin, about six metres away, had become the ceiling. Cal climbed up to it, by way of a shelf that had become a ladder, with the clock marked UP. John stayed on the floor with the clock marked DOWN. Outside, the probe dwindled behind them, blinking, as The Van pulled away from it, faster and faster.

“Flashes,” said John.

His clock’s lamp began to flash up the cabin, once every second by his clock. At the ceiling, Cal’s clock noted the moment each flash arrived, by Cal’s clock.

The flashes arrived slightly more than a second apart. Only very slightly: over six metres, by about six and a half parts in ten million billion. But Albert’s clocks could see it, and it was the same for every flash.

Then Cal sent flashes down, once a second by his clock, and John’s clock found that they arrived slightly less than a second apart, by the same tiny fraction.

“Your clock’s slow,” Cal called down.

“Or yours is fast,” said John. “Or something’s happening to the light on the way.”

What the probe saw

The probe, floating behind them in empty space with no engine running, saw a simpler story.

From the probe, The Van was speeding up the whole time. Each flash left the floor, crossed the cabin and arrived at the ceiling, and that took a little time: about 20 billionths of a second for six metres. While the flash was crossing, The Van sped up a little more. So when a flash arrived, the ceiling was moving away from the probe slightly faster than the floor had been moving at the moment the flash set off.

The floor sends a flash every second. Each flash then has to catch a ceiling that is running away from it a little faster than the floor was running when the flash left. A receiver that is moving away from the source of a signal sees the signal stretched out: each flash has slightly further to travel than the one before, so the flashes arrive further apart than they were sent. That’s the Doppler effect, which Chasing a Beam of Light explored with sound and with light. It’s why an ambulance’s siren drops in pitch as it races away from you.

Sending flashes down reverses it. Now the floor is rushing up to meet each flash a little faster than the ceiling was moving when it sent it, so the flashes are squeezed together and arrive slightly closer than a second apart.

So, seen from outside, the whole effect came from an accelerating Van and the ordinary Doppler effect.

Now put The Van back on the mountain

By the equivalence principle, nothing inside The Van could tell the engine’s steady push from standing on the ground. If flashes sent up the cabin arrive stretched out, then flashes sent up a tower on Teide, or up the mountain itself, must arrive stretched out too, by the same amount for the same height. Flashes sent down must arrive squeezed together.

Einstein followed essentially this line of thought, in 1907 and again in 1911, years before any clock was good enough to test it. Light that climbs out of a gravitational field arrives with its rhythm stretched; light that falls into one arrives with its rhythm squeezed. For light, a stretched rhythm means a lower frequency, and lower-frequency light is redder.

Gravitational redshift

Light (or any signal) that climbs upwards in a gravitational field arrives with a slightly lower frequency than it had when it set off, as measured by clocks at each end: its waves, or its flashes, arrive further apart than they were sent. Lower frequency means redder light, hence the name. Light sent downwards arrives with a higher frequency: a gravitational blueshift. Near the Earth’s surface the shift is tiny, about one part in ten million billion for every metre of height.

Counting the flashes

On the mountain, though, there’s a puzzle. In The Van, the explanation was that The Van was speeding up. On Teide, as far as anyone standing on it can see, nothing is speeding up: the mountain sits there, day after day, unchanged. (The accelerometer on the café table, steady at one g, may have its own view about that; the next journey hears it out. The argument here doesn’t depend on it.) So why should flashes sent up the mountain arrive stretched out?

Imagine a clock at the coast sending a flash up the mountain once a second, and a clock at the café receiving them. Because nothing on the mountain changes from one moment to the next, every flash takes exactly as long to climb as the one before it. The flashes can’t bunch up or spread out on the way, and none of them gets lost or added. If the clock at the bottom sends a million flashes, the clock at the top receives a million flashes, the first one arriving a certain time after it was sent and the last one arriving the same time after it was sent.

Now count. The bottom clock sent its million flashes in a million of its seconds. The top clock, receiving them, finds them spread over slightly more than a million of its seconds: about a fifth of a microsecond more, for two kilometres. So between the same first and last flashes, the same stretch of history, the top clock counted more seconds than the bottom clock did.

There’s only one way that can happen: the top clock really does run faster than the bottom one. By the count of any one clock, the flashes arrived exactly as often as they left; it is the clocks that differ, and the one at the top counts more seconds between the same two flashes.

So gravitational redshift and gravitational time dilation are one fact, seen two ways: the clocks at the top of the mountain and the clocks at the bottom tick at different rates, and light passed between them shows it.

Does the light slow down as it climbs?

A ball thrown up a mountain slows down as it climbs. Perhaps light does too, and that’s what stretches the flashes out?

It doesn’t. As Chasing a Beam of Light found, everyone who measures the speed of light as it passes them, with their own ruler and their own clock, gets the same answer: 299,792,458 metres a second. That’s as true for someone halfway up Teide as for someone floating in empty space. Light climbs the mountain at the full speed of light all the way.

What changes, measured by the clocks along the way, is the light’s frequency, and with it its energy. Light comes in tiny packets, photons (the smallest amounts of light there are, as The Most Expensive Doughnut in the Universe described), and each photon’s energy is proportional to its frequency: a photon of blue light carries more energy than a photon of red. So a climbing ball and climbing light both pay for the climb with energy, as someone at the top would measure it. The ball pays by slowing down. Light can’t slow down, so it pays by getting redder. The energy the light loses on the climb is the same fact again, described a third way. When Robert Pound and Glen Rebka first measured the effect, they called their paper “Apparent weight of photons”.

The flashes in The Van and on the mountain

The next figure shows why the flashes arrive further apart, in both places, with the light slowed right down and the effect magnified enormously. In The Van, seen from the probe, the floor sends a flash every second, and each flash’s journey is drawn as a bar beside it, from where the floor was when the flash left to where the ceiling was when it arrived. The Van is going a little faster each time, so each journey is a little longer than the one before, and the flashes arrive further apart than they were sent. On the mountain nothing moves, and every journey is the same length; yet the top clock records the flashes just as far apart as it did in The Van. With nothing on the way to stretch them, only the clocks are left to differ. Under each picture, the flashes are marked against each clock’s own seconds: in The Van the two clocks’ seconds are alike, and the lines from each flash’s sending to its arrival fan out; on the mountain the lines stay parallel, and it’s the top clock’s seconds that are shorter, so it counts more of them between one flash and the next. The top clock runs faster.

The first measurements

The first measurement came in 1959, at Harvard University. Robert Pound and his graduate student Glen Rebka sent gamma rays, a very high-frequency kind of light, up and down a 22.5-metre tower in the Jefferson Laboratory, between a radioactive source and a sample of iron that absorbs exactly that frequency. Over that height, the shift is only about two and a half parts in a thousand million million. They measured it, and found it agreed with Einstein’s prediction to within about ten per cent; in 1964, Pound and Joseph Snider pushed the agreement to about one per cent.

In 1976, Gravity Probe A carried a hydrogen maser, a kind of atomic clock, on a rocket to about 10,000 kilometres above the Earth and back, comparing it all the while with a matching clock on the ground. The higher clock ran faster, by the predicted amount, to within about 70 parts in a million.

Gravitational potential: how deep is the clock?

Up to now, the rule has been simple: higher clocks run faster, and the difference grows in proportion to the height. That’s an excellent approximation over a mountain, or a tower, or a laboratory bench, where gravity has much the same strength at the top as at the bottom.

Far above the Earth, though, gravity weakens: twenty thousand kilometres up, where the GPS satellites orbit, it is only about a seventeenth as strong as it is on the ground. So “in proportion to the height” can’t be the whole rule. The quantity that really matters has a name.

Gravitational potential

A measure of how deep something sits in a gravitational field: the energy, per kilogram, it would take to lift it from one place to another. Near the ground, lifting a kilogram by one metre takes about 9.8 joules, and each further metre takes about the same again, because gravity barely changes over the height of a mountain. Further from the Earth, each extra metre costs less, because gravity is weaker there. Clocks at lower potential (deeper down) run slower. The difference between two clocks’ rates, as a fraction, is the difference in their potential divided by the square of the speed of light. That square is so large (about 90 million billion, in metres and seconds) that near the ground each metre of height makes a difference of only about one part in ten million billion.

Climbing out of the Earth’s gravity is like climbing out of a well. The deeper you are, the slower your clocks run, compared with clocks further up. Near the bottom of the well, where we live, the sides are steep and every metre counts the same: that’s the rule we have been using, with the difference between two clocks growing in proportion to the height between them. Further up, the well opens out, and each metre counts for less. Infinitely far away, out of the well altogether, clocks run fastest of all, though only about seven parts in ten billion faster than ours.

Why GPS needs relativity

All of this might sound like a curiosity for people with atomic clocks and patient families, but it is built into something most people use every day.

You have met it before. In A Clock Made of Light, the map on your phone was the best evidence that moving clocks run slow, and it left a second effect, from the satellites’ height, for this journey. Now that we know what height does to a clock, the story can be finished.

Satellite navigation works by timing radio signals. Each GPS satellite carries an atomic clock and broadcasts the time, and your phone works out how far away each satellite is from how long its signal took to arrive. Radio signals travel at the speed of light, about 300 metres in a millionth of a second, so an error of a millionth of a second in a satellite’s clock means an error of hundreds of metres in a distance.

The GPS satellites orbit about 20,000 kilometres up, at about 14,000 kilometres an hour, and both of those facts affect their clocks.

  • Being so much higher, out of the Earth’s gravitational well, their clocks run faster than clocks on the ground, by about 45 microseconds a day. These are Teide’s clocks again, twenty thousand kilometres up instead of two.
  • Moving so fast, their clocks run slower, by the time dilation of A Clock Made of Light: about 7 microseconds a day.

The two effects pull in opposite directions, and gravity wins. Overall, a GPS satellite’s clock runs about 38 microseconds a day fast compared with clocks on the ground. In 38 microseconds, a radio signal travels more than eleven kilometres. That is the eleven kilometres a day of the third journey’s map, and now the whole story is told: speed slows the satellites’ clocks, as the third journey found, and height speeds them up by more, as this one has.

So the engineers allow for it. Before launch, each satellite’s clock is set to tick very slightly slower than it would on the ground: at 10.22999999543 million cycles a second instead of 10.23 million, so that once in orbit, high and fast, it keeps step with clocks on the ground.

There’s a nice story behind that adjustment. When the first caesium clock went into orbit, on a test satellite called NTS-2 in 1977, not everyone was convinced that relativity would really matter, or even which way it would push. So the satellite was given a switch: the clock ran at the ground rate for its first twenty days or so in orbit, while its rate was measured, and the correction could be switched on afterwards if it was needed. The clock was found to be running fast by 442.5 parts in a million million. General relativity had predicted 446.5. The correction was switched on.

Is the lower clock struggling?

It’s tempting, even now, to picture the clock at the coast as somehow struggling: working against something heavy, like a runner in deep water, its gears dragging.

In fact the clock at the coast did its job perfectly. It measured the time that passed for it, where it was: its proper time. So did the clock at the café. Each recorded faithfully the time along its own path through space and time, what When Exactly Is Now? called its worldline. The two clocks set off from the same table, at the same moment, and met again at the same table, a couple of days later. Between those two meetings, they followed different worldlines: one stayed high, one went low and came back. And different worldlines between the same two meetings can contain different amounts of time.

That should sound familiar. It’s what happened to the twins in A Return Ticket to Tomorrow: two travellers part, follow different paths through spacetime, meet again, and find that their clocks disagree. Neither clock was faulty; the paths were different. There, the difference came from speed. Here, it comes from height. The clocks are telling us something about time itself, and about the way gravity changes it from place to place.

And it raises a question that sounds, at first, almost too strange to ask. If gravity makes time run at different rates at different heights, could that difference in the rate of time have something to do with why things fall?

Back to Teide

They returned to the café, gave the clocks back to Albert, and finished their coffee while evening crossed the plateau.

John looked down the long road to the coast, where the lights of the towns were beginning to come on.

“The clocks at the bottom of that road are running slower than ours,” he said. “Everyone down there is living through time at a slightly different rate.”

“Only slightly,” said Cal.

“But genuinely.”

“Genuinely,” Cal agreed. “About twenty nanoseconds a day.”

Albert came out to collect the cups. “You have seen that gravity changes how clocks compare,” he said. “Next, we shall see what that tells us about the paths things take.”

He picked up an apple from behind the counter, and weighed it thoughtfully in his hand.

Cal moved his coffee out of range.
