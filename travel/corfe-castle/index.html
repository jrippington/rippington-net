Corfe Castle

A murdered king, two sieges, and the Pleiades

April 2023, 4 min read

Draft, for review

Corfe Castle stands on a steep hill in a gap in the Purbeck Hills, in Dorset. A Saxon king was murdered here in 978, the Normans raised one of the earliest castles in England to be built partly of stone, and after a Civil War siege Parliament had it blown apart. I photographed it from the hill overlooking it on Good Friday 2023, and made a fantasy of it under the Pleiades.

Corfe Castle stands on a steep hill in a gap in the Purbeck Hills, in Dorset, above the village named after it: Corfe comes from the Old English for a cutting, the gap itself. I photographed it from the hill overlooking it in the late afternoon of Good Friday, 7 April 2023. The picture on this page isn’t that photograph as it came out of the camera, though. It’s a fantasy, made from three photographs.

The picture

The castle is my photograph from the hill, taken in the late afternoon and heavily manipulated in Photoshop to look as though it’s night-time. The sky is the Pleiades, the Seven Sisters, from a photograph of them I took myself; they have a gallery of their own on Nyx. And the figure in the foreground is Cal, from a third photograph, standing looking at the scene. The sky isn’t to scale: in the real sky the Pleiades are a small, tight group of stars, about four times as wide as the full Moon and about 440 light-years away. The picture is on Ithil too, as a work of art: Corfe Castle Under the Pleiades.

A murdered king

Before the castle, a large Saxon building of timber stood on the hill. On the evening of 18 March 978 the teenage King Edward rode to Corfe, his stepmother Ælfthryth’s estate, to see his half-brother Æthelred, and was killed: armed men surrounded him as he sat on his horse. Nobody at the time named his killers, but the chroniclers writing after the Norman Conquest almost all blamed Ælfthryth, who wanted her own son to be king. Æthelred, later called the Unready, became king instead. Edward was buried at Wareham, moved to Shaftesbury Abbey about a year later, and became a saint, Edward the Martyr.

The Norman castle

After 1066 William the Conqueror built a castle on the hill, one of the first in England to be built partly of stone, guarding the way between Wareham and Swanage and his links across the Channel. Its keep, finished in 1105 for his son Henry I, stood 21 metres tall on a hill 55 metres high, built of Purbeck limestone quarried a few miles away. King John spent lavishly on it and kept prisoners in it. It stayed royal until Elizabeth I sold it in 1572, and in 1635 Sir John Bankes, Charles I’s Attorney General, bought it.

The sieges

In the Civil War the Bankes family were for the King. In 1643 Sir John’s wife, Mary, Lady Bankes, held the castle through a siege of about six weeks by 500 to 600 of Parliament’s soldiers, with a garrison of about 80. In a second siege it fell, in February 1646, when one of its own officers, Lieutenant-Colonel Thomas Pitman, let Parliament’s troops in disguised as reinforcements. In March Parliament ordered it slighted, made useless as a fortress, and sappers blew its walls apart with gunpowder, leaving it a ruin. The story is that Lady Bankes was allowed to keep the castle’s keys for her courage, though a pass from Parliament’s Speaker in July 1645 suggests she’d left Corfe months before the second siege began.

The family built a new house at Kingston Lacy instead of rebuilding, and the ruin was plundered for building stone. Corfe became a sight for tourists, and for painters, Turner among them. It stayed with the Bankes family for more than 300 years, until Ralph Bankes, who died in 1981, left it to the National Trust with Kingston Lacy and the rest of the family’s estates.
