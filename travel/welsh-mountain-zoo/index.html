A Day at the Welsh Mountain Zoo

Bob, the tigers and the snow leopard

On a weekend in Snowdonia, Cal and I spent a Sunday afternoon at the Welsh Mountain Zoo, on the wooded hill above Colwyn Bay. A white-tailed eagle we came to call Bob kept us company for hours, and Cal photographed the zoo's two Sumatran tigers, Kinnara and Budi, and its snow leopard, Sumbe.

A Weekend in North Wales, part 2 of 3

Cal and I were in Snowdonia for the weekend, and on the Sunday, 29 October 2023, we spent the afternoon at the Welsh Mountain Zoo. It’s on the coast, just outside the national park: 37 acres of woods and old gardens on a hill above Colwyn Bay, about 120 metres above the sea, looking out over the bay and west towards Conwy and the Carneddau. The gardens were laid out in 1898 and 1899 by Thomas Mawson, for a Manchester surgeon, Walter Whitehead, whose grand house was never built. Robert Jackson opened the zoo there on 18 May 1963. He was killed by a falling tree in 1969, and his widow and three sons kept it going; it’s been a charity since 1983, and the National Zoo of Wales since 2008.

Bob

Bob isn’t the zoo’s name for him. It’s the one we gave him, because he sat there with us for hours. He’s a white-tailed eagle, and he was in a huge aviary.

White-tailed eagles are Britain’s largest birds of prey, with wings up to about 2.5 metres across: the RSPB calls them “flying barn doors”. They were persecuted to extinction here. They’d stopped breeding in Wales by the middle of the 1800s, and the last native bird in Britain was shot in Shetland in 1918. Young eagles from Norway were released on the Isle of Rum from 1975, and later in Wester Ross and Fife, and Scotland has well over a hundred pairs again. Since 2019 they’ve been released on the Isle of Wight too, and in 2023 a chick fledged in the wild in England for the first time in over 240 years.

Bob’s yellow beak and eyes and his white tail make him an adult, at least about five years old; a young one is darker, with a dark beak and dark eyes.

The tigers

The zoo’s two Sumatran tigers are Kinnara and Budi, a pair since 2016. Kinnara, the female, was born at Krefeld Zoo in Germany in 2012 and came here the next year. Budi was born at London Zoo in 2014 and came to be her mate, the first time the zoo had been able to try breeding tigers, though they haven’t had cubs so far. In the photograph of the two at the top of this page, Budi is the one behind, with the broader head and the bigger ruff.

Sumatran tigers are the smallest tigers alive: a male weighs about 100 to 140 kilograms, and Budi was 118 in 2018, when he had root-canal treatment for a broken tooth. A tiger’s canines are the longest of any cat’s, up to about 7.5 centimetres. In the wild they live only on the Indonesian island of Sumatra, and they’re critically endangered: an estimate published in 2017 put their number at about 600, as the forests are cleared for oil palm and acacia plantations and the tigers are snared and poached. Europe’s zoos breed them in a shared programme, moving tigers between zoos to make pairs.

The snow leopard

The snow leopard is Sumbe, the zoo’s male, two and a half years old. A female, Padme, born at the Highland Wildlife Park in Scotland in 2022, had arrived only that week to join him, as part of a breeding programme. Their enclosure, the Silk Road, was finished in 2021.

Snow leopards live in the high mountains of twelve countries in Central and South Asia, usually between 3,000 and 5,000 metres up. The tail is almost as long as the body, and helps them balance on steep rock. Despite the name, they’re more closely related to tigers than to leopards, and unlike the other big cats they can’t roar. They’re listed as vulnerable, with perhaps 2,700 to 3,400 adults in the wild, though how many there really are is disputed.

The photographs

Cal took all of these photographs, with a Nikon D850 and a Sigma 150–600 mm zoom, at between 210 and 480 mm. They’re in their galleries on Thalassa too, Birds of Prey and Big Cats, with a note on each.
