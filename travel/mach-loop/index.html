A Weekend in North Wales, part 3 of 3

On Monday 30 October 2023, on the way home from a weekend of gravity karts and the Welsh Mountain Zoo, Cal and I stopped at the Mach Loop and climbed up to Cad West. It’s a patch of hillside on the west side of Bwlch Llyn Bach, the pass that carries the A487 between Cadair Idris and the hills to the east, about half a mile’s walk and 300 feet of climbing above the lay-by at the top of the road. The “Cad” is short for Cadair Idris.

We could hear the jet coming, but when it came out from behind the hill it was so sudden that we both jumped, and very nearly fell over into the mud.

The Mach Loop

The Mach Loop is a ring of valleys in mid-Wales, between Dolgellau to the north, Tal-y-llyn to the west, Machynlleth to the south and Dinas Mawddwy to the east. Its name comes from Machynlleth, not from the speed of sound: officially, it’s the Machynlleth Loop. It’s part of Low Flying Area 7, which covers almost all of Wales, where military jets train to fly fast and low through the hills, as low as 250 feet above the ground or anything on it, on weekdays. In the valleys they fly at up to about 450 knots, more than 500 miles an hour.

Through the pass below Cad West they usually come from the north, from the left as you look down on the road, and that’s what makes the hillside worth the climb: from up there you see the jets level with you, or even from above. The lay-by at the top of the pass sits on what’s left of a small lake, Llyn y Tri Greyenyn, the lake of the three pebbles. The story goes that the giant Idris found three pebbles in his shoe and threw them away here, and they became the boulders beside it.

The F-35

The jet was an F-35 Lightning II, a single-seat, single-engine stealth fighter built by Lockheed Martin at Fort Worth, in Texas, about 15 per cent of each one by value made in Britain. It’s 15.7 metres long, with wings 10.7 metres across, and at height it can reach 1.6 times the speed of sound. Its name honours two older Lightnings, the American P-38 of the Second World War and the English Electric Lightning.

From below, the photograph can’t show what tells the two versions flying over Wales apart: the F-35A’s gun, on its far side, or the big door on top of the F-35B for its lift fan. But the markings on its intake match the American F-35As of the 48th Fighter Wing at RAF Lakenheath, in Suffolk, whose jets were flying in the Loop that day, and the RAF’s own front-line F-35Bs, 617 Squadron’s, were aboard the carrier HMS Queen Elizabeth in Portsmouth that evening. So it was most likely an American F-35A, though which one, I can’t tell.

The cloud on its wings

The white cloud around the jet isn’t smoke, and it has nothing to do with the sound barrier: jets fly through the Loop well below the speed of sound. To pull up that hard, its wings had to make several times its own weight in lift, and the air pressure over them dropped sharply. Air that suddenly drops in pressure expands and cools, and in damp Welsh air it cooled past the point where its water vapour condenses, into a cloud that clings to the wing and vanishes again behind it. The spinning cores of the vortices that stream from the wingtips are low in pressure and cool too, so they leave the two thin trails. Spotters call them fluff and ribbons.

The photograph

Cal took it, with a Nikon D850 and a Sigma 150–600 mm zoom at its shortest, 150 mm, at 1/1600 of a second: short enough to freeze a jet, which at 450 knots moves only about 14 centimetres while the shutter is open.
