A Wet Weekend in Snowdonia

The Aberglaslyn Pass, Nant Gwynant and Llyn Padarn

July 2022, 5 min read

Draft, for review

On this page

  2. Nant Gwynant
  3. Llyn Padarn and Dolbadarn Castle
  4. The photographs

When Cal and I arrived in Snowdonia for a weekend in July 2022, it was incredibly wet: the rain came back to the mountains after a fortnight without a drop. By the next morning the cloud had lifted, and the rivers were running high under a clearing sky, from the Aberglaslyn Pass and Nant Gwynant to Llyn Padarn and Dolbadarn Castle.

When Cal and I arrived in Snowdonia for the weekend of 23 July 2022, it was incredibly wet. It was the week after the heatwave, when Britain passed 40 °C for the first time, and the hills above Beddgelert had gone a fortnight without any rain at all. On the Saturday it came back, more than 40 millimetres of it in a day, and the Afon Glaslyn rose to almost fourteen times the flow it had had the day before.

The Aberglaslyn Pass

Late on the Saturday afternoon we were on Pont Aberglaslyn, the old stone bridge a mile or so below Beddgelert, looking up the gorge the river has cut between the mountains. The bridge is a single arch, probably 17th century, and widened at the end of the 18th. Until the Cob was built across the estuary at Porthmadog, in 1811, the tide came all the way up to here, and there was a little quay by the pool below the bridge.

At seven the next morning the same view was another place: the cloud gone, the crags at the head of the gorge lit by a low sun, and the river still running high and white.

Nant Gwynant

Twenty minutes later we were on the hillside above upper Nant Gwynant, looking across the valley’s fields to Llyn Gwynant and Gallt y Wenallt, the broad hill at the south-western end of the Snowdon Horseshoe, 619 metres high. Snowdon itself was hidden behind it, and the mountains at the head of the valley were still in cloud, with patches of sun moving across the slopes below them.

Llyn Padarn and Dolbadarn Castle

By mid-morning we were on the shore of Llyn Padarn at Llanberis. The lake is about two miles long and about 27 metres deep at its deepest, and its Arctic charr, the torgoch, or “red belly”, are thought to have lived in it since the last Ice Age: one of only three natural populations in Wales.

Across the water, on its knoll between Llyn Padarn and Llyn Peris, stands the round keep of Dolbadarn Castle, probably built by Llywelyn the Great around the start of the 13th century to guard the way up the Llanberis Pass. His grandson Llywelyn ap Gruffudd probably kept his own brother, Owain Goch, prisoner in it for more than twenty years. Turner painted it, and gave the painting to the Royal Academy. Behind it, the pass runs up into the mountains, Crib Goch in cloud at its head, and on the left are the grey slate tips of the Dinorwig quarry, which worked the mountainside until 1969.

The photographs

I took them all with my Nikon D850, the gorge on the Saturday with a 12–24 mm zoom and the rest with a 24–120 mm. The view of Nant Gwynant is several frames stitched into one panorama, which you can explore at full size, and the valley and the lake are each blended from bracketed exposures, to hold the bright cloud and the shaded hills together.
