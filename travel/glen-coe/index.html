The Highlands and Skye, part 3 of 3

On Monday 8 May 2023, on the way home from a weekend on Skye, Cal and I drove the van back down the A82 through Glen Coe, the way we’d come up on our way to Glenfinnan. Early in the afternoon we stopped below the Three Sisters, with the cloud down on their tops.

The Three Sisters

The Three Sisters are the ends of three ridges that run north from Bidean nam Bian, at 1,150 metres the highest mountain in Argyll, hidden in the cloud behind them. From the left, they’re Beinn Fhada, whose top was lost in the cloud, Gearr Aonach and Aonach Dubh. Between the first two is Coire Gabhail, the Lost Valley, where the MacDonalds of Glencoe hid stolen cattle. Between the second two is Coire nan Lochan, and its stream falls down the gully to the floor of the glen.

A volcano that fell in

The glen is cut through what’s left of a volcano that was active about 420 million years ago. Its eruptions emptied the chamber of magma beneath it, and the ground above collapsed into the space, block by block, over an area about 14 kilometres by 8: a caldera. Glen Coe was the first place in the world where that kind of collapse was recognised in ancient volcanic rocks, by the geologists Clough, Maufe and Bailey in 1909. Most of the summits on this side of the glen, the Three Sisters’ included, are ignimbrite, laid down by avalanches of hot ash and gas, at about 600 °C, from the volcano’s eruptions. The glen’s shape is far younger, the work of the ice ages’ glaciers, the last of which melted only about 11,500 years ago.

The massacre

Glen Coe is best known for what happened here on 13 February 1692. The MacDonalds of Glencoe had taken part in the Jacobite rising of 1689, and the Highland clans had to swear allegiance to William and Mary by 1 January 1692. Their chief, MacIain, went first to Fort William, was sent on to Inveraray, and didn’t take the oath until 6 January. Soldiers of the Earl of Argyll’s Regiment, under Captain Robert Campbell of Glenlyon, had been living in the MacDonalds’ homes for about two weeks when they were ordered to kill them. Early that morning they killed about 38, MacIain and his wife among them, and more died of exposure as they fled into the winter hills.

The photographs

I took both with my Nikon D850 and a 24–120 mm lens. The Three Sisters are a panorama of several frames at 24 mm, stitched together into one picture 18,624 pixels wide, which you can open to explore at full resolution. The second, at 44 mm, looks up into Coire nan Lochan as the cloud filled it.
