St Nectan's Glen

A waterfall through a hole in the rock, and a suspicious peacock

On our trip to Cornwall at the end of June 2022, Cal and I went to St Nectan's Glen, near Tintagel, where a river falls through a slate gorge into a basin and pours out again through a hole it has worn in the basin's wall. The legend of the saint who lived above it is younger than it sounds. One of the peacocks was eyeing us very suspiciously while we had a coffee.

Cornwall and Home Across Dartmoor, part 2 of 4

On Wednesday 29 June 2022, on the same trip to Cornwall as Kynance Cove, Cal and I went to St Nectan’s Glen, at Trethevy, near Tintagel, on the north coast. The glen is a wooded valley that runs for about a mile along the Trevillet River, and in it is the waterfall it’s famous for, St Nectan’s Kieve.

The kieve

The river has cut its way down through slate, about 60 feet (18 metres) from the top of the falls to the pool at the bottom. The upper fall drops into a basin in the rock, the kieve (the Cornish word for a tub or basin), and the water has worn a hole right through the basin’s wall. It pours out through the hole and falls again into a shallow pool, which is where this photograph was taken from. Higher up the walls are the remains of older kieves, from before the river had cut down this far.

To many of the people who come here it’s a sacred place. They leave ribbons, crystals, photographs, and prayers on the branches and the rock walls near the falls, and build little stacks of flat stones from the stream. There’s one on the mossy ledge on the left of the photograph, with a small heart-shaped token further along.

St Nectan

The story goes that Nectan, a hermit, lived in a cell above the falls and rang a silver bell in stormy weather to warn ships off the rocks at the mouth of Rocky Valley. In the version Robert Hunt wrote down in 1865, as he lay dying he rang the bell three times and dropped it into the kieve, and two strange ladies from a foreign land buried him and his treasure in an oak chest under the river bed, turning the river aside to dig the grave. The house at the top of the falls, the Hermitage, is said to stand on the site of his cell, with the ruins of an early chapel in its walls, and people still go in to light a candle or to pray.

How much of it is true is another matter. There was a St Nectan, but he’s remembered at Hartland, in Devon, about 25 miles up the coast, and nothing places him here. In 1799 the waterfall was called Nathan’s Cave, probably after a local man. The saint seems to have arrived in the 1800s, helped along by poets, among them Robert Stephen Hawker, the vicar of Morwenstow, and by a Trethevy farmer who showed visitors around. The Hermitage was most likely built over an 18th-century summerhouse.

The peacock

There were peacocks around the café on top of the hill, above the falls, and one of them was eyeing us very suspiciously while we had a coffee.

He’s an Indian peafowl, a male, with the shining blue neck, the little crest of bare-shafted feathers, and the black-and-white barred feathers over his wings. Peafowl come from India and Sri Lanka, but people have kept them in parks and gardens far beyond for thousands of years: they’d reached Athens by about 450 BC. He’s in Pheasants and Peafowl on Thalassa too.

The photographs

I took both with a Nikon D850. The waterfall is an HDR picture, blended from bracketed frames exposed differently, which keeps the detail in both the dark walls of the gorge and the white water; it’s at 14 mm, with a 12–24 mm lens. The peacock is at 550 mm, with a Sigma 150–600 mm zoom.
