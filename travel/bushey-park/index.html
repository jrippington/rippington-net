Britain and Ireland, Arda

An Afternoon in Bushy Park

Deer and parakeets beside Hampton Court

August 2022, 4 min read

Draft, for review

On this page

  1. The deer
  2. The parakeets

Late one afternoon in August 2022, Cal and I were in Bushy Park, Henry VIII's old hunting ground beside Hampton Court, and saw the deer and the parakeets that live there: two red deer stags lying in the dry grass, fallow deer among the bracken, and London's wild green parakeets on an old tree.

Late one afternoon, on Thursday 25 August 2022, Cal and I were in Bushy Park, in south-west London, with Bear the dog. It’s the second largest of London’s Royal Parks, after Richmond Park: 445 hectares of grassland, bracken and old trees just north of Hampton Court Palace, between Teddington and Hampton. When Cardinal Wolsey gave Hampton Court to Henry VIII in 1529, Henry made the park his hunting ground, fenced with wooden palings and stocked with deer. It had been a dry summer. A drought had been declared across London a fortnight before, and the long grass had turned gold.

The deer

A few hundred red and fallow deer roam the park. The fallow have been here since the Tudors; the red deer are descended from a herd brought in after the Second World War.

Red deer are Britain’s largest native land mammal, and a stag can weigh up to about 190 kilograms. The two stags at the top of this page were lying in the long grass near the end of the day. A stag grows a new set of antlers every year, under a soft covering called velvet, and rubs it off once they’re fully grown, in August or September, in time for the autumn rut. The one on the left was still trailing strips of his.

Fallow deer are smaller, and most of them are spotted. The Romans brought them to Britain first, but they died out, and they were brought again in the 11th century to stock deer parks. They’re the only British deer whose antlers flatten into broad palms, and a buck’s antlers don’t reach their full size until he’s three or four. A young buck with the simple spikes of his first antlers is called a pricket.

Fallow come in four colours: the common chestnut with white spots, a paler kind called menil, black, and white. White fallow aren’t albinos. They range from pale sandy to white, and grow whiter as they age, and the doe in the bracken may be one of them.

The parakeets

The parakeets are rose-ringed parakeets, also called ring-necked parakeets: Britain’s only naturalised parrot, about 40 centimetres long, much of it tail, from Africa south of the Sahara and from South Asia. Wild ones were reported here as long ago as the 1860s, but the population that lasted began breeding in 1969, in Croydon, and by 2016 there were about 12,000 pairs in Britain, most of them in and around London.

Nobody knows for certain how they got loose. The stories blame Jimi Hendrix, letting a pair go in Carnaby Street, or the filming of The African Queen, and here, next door to Hampton Court, there’s a rumour that they escaped from Henry VIII’s menagerie. None of them has much evidence behind it. A study published in 2019 found the first lasting populations around Croydon and Dartford, and suggested that they may have come from pets let go during the parrot-disease scares of the 1930s and 1950s.

Only the adult males have the ring that gives them their name, rose-pink and black; the females and young birds have a faint grey one, or none. At least two of the three on the tree are males, and so is the one below.

The deer are in Hoofed Mammals on Thalassa, and the parakeets in Parrots, with a note on each.
