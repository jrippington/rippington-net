The Church on Brent Tor

St Michael de Rupe, on the western edge of Dartmoor

June 2022, 4 min read

Draft, for review

On the way home from Cornwall at the end of June 2022, Cal and I stopped late in the day at Brent Tor, on the western edge of Dartmoor, to photograph St Michael's Church on the top: one of the smallest parish churches in England, on a hill of lava that erupted under the sea about 350 million years ago, with Devon and Cornwall spread out below it.

Cornwall and Home Across Dartmoor, part 3 of 4

Cal and I had been in Cornwall, and on Thursday 30 June 2022 we drove back up across Dartmoor, stopping on the way at Tintagel to see if we could photograph some seabirds. Late in the day, at about half past seven, we stopped at Brent Tor, on the moor’s western edge about four miles north of Tavistock, to photograph the church of St Michael on its top. The tor is a steep hill standing on its own, about 330 metres above the sea, and with the church on its summit you can see it from miles around.

St Michael of the Rock

The church’s full name is St Michael de Rupe, St Michael of the Rock, and it’s tiny: 37 feet long and 14 feet 6 inches wide, about 11 metres by 4.4, with room for about forty people. The church itself says it’s the fourth smallest complete parish church in England. Robert Giffard, lord of the manor of Lamerton, built the first church here in about 1130, and Tavistock Abbey served it until Henry VIII dissolved the monasteries in 1539. Most of what stands now dates from the 13th and 14th centuries, built of local volcanic stone, and the battlemented tower at its west end was added in the 15th. It’s still the parish church of Brentor, the parish below, with services up here on Sundays from Easter to Michaelmas, though most of the parish’s services are now held in its 19th-century church in the village.

The devil and the merchant

There are stories about why the church is on the top. In one, it was begun at the foot of the tor, and every night the devil carried the work up to the summit, until in the end the people built it where he’d left it; in some tellings St Michael himself came down, threw the devil off the rock and dropped a boulder on him. In another, a merchant caught in a storm at sea promised to build a church on the first land he saw if he came home safe, and the first land he saw was Brent Tor.

A hill of lava

Brent Tor isn’t like most of Dartmoor’s tors. They’re granite; it’s what’s left of lava that erupted into a shallow sea about 350 million years ago and piled up in a mound on the sea floor, some of it in the rounded “pillows” that lava makes when it meets water. The currents wore the mound down, and it has been weathering ever since.

People were here long before the church. In the Iron Age they built ramparts around the north and east sides of the tor’s foot, unusually low down for a hillfort, and in the early 1900s some of their stone was taken to build the path up to the church.

The view

On a clear day, the church says, you can see the high moor to the east, Plymouth Sound and Whitsand Bay to the south, the Tamar valley and Bodmin Moor to the west, and Exmoor to the north. That evening the sky was full of cloud, and the low sun lit the fields below in patches. To the north-north-east is the village of North Brentor, with the tower of its church, and beyond it the open moor.

The other way, west-south-west into Cornwall, Kit Hill is on the horizon about eight miles away, with the old mine chimney on its top, and beyond it the long skyline of Bodmin Moor.

An hour and a half later, from the moor above Tavistock, I photographed the church again as the sun went down, a notch on the skyline five miles away: that’s in Dartmoor at Sunset.
