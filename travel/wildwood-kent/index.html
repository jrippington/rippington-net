An Afternoon at Wildwood

A brown bear and a wolf in the Kent woods

April 2023, 3 min read

Draft, for review

On this page

  1. The bears
  2. The wolves

One Sunday afternoon in April 2023 I was at Wildwood, the wildlife park in the ancient woods between Herne Bay and Canterbury, in Kent, which keeps Britain's animals, both those we still have and those we've lost. Among the lost are the brown bear and the wolf, and I photographed one of each.

On the afternoon of Sunday 2 April 2023 I was at Wildwood, near Herne Bay in Kent, and photographed two of its animals: one of its brown bears, and one of its wolves.

Wildwood is a wildlife park and a conservation charity, with over 200 animals in about 40 acres of ancient woodland at Herne Common, on the road between Herne Bay and Canterbury, part of the old woods of the Blean. It keeps British wildlife, both the animals that live here now and the ones Britain has lost, and it had turned 21 that March. Besides showing them, it breeds water voles for release into the wild, and with Kent Wildlife Trust it’s bringing bison back to the Blean. Three European bison were released into West Blean and Thornden Woods in July 2022; a calf was found that September, the first bison born in the wild in Britain for thousands of years, and the bull arrived in December.

The bears

Brown bears died out in Britain more than a thousand years ago. Wildwood’s two grown bears, the brothers Fluff and Scruff, came here in November 2014 from a disused bear-hunting centre in the Rhodope Mountains of Bulgaria, where bears were bred to be shot. They’d spent about 15 years in concrete pits, and arrived underweight and badly stressed. Here they have a woodland enclosure, with caves and a pond, and every winter since 2017 they’ve slowed down into torpor, a winter sleep short of full hibernation. This year they’d come out of it, and back into their enclosure, only the week before.

In December 2022 Wildwood had taken in a cub, too. Boki’s mother had rejected him, and the keepers at Port Lympne had reared him by hand; at ten months old he came here, to be taught how to be a bear.

The bear at the top of this page has the broad, grizzled face of a grown bear, not a cub, so it’s Fluff or Scruff, I think. Which of the two, I can’t tell.

The wolves

Wolves lasted longer here than bears. They were gone from England by about 1500, in the reign of Henry VII, and from Scotland by about 1700. Wildwood’s pack began with a pair, Nuna and Odin, two years old, from Switzerland and Sweden, who came in 2018 to a new enclosure in the woods and had four cubs that same year.

Which of the pack this is, I can’t tell either.

The bear is in Bears on Thalassa, and the wolf in Wolves and Foxes, with a note on each.
