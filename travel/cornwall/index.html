Cornwall, Again and Again

Perranporth, Godrevy and the gulls of Mevagissey

May 2023, 5 min read

Draft, for review

Cal and I go to Cornwall a lot, and these photographs are from several trips: bodyboarding, one of our favourite things to do down there, Chapel Rock at Perranporth from our drone, sunset on the long beach from Hayle to Godrevy lighthouse, and the herring gulls of Mevagissey, which Cal photographed once we'd defended our chips from them.

Cal and I go down to Cornwall a lot, and the photographs here are from several of those trips: Perranporth and the beaches near it on the north coast, the long beach from Hayle to Godrevy lighthouse, and Mevagissey, on the south coast, where we stopped for chips. Kynance Cove and St Nectan’s Glen, from the same trips, have pages of their own.

Perranporth

Bodyboarding is one of our favourite things to do down in Cornwall when we’re there, and these are two pictures of Cal doing it at Perranporth.

Perranporth is about 7 miles south-west of Newquay, and its name is Cornish: Porth Peran, the cove of St Piran, Cornwall’s patron saint, who’s said to have landed on this beach from Ireland. The sand runs about 2 miles north to Ligger Point, with dunes behind it. At the south end the cliffs are cut into arches and stacks, with the mouths of old tin mines in them, and out on the sand in front of them stands Chapel Rock, with a tidal pool built into it.

The photograph of the two of us walking out into the sunset with our bodyboards was actually taken at Watergate Bay, just up the road, where 2 miles of sand lie between the cliffs north of Newquay.

Hayle and Godrevy

Further down the coast, in St Ives Bay, 3 miles of sand run from the mouth of the River Hayle to Godrevy, backed by towans, the Cornish word for dunes. I took this one at sunset from the Godrevy end of the beach, among the mussel-covered rocks at Gwithian, with the tide out and the lighthouse about 2 kilometres away.

The lighthouse stands on Godrevy Island, about 300 metres off Godrevy Head. Beyond it lie the Stones, a reef that had been a hazard to ships for centuries, and after the steamer Nile was lost in a storm on 30 November 1854, with everyone aboard, Trinity House built the lighthouse to mark the reef: a white octagonal tower 26 metres high, designed by James Walker and first lit on 1 March 1859. Since 2012 the light itself has been on a steel frame on the rocks beside it, and the tower is kept as a daymark. Both are in Cal’s photograph, taken further round, from the headland, with Sparky, our drone, flying towards the lighthouse.

Godrevy is said to be the lighthouse behind Virginia Woolf’s To the Lighthouse (1927), though the novel moves it to Skye. Her family spent their summers at Talland House in St Ives, across the bay, in sight of its light, and in September 1892, aged ten, she went out to it and signed the visitors’ book.

Mevagissey

Mevagissey is about 5 miles south of St Austell: a fishing village that once lived on pilchards and smuggling, with an inner harbour authorised by Parliament in 1775 and an outer one added in 1888. We had stopped there to get some chips from the amazing fish and chip shop, and after defending our chips from attacks by the gulls, Cal took these photographs.

The gulls are herring gulls, and once they’re used to people they’ll swoop down and take food out of your hands. An adult has a yellow bill with a red spot near the tip, which its chicks peck at to ask to be fed.

The two brown birds squabbling with an adult below aren’t another kind of gull: they’re young herring gulls, which take about four years to grow into the grey and white, and one already has a grey back coming through. For all the gulls you see around a harbour, herring gulls have been on the UK’s red list since 2009, because their numbers have fallen so far.

The gulls are in the Seabirds gallery on Thalassa too, with a note on each.

The photographs

The two views of Chapel Rock are from our drone, a DJI Mavic 2 Pro, which we call Sparky, in the last few minutes before sunset. I took the pictures of Cal bodyboarding with my Nikon D850 and a Sigma 150–600 mm zoom at 550 mm, and the sunset at Gwithian with the D850 and a 12–24 mm lens, merged from several bracketed exposures. Cal took Godrevy and the gulls, on a Nikon D5300 and a Nikon D50.
