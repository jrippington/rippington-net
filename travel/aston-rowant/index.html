Aston Rowant

The edge of the Chilterns, above the M40

Aston Rowant National Nature Reserve is close to home: chalk downland on the edge of the Chilterns, where the hills fall away to the Oxfordshire plain and the M40 comes down through a deep cutting in the escarpment. Two panoramas from July 2022: the sun going down over the plain, and the reserve, the motorway and the fields from our drone.

Aston Rowant is close to home. It’s a National Nature Reserve on the north-western edge of the Chiltern Hills, on the border between Oxfordshire and Buckinghamshire, where the chalk escarpment drops steeply to the plain and the view from the top goes out over the fields of Oxfordshire. The M40 runs straight through it, in a cutting that splits the reserve in two. These are two stitched panoramas from July 2022: the sun going down over the plain, and the reserve, the motorway and the fields from our drone.

The downs

The reserve covers about 159 hectares of chalk grassland, beech woods and juniper scrub. When it was declared, in 1958, it was only about 28 hectares around Beacon Hill, the ridge at its northern end. Grassland like this comes from a thin, poor soil and a very long history of grazing: centuries of sheep and rabbits kept coarse grass and scrub from taking over, and sheep still graze it to keep it that way. Its flowers include at least seven kinds of orchid and the Chiltern gentian, and its butterflies the chalkhill blue and the silver-spotted skipper. It also has the last of the area’s junipers. In the hot, dry July of 2022, the grass had dried to the colour of straw.

The cutting

The M40 came through in the early 1970s. There was a lot of opposition at the time: objectors suggested a tunnel, or a different route, and a public inquiry in April 1969 turned both down. Work on the stretch from Stokenchurch down across the escarpment began in March 1972, and it opened in 1974. Where it crosses the escarpment, the cutting is 47 metres deep and about 1,200 metres long. The chalk in its walls, about 88 million years old, is the best exposure of rock of that age in central England, and the cutting has been protected for its geology since 1986. As part of the work, juniper cuttings were planted on the upper slopes of the cutting.

The red kites

Red kites are a common sight over the reserve, but they’re newcomers. They were persecuted out of England in the 1800s, and by the 1930s fewer than ten pairs were left in the whole of Britain, all of them in mid-Wales. In July 1990 the first 13 young kites were released in the Chilterns close by, on the Oxfordshire and Buckinghamshire border: 11 from Navarra, in northern Spain, and two from Wales. More followed every summer until 1994. Within a couple of years they were breeding in the wild, and since then the kites of the Chilterns have been taken to start new populations in other parts of England.

The photographs

I took the sunset with my Nikon D850 and a Sigma 12–24 mm lens at 23 mm. It’s stitched together from several frames, and each of those was taken at a range of exposures and merged (HDR, for high dynamic range), so that the sun and the shadows in the grass both keep their detail. Open it at full size to look around it. The other is from our drone, stitched together from its own frames.
