The Highlands and Skye, part 1 of 3

In May 2023 Cal and I took the van on a trip north, up through Glen Coe to the Glenfinnan Viaduct, and then on to the Isle of Skye. We were at Glenfinnan, at the head of Loch Shiel, at about midday on Saturday 6 May, and this panorama is from our drone, over the glen just north of the viaduct, looking south over it and down the loch.

The viaduct

The viaduct carries the railway from Fort William to Mallaig, the West Highland Line’s extension to the coast, across Glen Finnan and the River Finnan. It’s 380 metres long and about 30 metres high, with 21 arches, each about 15 metres across, and it’s built on a curve. Robert McAlpine and Sons built it, to the design of the engineers Simpson and Wilson. Work on the extension began in 1897, the viaduct was carrying materials across the glen by October 1898, and the line opened on 1 April 1901.

It’s made of mass concrete, with no steel reinforcement, because the local rock, a hard schist, was difficult to cut and dress into building stone. Robert McAlpine, the head of the firm, was known as “Concrete Bob”, and this is still the longest concrete viaduct in Scotland.

There’s an old story that a horse and cart fell into one of the piers while it was being built, and was left there. In 1987 Roland Paxton, of Heriot-Watt University, looked inside two of its piers with a camera and found nothing. In 2001 a radar scan found a horse, and the wreck of a cart, in the central pier of the Loch nan Uamh viaduct, further along the same line towards Mallaig.

Steam trains still cross it: West Coast Railways’ Jacobite runs between Fort William and Mallaig from spring to autumn. The viaduct is famous now from the Harry Potter films, where the Hogwarts Express crosses it; in the second, Chamber of Secrets, Harry and Ron fly their Ford Anglia alongside the train over it.

The monument and the loch

The tower on the shore at the head of the loch is the Glenfinnan Monument. Prince Charles Edward Stuart raised his standard at Glenfinnan on 19 August 1745, at the start of the Jacobite rising that ended in defeat at Culloden on 16 April 1746. About seventy years later, Alexander MacDonald of Glenaladale built the tower in memory of the Highlanders who had fought for the prince, though he died before it was finished. The figure of a Highlander on the top was added in 1835.

Loch Shiel runs away south-west from here for about 28 kilometres, to Acharacle. It’s a freshwater loch, up to 128 metres deep. The pointed peak above its far shore is Sgùrr Ghiubhsachain, 849 metres high.

The photograph

I took it from our drone, Sparky, a DJI Mavic 2 Pro, as several frames stitched together into one panorama.
