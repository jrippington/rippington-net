Dartmoor at Sunset

Brent Tor on the skyline, and the hill ponies

June 2022, 4 min read

Draft, for review

On this page

  1. Brent Tor on the skyline
  2. The ponies
  3. The photographs

Crossing Dartmoor on the way home from Cornwall at the end of June 2022, Cal and I were up on the moor above Tavistock as the sun went down. Brent Tor's church stood on the skyline five miles away, and some of the moor's ponies, a mare and her foal among them, were grazing at the side of the road.

Cornwall and Home Across Dartmoor, part 4 of 4

On Thursday 30 June 2022, at the end of a trip to Cornwall, Cal and I drove back up across Dartmoor. About an hour after Brent Tor, at nine in the evening, we were up on the moor at Pork Hill, on the B3357, the road from Tavistock over the moor to Two Bridges, just below Cox Tor and more than 300 metres up. The sun was about half an hour from setting, in the north-west, and some of the Dartmoor ponies were grazing by the road as it went down.

Brent Tor on the skyline

Sixteen minutes later I photographed Brent Tor itself through a long lens. It’s five miles to the north-west, standing up on its own above the fields, and its church is a notch on the summit. At the end of June, seen from Pork Hill, the sun goes down almost exactly behind it; just then it was a little to the left and just above the horizon, behind the cloud. The rays are sunlight coming through gaps in the cloud, lit up by the haze in the air. They seem to spread out from the sun, but they’re very nearly parallel: it’s perspective that fans them out, the way it makes a long straight road narrow to a point in the distance.

The ponies

None of Dartmoor’s ponies is wild. Every one belongs to a commoner, a farmer with the right to graze the moor’s common land, and every autumn they’re gathered in the drifts: rounded up off the commons into pounds, where each owner takes their own ponies home. The foals born that spring are weaned, and some are sold at the markets in Tavistock and Chagford; others are branded and turned back out on the moor to breed. The mare grazing the verge with her foal has what looks like her owner’s brand on her hindquarters.

They aren’t pure-bred Dartmoor ponies, either. The breed, with its own society and studbook, is bay, brown, black, grey, chestnut or roan, and never patched. Patched ponies like these are Dartmoor hill ponies, a hardy mix of breeds born on the moor, and most of the ponies out there are hill ponies. The first written record of ponies on Dartmoor is from 1012, and hoofprints found in a dig are more than 3,500 years old. In 1950 there were several thousand on the moor; now there are about 1,500.

The roads across the moor have a 40 mph limit, and they need it: in 2016, 32 ponies were killed on them, with 123 sheep and five cows.

The photographs

I took them with my Nikon D850: the road with a 12–24 mm zoom at its widest, and the rest with my Sigma 150–600 mm, Brent Tor at 150 mm and the ponies at 300 to 500 mm. The road and Brent Tor are each blended from bracketed exposures, to hold the bright sky and the dark moor together. I photographed the ponies at 1/2500 of a second, which at sunset took between ISO 11,400 and 20,000. They’re in the Hoofed Mammals gallery on Thalassa too.
