On a trip to Cornwall at the end of June 2022, Cal and I went to Kynance Cove, on the Lizard: small beaches of pale sand among islands and stacks of serpentine, a dark green rock that was once part of the mantle beneath an ocean. The Victorians loved it. The royal family is said to have come ashore here in 1846, and Tennyson came two years later.

Cornwall and Home Across Dartmoor, part 1 of 4

On Sunday 26 June 2022, on a trip to Cornwall, Cal and I went to Kynance Cove, on the west side of the Lizard, less than 2 miles north-west of Lizard Point, the southern tip of mainland Britain. The cove is a scatter of small beaches of pale sand among islands and stacks of dark rock, with the coast path running along the cliffs above, and the National Trust looks after it. Cal took the photographs on this page from the cliffs on its south-east side.

The stacks

From up there the four biggest rocks stand in a row. The Bishop is the smaller point on the left, beside the pyramid of Gull Rock. The broad, grassy island is Asparagus Island, named after the rare wild asparagus that grows on it. You can walk out to it at low tide, but it’s cut off on almost every high tide, and at about half-tide it snorts: the sea has tunnelled into it along a fault, and the blowhole is called the Devil’s Bellows. The tall stack at the edge of the sand to its right is probably Steeple Rock.

Serpentine

The dark rock is serpentinite, green and red, polished by the waves until it looks like the skin of a snake, which is how serpentine got its name. It didn’t start out as part of Britain at all. About 400 million years ago it was deep under an ocean, part of the mantle beneath the ocean floor, at a ridge where two plates were moving apart. Water getting into it turned it to serpentine. When the continents later collided, a slice of that ocean floor, with the mantle under it, was pushed up onto the land. Geologists call a slice like that an ophiolite, and the Lizard is one. In places, granite and basalt forced their way into the serpentine; the sea wore those away more easily, and left the blocks of serpentine standing as islands and stacks.

Victorian visitors

The cove became popular with visitors in the early Victorian years. On Sunday 6 September 1846 the royal yacht, on its way back to Falmouth from Mount’s Bay, called here, and The Times reported that “it is said” Queen Victoria, Prince Albert, and two of their children landed on the beach and spent some time picking up shells. A watercolour in the Royal Collection by Nicholas Condy shows the royal barge coming in, with Gull Rock and Asparagus Island. Albert’s interest in the stone, the story goes, made polished serpentine fashionable, and workshops on the Lizard turned it into ornaments for Victorian homes.

Two years later, on 8 July 1848, Tennyson walked down to the cove and wrote in his journal: “Glorious grass-green monsters of waves. Into caves of Asparagus Island. Sat watching wave-rainbows.”
