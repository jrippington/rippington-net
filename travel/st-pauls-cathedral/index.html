St Paul's Cathedral

Looking up into Wren's dome

September 2022, 5 min read

Draft, for review

On this page

  1. The dome
  2. The quire
  3. The high altar
  4. The photographs

Two visits to St Paul's Cathedral, in the City of London, in the summer of 2022, with a very wide lens and a lot of bending over backwards: straight up into Christopher Wren's dome and James Thornhill's paintings, down from the crossing into the quire, up over the quire's gold mosaics, and over the high altar beneath its baldacchino.

These photographs are from two visits to St Paul’s Cathedral, in the City of London, in the summer of 2022, and all four of them look up: straight into the dome, across the crossing beneath it, over the mosaics of the quire, and over the high altar.

The cathedral is Christopher Wren’s, built after the Great Fire of London gutted the old St Paul’s in September 1666. The first stone was laid in June 1675 and the last stone of the lantern on top of the dome in October 1708, and Parliament declared the cathedral finished on Christmas Day 1711.

The dome

The photograph at the top of this page looks straight up into the dome from the floor. What you see from inside is only the innermost of three: a dome of brick, a cone of brick above it that carries the stone lantern, and over both, the outer dome of timber and lead that you see from the street. The round opening at the top of the painted dome, an idea from the Pantheon in Rome, looks up towards the lantern.

James Thornhill won the commission to paint the dome in 1715. His eight scenes from the life of St Paul are in monochrome, as the commission asked, so that they’d look like carved relief, and their painted architecture carries on the real niches of the drum below. Beneath the drum’s 24 windows, 30 metres above the floor and 259 steps up, runs the Whispering Gallery. In the eight spandrels between the great arches that carry it all are mosaics of four prophets and the four evangelists, made in Venice from the 1860s onwards.

From the floor of the crossing, looking east, the quire opens between the two halves of the organ case, designed in Wren’s office and carved by Grinling Gibbons. In the early 1870s the architect F. C. Penrose took the case down to open up the view through the cathedral, and set its two halves on either side of the quire’s entrance.

The quire

The quire, the cathedral’s east arm, is where the choir sings the services. Wren left its ceiling as plain carved stone. Between 1891 and 1904 William Blake Richmond covered it in mosaic, made by Powell’s of Whitefriars from tiny pieces of coloured glass and gold leaf, and its three shallow domes show the Creation: from the east, the birds, the fishes and the beasts.

The high altar

Wren wanted the altar under a canopy on twisted columns, like Bernini’s over the altar of St Peter’s in Rome, and a wooden model of it was made in about 1693, but it was never built; the clergy may have found it too Roman Catholic. On the evening of 10 October 1940 a bomb came through the roof of the east end and destroyed the high altar. Its replacement, designed by Stephen Dykes Bower and Godfrey Allen and dedicated in 1958, is a canopy at last: a baldacchino on twisted “Solomonic” columns inspired by Wren’s.

Behind it, in the apse, is the American Memorial Chapel, dedicated by the Queen on 27 November 1958, with Vice-President Richard Nixon there. It remembers about 28,000 Americans who died on their way to Britain, or while they were based here, in the Second World War. Their names are in its roll of honour, and a page is turned every day. People all over Britain paid for it, and its windows carry the emblems of the 48 states and four dependencies of 1958.

The photographs

I took them with my Nikon D850 and a Sigma 12–24 mm lens at 12 mm, its widest. The one straight up into the dome is a single frame. The other three are stitched together photographs, taken by contorting myself like a gymnast with the camera passing overhead, until I couldn’t bend over backwards any further to get the roof shots, and then stitching those together. The quire and the high altar are HDR (high dynamic range) as well: each position was taken at several exposures and merged, so that the windows and the shadows both keep their detail.
