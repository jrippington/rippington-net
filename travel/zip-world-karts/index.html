A Weekend in North Wales, part 1 of 3

On Saturday 28 October 2023, the first afternoon of a weekend in Snowdonia, Cal and I went to Zip World at Penrhyn Quarry, just above Bethesda. We didn’t go on any of the zip lines. We went on the Quarry Karts instead: three-wheeled karts with no engine, a low seat between two fat back wheels, handlebars, and a brake lever at each hand, which you ride down a track carved into the side of the quarry, through chicanes of old tyres, banked corners and a tunnel. A red military truck takes you back up to the top.

It was absolutely hilarious. Cal set off first and I followed about twenty seconds later, and the run took us about five minutes, including the moment where Cal nearly took a very close look at the scenery by disappearing into it.

The quarry

Penrhyn was once the largest slate quarry in the world. Slate had been dug here since at least the 1500s, but large-scale working began in 1770 under Richard Pennant, whose family’s fortune had come from slave labour on its sugar plantations in Jamaica. The quarry was cut into the mountain in great terraces, a way of working begun here that quarries all over the world later copied, and its main pit is almost a mile long. At its height about 3,000 men worked in it, and between 1780 and 1940 the slate quarries of north-west Wales dominated the world’s production of roofing slate.

In November 1900 about 2,800 of Penrhyn’s quarrymen walked out, over their union and their pay and conditions, and most stayed out for three years, until November 1903: the Great Strike of Penrhyn, often called the longest dispute in British industrial history. Since 2021 the quarry and Bethesda have been part of the Slate Landscape of Northwest Wales, a World Heritage Site. Zip World came in 2013, with a zip line about a mile long over the quarry, and the karts followed in 2019.

What drove the karts

Nothing but gravity. The start is about 1,500 feet above the sea, Zip World says, and the track drops about 240 metres to the finish. If nothing held it back, a kart falling that far would be doing more than 150 miles an hour by the bottom. The slate under its tyres and the air in front of it hold it to a fraction of that, and the brakes do the rest: when the karts opened, Zip World said they could reach 40 miles an hour.

What gravity really is turns out to be far stranger than it looks from a kart. There’s a whole run of articles about it on Theoria, starting with Einstein’s Happiest Thought and The Apple That Fell in a Straight Line.
