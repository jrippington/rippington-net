Wells Cathedral

Scissor arches, a palm of ribs and a wall of statues

September 2022, 5 min read

Draft, for review

On this page

  2. The organ
  3. The quire
  4. The chapter house

On a September afternoon in 2022 I photographed Wells Cathedral, in Somerset, the first cathedral in England built in the Gothic style from the start: the scissor arches put in to brace its central tower, the organ beneath it, the net of ribs over the quire, the chapter house, and the west front, with about 300 of its medieval statues still in place.

On the afternoon of Thursday 22 September 2022 I photographed Wells Cathedral, in Somerset. It was begun in about 1175, and it was the first cathedral in England to be built in the Gothic style from the start. Every one of these photographs from inside looks up.

The scissor arches

In the early 1300s the central tower was built higher and given a spire, and cracks began to appear. In 1338 the master mason, William Joy, braced the piers that carry the tower by putting in these: on three sides of the crossing, a pointed arch with an upside-down arch standing on top of it, the two meeting in the middle like an open pair of scissors, with a round eye on each side. They took until 1348. People often take them for a modern addition, but they’re nearly 700 years old, and the tower has stood ever since.

Above the western arch is a rood, Christ on the cross between Mary and John. The nave’s vault was painted with its sprays of red foliage in the 1800s. Through the arch you can see the organ, on the stone screen between the crossing and the quire, and through the doorway beneath it, far off, a glimpse of the stained glass at the east end.

The organ

The next photograph looks straight up from beneath the tower, inside the scissors, with the arches at its edges and the back of the rood at the top. Above the organ is the tower’s own vault, a later fan of ribs in the Perpendicular style. The organ goes back to an instrument Henry Willis built in 1857, with pipes from 1786 by Samuel Green inside it. Harrison and Harrison enlarged it in 1910 and rebuilt it in 1973 and 1974, when it was given this case, designed by Alan Rome, with a screen carved by Roger Large.

The quire

East of the organ is the quire, rebuilt and extended by William Joy in the second quarter of the 1300s, with a vault quite unlike the nave’s simple one: a dense net of ribs. At its east end is the Jesse Window, from about 1340: seven lights in greens and golds showing the Tree of Jesse, the family and ancestors of Christ. It narrowly escaped destruction in the Civil War. Beneath it stands a row of saints, and on the right in the photograph is the tall canopy of the bishop’s throne, also from 1340, restored by Anthony Salvin in about 1850.

The chapter house

The photograph at the top of this page is the chapter house, the room where the cathedral’s chapter, its governing body of clergy, met. Its stair was built first, from 1265, and the octagon itself between 1286 and 1306. Around its walls run 51 seats, and from the column in the middle, ringed with shafts of dark Purbeck marble, 32 ribs spring up and out across the vault like the fronds of a palm. The architectural writer Alec Clifton-Taylor called it “architecturally the most beautiful in England”.

The west front

The west front was designed by Thomas Norreys, after the death in 1229 of Adam Lock, the master mason who had built only its plinth. It’s about 45 metres wide and 30 high, and covered from the ground to the gable in niches for statues. There were once about 400 of them, painted in bright colours; about 300 are still there, cleaned and conserved between 1974 and 1986. The two towers came much later, the south-west between 1365 and 1395 and the north-west between 1425 and 1435.

The photographs

I took these with my Nikon D850 and a Sigma 12–24 mm lens, at or near its widest, 12 mm. The four inside are stitched together photographs, taken by contorting myself like a gymnast with the camera passing overhead, until I couldn’t bend over backwards any further to get the roof shots, and then stitching those together. Each position was taken at several exposures and merged, too (HDR, for high dynamic range), so that the windows and the shadows both keep their detail. The west front obviously wasn’t taken like that: it’s a single view, though from merged exposures as well. I’d photographed St Paul’s Cathedral the same way that summer.
